# Autonomous and teleoperated driving of lunar rovers on regolith: numbers for a learned driving model and a traversal-time/energy model (south-pole cargo rover)

Scope note: current as of 15 Oct 2026. Every number is tagged **[REQ]** (requirement / design target), **[DEMO]** (measured on hardware or in flight), **[SIM]** (simulation or human-in-the-loop simulator study), or **[CLAIM]** (company statement without test data). ## Key Question 1: What speed, slope, slip and energy numbers should a south-pole cargo rover model use, by autonomy level (teleop at 5-10 s RTT, supervised, full autonomy)?

### Takeaway
The only hardware-grounded lunar numbers are Apollo LRV (crewed, ~8-9 km/h average while moving, ~1.6 Ah/km ≈ 57 Wh/km at 36 V) and Yutu-2 (teleoperated, 1.6 km in ~6 years). For Earth-teleop at 6-8 s delay, NASA JSC's 2023 simulator studies give a "speed made good" of 2.0 km/h (6 s) and 1.8 km/h (8 s) versus 3.2 km/h at 0 s, with operators settling into a 1-1.5 km/h cruise; VIPER's concept of operations (6-10 s latency, waypoint stops every ~4.5 m) was 0.36-0.72 km/h. No lunar rover has yet demonstrated supervised or full autonomy on the surface; the best flight analogue is Mars AutoNav (~0.12 km/h), while 2026-era LTV designs target 10-15 km/h crewed and the Endurance-A concept requires "~10x Perseverance" autonomous speed, mostly at night.

**Earth teleoperation at lunar latency [SIM, human-in-the-loop]**
- JSC/Aerospace Corp. studies (Apr 2023: 0 s and 4 s, 8 operators, ~30 min training; Aug 2023: 6 s and 8 s, ~3 h training per latency), government-reference LTV in a simulated south-pole environment, single mast PTZ camera, six terrain types, five traverse scenarios, natural lighting. **Average speed including stops ("speed made good"): 0 s = 3.24 km/h; 4 s = 2.56 km/h (12% slower, p = .028); 6 s = 2.03 km/h; 8 s = 1.76 km/h (6 vs 8 s n.s.). Speed without stops: 0 s = 3.80 km/h; 4 s = 3.55 km/h.** Derived times from the SRD distances: 6 km in 1:51 (0 s), 2:20 (4 s), 2:57 (6 s), 3:24 (8 s); 8 km in 2:28 / 3:07 / 3:56 / 4:32; 20 km in 6:10 / 7:48 / 9:51 / 11:21 (h:mm). Operators settled into a **1-1.5 km/h cruise**; control was hardest at **0.4-0.8 km/h** and the vehicle "more responsive" at **2.9-3.6 km/h**, but **1 m/s (3.6 km/h) "equated to a lot of over correction."** Operators braked more and had significantly higher NASA-TLX workload under delay; >95% of navigation at 6-8 s used the 180° camera pan to ground-truth boulders/craters; all operators completed all traverses; the authors call the result "a very conservative average" that nonetheless met LTV-SYS-071, assuming flawless high-bandwidth comms and faultless navigation. Operators estimated 20-100 h of training for ~60% proficiency and a year-long program for full proficiency. The 6-8 s delays were derived from a DSN analysis (DSN "not designed for fast bit rate communications") — [Litaker, Li, Beaton, Lewis 2025, IEEE Aerospace, NTRS 20240011392](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240011392); TM version [NTRS 20240001217](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240001217)
- Rock-contact metric used in those studies: count of contacts with rocks 0.15-0.37 m above the surface (counts not reported in the paper) — [Litaker et al. 2025 (PDF)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240011392/downloads/Teleops%20IEEE%20Paper%20FY24%20FINAL.pdf)
- CU Boulder lunar-analog telerobotics: time-to-discovery rose **150%** (188 to 470 s) at **2.6 s** round-trip latency vs ≤0.4 s; operators need **>5 fps**; H.264 at 5 fps 640x480 8-bit ≈ 1.72 Mbps vs ~4 Mbps Earth-Moon L2 link budget; 2-3 operators, 44-153 trials — [Mellinkoff et al. 2018, arXiv 1710.01254](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1710.01254)
- VIPER CONOPS: "operated interactively in near-real time" from Earth with **6-10 s latency**; drivers command stops about every **15 ft (4.5 m)**; travel speed **10-20 cm/s (0.36-0.72 km/h)**, 5-10 cm/s while prospecting; 24/7 ops in three 8-h shifts; ten drivers trained >1 year; Earth out of view two weeks per month (libration) — [NASA VIPER lunar operations](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/viper/lunar-operations/)

**South-pole lighting, night and dust constraints on driving**
- Apollo had the sun 8-50° above the horizon; Artemis south-pole sites will have **1-2°** (1.2° used in the JSC sim). Results: at low sun angles, "especially when driving at high speed," sun direction "can severely impact the crew's ability to safely drive"; **up-sun** drivers consistently tacked **±20-30°** (costs time and energy); down-sun the rover's own long shadow forced tacking too; entering shadowed terrain while lit terrain is in view makes **artificial lights useless** because of pupil contraction; slope and depth of shadowed craters are "very difficult" to judge, "naturally leading to slower driving speeds than originally anticipated"; stars invisible for navigation; **up-sun on a slight up-slope was judged unacceptable regardless of artificial lights** and must be avoided in route planning; crew wanted a directable spotlight and suggested radar/LiDAR; driver attention 80% terrain / 20% displays; Phase 1: 6 astronauts x 10 lighting conditions, 200 m target; Phase 2: 4 astronauts + remote operator, 5 scenarios of 275-2,597 m incl. 7° and 15° slopes; sim rover 15 km/h, ±20° slopes; "all these lighting conditions ... would affect the accuracy and speed of any teleoperations from Earth" — [Litaker et al. 2025, South Pole Lunar Lighting Studies, NTRS 20240011393](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240011393) ([PDF](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240011393/downloads/Lighiting%20Study%20IEEE%20Paper%20FINAL-1.pdf))
- VIPER site: sun only ~10° above the horizon, long fast-moving shadows — [NASA VIPER lunar operations](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/viper/lunar-operations/)
- VIPER lights: 8 LED-array lights (2 mast "NavLights" for long-range stereo; 6 "HazLights" near-field), **blue wavelength tuned for PSR sensitivity**; "shadowed and lit areas are in such high contrast that any contours in the landscape are effectively invisible"; regolith "reflects a lot of light, blinding VIPER's cameras" (backscatter drove light placement) — [NASA, VIPER headlights](https://www.nasa.gov/humans-in-space/viper-rover-will-get-driving-headlights/); [Bluethmann 2024](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240013903/downloads/LSIC_2024-Tech%20Infusion_Panel-VIPER_Mob-HW.pdf)
- Sun-synchronous route planning: paths that follow sunlit corridors to keep solar power and avoid night — [Otten et al., NSS](https://nss.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/07/Space-Manufacturing-conference-13-135-Sun-Synchronous-Navigation.pdf); "Sunlit Pathways" study of linked sunlit routes between south-pole sites — [Universe Today](https://www.universetoday.com/159464/a-rover-could-weave-its-way-between-patches-of-sunlight-on-the-lunar-south-pole/); rotating long shadows can disrupt visual-inertial navigation; micro-rovers proposed sun-referenced navigation — [IAC 2025 paper](https://dl.iafastro.directory/event/IAC-2025/paper/102536/)
- Dust on cameras (Apollo): LRV TV images showed "noticeable deterioration ... particularly when the camera is pointed up-Sun"; lens brushing "always produced a noticeable - sometimes dramatic - improvement" — [ALSJ "Working on the Moon: TV lens brushing"](https://www.nasa.gov/history/alsj/WOTM/WOTM-TVLensBrushing.html) (page has since moved to apollojournals.org; quotes from search snippet); lost fender extensions increased dust on radiators and the TV camera, and ground brushing tests were "inadequate and misleading" — Gaier & Creel presentation (mirror: [tika corpus 232403.pdf](https://corpora.tika.apache.org/base/docs/govdocs1/232/232403.pdf)); dust is electrostatically charged and "stuck to almost everything, including ... the lenses of cameras" — [NASA Enabler report 1994](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19940021187/downloads/19940021187.pdf)

### Inferences
- Suggested model values by autonomy level (speed made good, flat-to-moderate terrain, daylight):
  - **Earth teleop, 5-10 s RTT:** 1.8-2.0 km/h (JSC 6-8 s, trained ~3 h, single PTZ camera); use 1.0-1.5 km/h for the "cruise" regime and 0.4-0.7 km/h for VIPER-style stop-every-4.5-m waypoint driving. Expect 12% slowdown per first 4 s and roughly linear further decline (3.24 to 1.76 km/h over 0-8 s ≈ -0.19 km/h per second of delay).
  - **Supervised autonomy (onboard hazard avoidance, periodic human approval):** no lunar demonstration exists. Flight-proven Mars AutoNav is 0.12 km/h; ESA targets 3.6 km/h; Endurance-A implies roughly 1 km/h-class (10x Perseverance). A defensible modelling range is 0.5-3.6 km/h with the uncertainty carried explicitly.
  - **Full autonomy / crewed:** vehicle limits of 10-15 km/h (CLV-1 10, RACER 15, government-reference LTV 15) but Apollo's realized moving average was ~8-9 km/h (27.8 km / 3.03 h = 9.2; 35.9 km / 4.43 h = 8.1), and the lighting study shows up-sun legs cost extra time via tacking.
- Energy: Apollo LRV 1.58-1.67 Ah/km at 36 V ≈ **57-60 Wh/km** for ~700 kg (vehicle + payload, crew driving) → ~0.08 Wh/(kg·km). Scaling to a 1,600 kg-payload LTV traverse would suggest ~150-200 Wh/km before slope and slip penalties; VIPER's 5,420 Wh battery against a 20 km range requirement likewise implies order-100-250 Wh/km budgets. These are inferences, not measurements.
- Slope: all current designs converge on **15° nominal / 20° requirement** (VIPER 15°, LTV 20°, RACER 20°, Endurance 20°, Intrepid 15°); 25-30° is "when necessary" with high slip (VIPER) — a traversal model should treat >20° as an exceptional, high-slip, low-speed regime.
- The two JSC studies imply that the biggest teleop penalty is not latency per se but stopping/ground-truthing time: speed without stops only fell 3.80 → 3.55 km/h (0 → 4 s) while speed with stops fell 3.24 → 2.56 km/h.

## Key Question 2: What is the measured relationship between slip and slope on lunar regolith / simulant, and the maximum traversable slope for wheeled rovers?

### Takeaway
The best quantified slope-slip data for a lunar-class rover come from NASA Glenn SLOPE-lab tests of the 73-88 kg MGRU3 (1/6-mass VIPER) on GRC-1/GRC-3 and Chrono physics simulations validated against them: slip rises steeply above ~15-20°, and the key 2024 finding is that gravity-offloaded Earth tests are optimistic — a 440 kg VIPER on the Moon at 30° is predicted at ~85% slip (dig-in territory, >80%) where MGRU3 on Earth climbed at 42%. Practical limits cluster at 15-20° for grousered rigid wheels (20% slip is the traditional mobility criterion), with 25-30° reachable only at high slip and low speed.

### Cited Findings
- **Hu, Li, Rogg, Schepelmann, Creager, Chandler, Kamrin, Negrut (2024) [SIM validated vs DEMO]:** Chrono CRM (SPH continuum) simulator correlated with SLOPE-lab TREC single-wheel and MGRU3 full-rover tests on GRC-1 (highlands analogue; densities 1,660/1,730/1,760 kg/m³ with friction angles 33.4°/37.2°/38.4°) and GRC-3 (1,627/1,734/1,839 kg/m³; 37.8°/42.0°/47.8°). Single-wheel tests: 17.5 kg (GRC-3) or 22 kg (GRC-1) wheel load, 0.2 m/s, slip 0-0.8; full rover 73 kg (GRC-3) / 88 kg (GRC-1) digital twins; slope-mode tests at 0-30° in 5° steps (252 simulations). Findings: (i) single-wheel and full-rover slope/slip curves are comparable; (ii) slope-vs-slip curves are invariant to wheel angular velocity (0.33 vs 0.8 rad/s); (iii) at 15° slope the rover's steady speed was 0.125 m/s on Earth vs 0.05 m/s on the Moon — ratio ≈ √6, the granular scaling law; (iv) **on GRC-3 at φ = 47.8°, the 73 kg MGRU3 climbs 30° at ~42% slip, whereas the 440 kg VIPER in lunar gravity would see ~85% slip; "slip values above 80% place the rover in a situation that increases the propensity for dig-in"**; for the weaker GRC-3 case (φ = 37.8°, 1,627 kg/m³) MGRU3 climbed 10° at 18% slip while VIPER on the Moon is reported at ~75% slip (the comparison slope for that figure was truncated in my text extraction); GRC-3 performs slightly better than GRC-1; VIPER mass grew 440 → 520 kg during design; the authors conclude mass reduction / gravity offload is unnecessary and misleading for slope-slip prediction — [arXiv 2405.11001](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.11001)
- **Unjhawala et al. (2025) [SIM validated vs DEMO]:** Chrono::CRM validated against MGRU3 single-wheel slope tests at SLOPE lab (GRC-1 at 60% relative density, φ = 38.4°, 1,760 kg/m³; wheel radius 0.25 m, width 0.2 m; ramps simulated by rotating gravity); slip-vs-slope curve matches DEM and experiment; simulator handles 10 km terrain stretches at near-interactive rates; open-source BSD-3 — [arXiv 2507.05643](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.05643)
- **Lugged micro-rover wheel on lunar simulant [DEMO + DEM]:** 2D DEM compared with slope-bed tests; metrics "slope angle for 20% slip," "power number at self-propelled point," "power number on 15° slope"; conclusion: the selected lugged wheel "might not be able to move up a slope steeper than 20 degrees" — [Nakashima et al., J. Terramechanics 2010 (Kyoto repository)](https://repository.kulib.kyoto-u.ac.jp/dspace/bitstream/2433/126852/1/j.jterra.2010.04.001.pdf)
- **Apollo LRV wheel program:** Boeing/GM wheel comparisons used performance at **20% wheel slip** as the standard for limiting mobility — [NASA/Boeing LRV wheel performance report (ALSJ)](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/static/history/alsj/PerfBoeingLRVWheelsRpt1.pdf) (search snippet; PDF download failed)
- **Bekker-Wong-Janosi-Hanamoto (BWJH) applicability:** valid only when sinkage is small, slip ratio is low, and soil is not in plastic flow; bevameter parameters are gravity-dependent, so Earth-measured k_c, k_φ, n, c, φ, K do not transfer directly to the Moon — [Hu et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.11001)
- **Yutu-2 in situ [DEMO]:** slip 10.5-19.7% on gentle slopes; sinkage ~10 mm; friction angle 21.5-42°, cohesion 0.5-3.2 kPa (see Q1 citations) — [Jilin paper](https://cjournal.hep.com.cn/1671-5497/CN/PDF/10.13229/j.cnki.jdxbgxb.20211193); [Nature Geoscience ESM](https://media.springernature.com/original/springer-static/esm/art%3A10.1038%2Fs41561-022-00905-6/MediaObjects/41561_2022_905_MOESM1_ESM.pdf); [Remote Sensing 2023](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.3390%2FRS15174270)
- **Rover slope capabilities (requirement/design):** VIPER 15° req., 25-30° "when necessary" with slip; LTV 20°; Moon RACER ~20°; Endurance/INSPIRE <20° (dual side-rocker), Intrepid <15° — [Bluethmann 2024](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240013903/downloads/LSIC_2024-Tech%20Infusion_Panel-VIPER_Mob-HW.pdf); [NASA VIPER ops](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/viper/lunar-operations/); [Moon Monday #171](https://jatan.space/moon-monday-issue-171/); [collectSPACE](https://www.collectspace.com/news/news-110724a-intuitive-machines-racer-lunar-terrain-vehicle-reveal.html); [NASA comparison sheet](https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/intrepid-endurance-inspire-comparison.pdf)
- **Learned slip-vs-slope (Earth analog, JPL 2006):** on a 10° tilt-table of deep sand Rocky8 showed "significant slip in the downslope direction"; Mojave sandy slopes of -5° to 10° pitch and up to 12° roll; soil going upslope produced "much larger positive slip" than the same slope downslope — [Angelova et al. 2006](https://robotics.jpl.nasa.gov/media/documents/Angelova06LearningSlip.pdf)
- Terramechanics-driven speed effect: high-speed single-wheel tests on grouser wheels and velocity effects on tractive performance exist (ISTVS 2024; arXiv 2306.02167) but were not read in full — [ISTVS 2024 #0412](https://2024.istvs.org/submissions/papers/0412); [arXiv 2306.02167](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.02167)

### Inferences
- A usable slip-vs-slope curve for a ~500 kg-class lunar rover on highlands-like regolith (GRC-1, φ ≈ 38°): slip ≈ 5-10% at 0-5°, ~20% around 10-15°, climbing rapidly past 20°, and ≥75-85% (dig-in risk) by 30°. The exact curve should be read from Fig. 7b/13 of Hu et al.; the numbers above are anchored to the quoted points (10° at 18% on weak Earth soil, 30° at 42% Earth vs 85% Moon).
- Speed on slopes scales with √g: a traversal-time model should cut speed by ~1/√6 ≈ 0.41 relative to Earth test data at equal wheel RPM, or equivalently expect ~60% lower ground speed at a given slope/slip than Earth-gravity tests suggest.
- For a cargo rover (1,600 kg payload on the LTV), wheel loads will exceed VIPER's by 3-4x; since heavier vehicles on the Moon slipped more in the Hu et al. comparison, the 20° requirement is likely a hard planning limit and 15° a nominal design limit.

### Gaps
- No digitized slip-vs-slope table from the SLOPE-lab MGRU3 tests (only plots in the two arXiv papers); extracting numbers requires reading the figures.
- Apollo LRV in-flight slip percentages and sinkage (crews reported fishtailing at speed, but no quantitative slip data found in the sources reached).
- No measured slip-slope data for any LTV contender's tires (Goodyear/Michelin airless) on simulant were published.
- Effects of speed (>1 m/s) on slip/sinkage in lunar simulant were not extracted (ISTVS 2024 and arXiv 2306.02167 cover this).

## Key Question 3: What learned driving approaches for rovers exist, how are they evaluated (interventions per km, Nvidia's autonomy metric), and what performance has been reported?

### Takeaway
Learned components fielded or near-fielded on planetary rovers are perception-side (terrain classification, slip/traversability prediction), not end-to-end control: SPOC on Curiosity ops, Angelova's 2006 slip-from-stereo (~15% error), and the Sep-2026 "Learning to Drive on Mars" model trained on 500 sols / 45 km of Perseverance logs (AUROC 0.874). End-to-end behavioral cloning à la Nvidia 2016 (98% "autonomy" by the 6-s-per-intervention metric) has only reached lunar rovers in simulation theses (Pisa 2025, ESA VORTEX) with no reported intervention metrics; the strongest sim-to-real lunar result is RL waypoint tracking (Sim2Dust, LunaLab, 2-4 cm tracking error zero-shot). No planetary study reports interventions per km.

### Cited Findings

**Nvidia 2016 end-to-end design and metric**
- PilotNet: 9 layers (normalization, 5 conv, 3 FC), ~27M connections, 250k parameters; ~72 h of human driving data; **autonomy = (1 - (interventions x 6 s)/elapsed time) x 100**, a virtual intervention counted when the car departs >1 m from lane center in simulation; ~100 miles / 3 h simulated routes; **~98% autonomy** on a Holmdel-Atlantic Highlands drive and 10 miles on the Garden State Parkway with zero interventions — [Bojarski et al. 2016, arXiv 1604.07316](https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.07316)
- Known limitations of behavior cloning for driving (dataset bias, overfitting, dynamic objects, training instability) and the NoCrash benchmark — [Codevilla et al., ICCV 2019](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content_ICCV_2019/papers/Codevilla_Exploring_the_Limitations_of_Behavior_Cloning_for_Autonomous_Driving_ICCV_2019_paper.pdf)

**Behavioral cloning / imitation learning on lunar/planetary rovers [SIM]**
- Univ. of Pisa MSc (May 2025, supervisors Cimino and Cowley/ESA): CNN-LSTM behavioral cloning mapping 3 camera views + rover state to steering, trained in ESA's VORTEX (Unreal) with CSA real-world data, domain randomization; compared with DQN on LiDAR in Gazebo; qualitative conclusions only (BC data-efficient but brittle out-of-distribution; LSTM reduced erratic steering); full text not public; **no success-rate or intervention numbers** — [Pisa ETD](https://etd.adm.unipi.it/t/etd-05112025-215810)
- Aalborg MSc (Jun 2025): DAgger teacher-student distillation from privileged heightmap to noisy RGB-D for rover navigation in Isaac Sim (RLRoverLab), Mars-like terrain — [AAU thesis](https://projekter.aau.dk/projekter/files/785256569/P10_Master_s_thesis_Thomas_Schou_Sorensen_20204534.pdf)
- **Sim2Dust (Orsula et al., iSpaRo 2025) [SIM-to-DEMO]:** RL waypoint tracking trained in Space Robotics Bench (Isaac Lab) with procedural terrain + domain randomization, transferred zero-shot to a Leo Rover (skid-steer, 29.5 cm wheelbase, max 0.4 m/s) in Univ. Luxembourg LunaLab (~20 t basalt gravel). Real-rover average tracking error (position/heading) at 5/15/25 cm/s: **DreamerV3 2.3 cm/1.7°, 3.3 cm/1.9°, 3.6 cm/2.3°**; PPO 13.2-14.8 cm; static-scene training degraded to 3.4-4.4 cm and 4-7°; vision-based (64x64 depth) variant 9.2 cm / 6.5° at 15 cm/s; particle-physics fine-tuning gave minor gains for +82 h compute; randomization: obs noise N(0,[1 cm,2.5°]) per episode, action delay up to 120 ms — [arXiv 2508.11503](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.11503)
- **NASA Lunar Autonomy Challenge (2025):** six-month competition; digital twin of NASA's IPEx excavator in CARLA with lunar environment models; software had to navigate autonomously, map the surface, identify obstacles and manage power "without human intervention"; 31 teams; winners Stanford NAV Lab (1st), MIT MAPLE (2nd), CMU Moonlight (3rd); scoring rubric not given on the NASA page — [NASA STMD, May 2025](https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/stmd/top-prize-awarded-in-lunar-autonomy-challenge-to-virtually-map-moons-surface)

**Learned slip prediction and traversability**
- Angelova, Matthies, Helmick, Sibley, Perona (JPL/Caltech 2006): nonlinear regression of slip vs. vision-derived longitudinal/lateral slope per terrain type (soil, sand, gravel, asphalt), trained from the rover's own VO-measured slip; **slip prediction error ~15%, "comparable to the measurement errors for slip itself"**; Rocky8 Mojave dataset only ~200 examples; LAGR datasets with 0.2 m steps — [Angelova et al. 2006](https://robotics.jpl.nasa.gov/media/documents/Angelova06LearningSlip.pdf)
- SPOC (Rothrock et al.): CNN pixel-wise classifier, 17 terrain classes, human-expert labels, deployed in Curiosity ground operations — [Survey, arXiv 2204.10883](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2204.10883); AI4Mars crowdsourced terrain-label dataset for MSL/M2020 — [Swan et al., CVPRW 2021](https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/CVPR2021W/AI4Space/papers/Swan_AI4MARS_A_Dataset_for_Terrain-Aware_Autonomous_Driving_on_Mars_CVPRW_2021_paper.pdf) (PDF blocked; sizes not verified)
- Self-supervised terrain classification from vibration → vision (Brooks & Iagnemma, MIT) — [NASA ESTO 2007 abstract](https://esto.nasa.gov/conferences/nstc2007/abstracts/Brooks.htm); IMU-labelled self-supervised BEV costmaps from camera+LiDAR for Mars (Xie et al., HIT 2025) — [arXiv 2509.11082](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.11082v1)
- **"Learning to Drive on Mars" (Chiu, Wilson, Tumbar, Sukhatme, Myint, arXiv 21 Sep 2026):** uncertainty-aware traversability from **500 sols / 45 km of Perseverance driving logs** (stereo pairs, poses, accelerometer, rocker-bogie angles, tilt, estimated wheel slip; human- and ENav-driven); **AUROC 0.874, F1 0.758**, +0.058 AUROC / +0.156 F1 over the best baseline; representations integrated into ENav on a physical testbed — [arXiv 2609.24952](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24952)
- Deep probabilistic traversability with test-time adaptation (Endo, Taniai, Ishigami 2024): end-to-end model predicting slip distributions from traverse observations; uncertainty used as planning cost; **simulation only**, no numeric results in abstract — [arXiv 2409.00641](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.00641); slope-from-3D + terrain-class slip risk (Endo & Yoshida, Robotica 2021) — via [ISTVS/CMU summaries](https://ri.cmu.edu/?p=120670)
- SlipNet (Khalifa Univ. thesis): DeepLabv3+ segmentation + ViT slip estimation, beats TerrainNet in deformable-soil simulation — [KU thesis](https://khazna.ku.ac.ae/en/studentTheses/towards-autonomous-lunar-exploration-a-learning-based-lunar-rover/)
- Proprioceptive slip/immobilization detection: SOM-based classifiers **>96% success**; performance depends strongly on speed, terrain and tire type — [ISTVS publication news](https://www.istvs.org/publication-news/characterization-of-machine-learning-algorithms-for-slippage-estimation-in-planetary-exploration-rovers); [CMU RI](https://ri.cmu.edu/?p=120670)

**Datasets for lunar driving perception**
- **SPICE-HL3** (Univ. Luxembourg LunaLab, 2025): 88 sequences, **~1.3M images**, 7 trajectories, stereo RGB-inertial (ZED2) + mono monochrome (FLIR) + SPAD single-photon camera + IMU + wheel odometry; illumination dawn-to-night with/without headlights replicating high-latitude lunar optics; rover speeds **5 and 50 cm/s**; 68.9 GB on Zenodo; no VO/SLAM benchmark numbers reported — [arXiv 2506.22956](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.22956); [Zenodo 13970078](https://zenodo.org/records/13970078)
- **NASA Ames POLAR** (~2,600 HDR stereo pairs, 13 polar-analogue scenarios) and **POLAR-Sim** (~23,000 rock/shadow/crater labels + digital twins for synthetic rendering under varied sun position, exposure and active illumination) — [arXiv 2309.12397](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.12397)
- LunarSim Shoemaker-crater synthetic set (Isaac Sim; RGB, depth, semantic/instance masks, COCO rock boxes; ~0.11 h of footage; CC0) — [claru.ai listing](https://claru.ai/datasets/elementrobotics-lunarsim-shoemaker-traverse-100m-v1)
- Perseverance driving logs (500 sols, 45 km) as used by Chiu et al. — [arXiv 2609.24952](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24952)

### Inferences
- Transferring Nvidia's design to a lunar cargo rover: the sensing/actuation mapping (camera → steering) is directly reusable, but the evaluation metric must change. Six seconds per intervention assumes a co-located human; with a 5-10 s RTT an Earth-based intervention costs at least one RTT plus re-planning, so "autonomy %" should be recomputed with an intervention cost ≥ RTT + stop/restart time (JSC data suggest tens of seconds per ground-truthing stop). Interventions per km is the natural logistics-facing metric, but **no planetary paper reports it** — Mars work reports AUROC/F1 or slip error, LunaLab work reports tracking error, and the JSC studies report speed made good.
- The Sim2Dust result (2-4 cm tracking error zero-shot at 5-25 cm/s) shows the low-level control problem on granular media is largely solved in sim-to-real at VIPER-like speeds; the open problems are perception under polar lighting and hazard/slip-aware speed selection at LTV speeds (3-15 km/h).
- Because the Perseverance-log approach (Chiu et al.) uses operational drive logs as self-supervision, the same recipe could be applied to LTV teleop logs or the JSC simulator data, making "learned traversability from teleop logs" the most direct bridge from the autonomy levels in Q1 to a learned model.

### Gaps
- No end-to-end learned driving policy has been evaluated on a lunar-analogue rover with intervention counts, collision counts or km-per-intervention; the Pisa thesis is not publicly readable.
- Lunar Autonomy Challenge scoring formulas (mapping error, power, distance) were not retrievable (site timed out); team reports may contain them.
- No published learned model trained on polar-lighting imagery (SPICE-HL3/POLAR) reports driving-level outcomes (speed, stops, interventions) — only dataset descriptions.
- AI4Mars dataset sizes and baseline IoU not verified (PDF blocked).

## Key Question 4: What are the main gaps (no learned driving tied to logistics outcomes? no polar-lighting driving models?) that a new study could fill?

### Takeaway
Three gaps are well supported by the evidence above: (1) no study links a learned driving or traversability model to logistics outcomes (traverse time, energy per km, cargo delivered per day) — the JSC teleop numbers are the only speed-vs-autonomy data and they are human-in-the-loop sim; (2) no learned driving model is trained or evaluated under south-pole lighting (1-2° sun, up-sun tacking, shadow transitions) even though NASA's lighting study shows it dominates driving speed and route feasibility; (3) slip-vs-slope for heavy (>1 t gross) cargo rovers on lunar-gravity regolith is only predicted by physics simulation, with 2024 evidence that Earth tests are optimistic.

### Cited Findings
- NASA's own authors label both the latency and lighting studies "first-look" answers and note testing assumed "flawless" high-bandwidth comms and "faultless navigation," single-camera views, and minimal operator training — [Litaker et al. 2025 (teleop)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240011392); [Litaker et al. 2025 (lighting)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240011393)
- The lighting study states drivers slowed more than anticipated in shadow transitions and that some lighting geometries must be avoided outright in route planning; it recommends radar/LiDAR and directable spotlights, and says the same effects "would affect the accuracy and speed of any teleoperations from Earth" — [Litaker et al. 2025 (lighting PDF)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240011393/downloads/Lighiting%20Study%20IEEE%20Paper%20FINAL-1.pdf)
- SPICE-HL3 was built specifically because of a lack of data for "perceptually degraded" high-latitude lunar environments, and POLAR-Sim notes a "scarcity of lunar images from around the lunar poles" — [arXiv 2506.22956](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.22956); [arXiv 2309.12397](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.12397)
- Endurance-A needs ~70% of 2,000 km driven at night with <10 m localization — a requirement with no flight precedent; ShadowNav exists only as simulation and Cinder Lakes field data — [JPL ShadowNav poster](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/site/research/media/posters/2023/R21009p.pdf)
- Hu et al. conclude "a paradigm shift is justified when judging trafficability in different gravity fields," i.e., existing Earth test campaigns (including MGRU-based VIPER mobility V&V) may overstate slope capability for full-mass lunar vehicles — [arXiv 2405.11001](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.11001)
- Pegasus lidar performance claims "lack independent qualification data, test regimes, or detailed specifications" — [Highways Today, Sep 2026](https://highways.today/2026/09/03/pegasus-lunar-rover-lidar/)
- Mars learned-traversability work (Chiu et al. 2026) evaluates with AUROC/F1 rather than drive outcomes; Sim2Dust evaluates tracking error; JSC evaluates speed made good — none evaluate delivered cargo or energy — [arXiv 2609.24952](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24952); [arXiv 2508.11503](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.11503); [NTRS 20240011392](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240011392)
- The LTV requirement set now includes explicit logistics quantities (6/8 km per 24 h remote; 20 km per charge; 1,600 kg uncrewed payload; 800 miles/yr) that a logistics model can be scored against — [NTRS 20240011392](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240011392); [Moon Monday #171](https://jatan.space/moon-monday-issue-171/); [National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/science/article/moon-rover-space-innovation-nasa-artemis)

### Inferences
- A new study could (a) build a traversal-time/energy model parameterized by autonomy level using the JSC speed-made-good curve (3.24 → 1.76 km/h over 0-8 s), VIPER stop-and-go cadence, Apollo Wh/km and the Hu et al. slope-slip-speed scaling; (b) train a behavioral-cloning or learned-traversability model on polar-lighting data (SPICE-HL3, POLAR-Sim renders, or a south-pole simulator with 1-2° sun) and (c) score it with logistics metrics — interventions per km converted to time via RTT, energy per km with slip penalties, and cargo delivered per 24 h against LTV-SYS-071/029. No such end-to-end chain exists in the literature reached here.
- The Nvidia autonomy metric can be adapted by replacing the 6 s intervention cost with (RTT + operator ground-truthing time), which the JSC data suggest is tens of seconds to minutes; this converts a perception benchmark into a logistics-time benchmark.
- Polar-lighting-aware route cost (tacking ±20-30° up-sun, forbidden up-sun-up-slope geometries, shadow-entry slowdowns) is absent from every traversability model found; adding it would change both time and energy per km for east-west vs north-south legs.

### Gaps
- Web-search budget ran out before confirming whether any 2025-2026 paper ties learned rover driving to lunar logistics/ISRU throughput; none appeared in the ~30 searches run.
- No measured data on how shadow fraction or sun azimuth changes a human or autonomous driver's speed in km/h (the lighting study collected speed, stops and rock contacts but published them only qualitatively).
- Dust-on-lens degradation is qualitative only (Apollo TV, Enabler report); no quantitative MTF-vs-dust-loading data for rover cameras were found.
- No published slip or energy data for airless LTV tires (Goodyear, Michelin, Bridgestone) on simulant.
