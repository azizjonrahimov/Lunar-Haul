# Rocket plume-surface interaction (PSI) on the Moon: ejecta, damage, pad siting and rover operations

Research notes compiled 2026-10-15. Scope: quantitative plume-ejecta findings, damage evidence, keep-out/blast-zone guidance, landing-pad construction and mitigation, pad-to-base siting guidance, erosion/dust numbers and operational windows. Tags used in findings: **[MEASURED]** = derived from flight imagery, returned hardware or in-situ instruments; **[MODELLED]** = analytical/CFD/ballistic model output; **[LAB]** = ground test; **[DESIGN ASSUMPTION]** = value adopted in a concept design, not derived; **[POLICY]** = recommendation/placeholder; **[SNIPPET-ONLY]** = taken from a search-result snippet, full text not opened.

Method note: ~25 web searches and ~35 page/PDF fetches; several key PDFs (Metzger & Mantovani 2023, Metzger & Autry 2022, Metzger 2021 ISRU/plume paper, NASA KSC Lunar Innovation Park site-plan deck, Purdue pad-design paper, NASA 2011 heritage-site recommendations, SCALPSS post-landing DEM paper) were text-extracted and read in full or in part. ## Key Question 1: How far does ejecta travel at the lunar south pole for a 3 t, 15 t and 100 t lander, and at what velocity? What distances are considered safe for unshielded rovers and for habitats?

### Takeaway
Measured and modelled work agrees that a lunar lander's plume throws a thin sheet of ejecta 1–3° above the local horizon; dust reaches 1–3 km/s (some exceeds the 2.38 km/s escape speed and goes global), sand 70–1000 m/s, 1-cm gravel ~30 m/s (lands within ~600 m) and 10-cm rocks ~10 m/s (within ~60 m) for an Apollo-LM-class (~5 t, 45 kN) lander; ejecta mass scales roughly as thrust^2.5 (2.6 t for the LM; ~470 t worst-case bound for a 40 t lander) while ejection velocity scales only logarithmically with lander mass. No agency or paper has published a validated "safe" standoff for rovers or habitats: NASA's 2 km heritage-site exclusion radius is an admitted placeholder, NASA KSC's 2026 site-plan work states that "no safe scientific or operational standoff distances have been defined for various landers", and no source gives explicit numbers for 3 t / 15 t / 100 t landers at the south pole (see Inferences for scaling-based estimates).

### Cited Findings

**Ejecta geometry, velocity and size-sorting (LM-class lander)**
- Dust-sized particles ejected by an LM-sized lander reach 1–3 km/s (Lane & Metzger 2012); lunar escape velocity is 2.38 km/s so the ejecta are "distributed globally and exceed orbital altitudes". Sand-sized particles leave at 70–1000 m/s (also global). Gravel-sized (1 cm) particles leave at ~30 m/s and "strike within 600 m of the landing site"; fist-sized rocks (10 cm) at ~10 m/s strike "within about 60 m". Even small landers "will eject particles to the 10s or 100s of kilometers, at least"; larger landers "will travel at much higher velocities and in much greater quantity than the LM". Damage type depends on size: abrasion/contamination by dust, sandblasting/pitting by sand, impact damage by gravel. [MODELLED, anchored on Apollo imagery] — [Metzger, GLEX 2021 abstract](https://p.iafastro.directory/iac/paper/id/62796/abstract-pdf/GLEX-2021,12,3,7,x62796.brief.pdf)
- Apollo descent-video photogrammetry: dust ejection angle 1–3° above horizontal; lofted particle density 10^8–10^13 particles/m³; objects 10–15 cm in size were seen ejected; ejection angle depends on thrust. [MEASURED] — [Immer, Lane, Metzger & Clements 2008, NTRS 20130012063](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20130012063); conference version at [arXiv 2104.07669](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.07669)
- Numerical plume simulation gives dust velocity "on the order of 1 km/s, in agreement with Surveyor III data, and blowing at angles between 1 and 3 degrees". The low angle holds only while the surface stays flat; ejecta leaving non-flat features ramps upward. Apollo 15's ejection angle was measured at 7–11° because it descended over a broad shallow crater, and a dense blast in the final seconds sent ejecta above 22°. If a scour hole forms "the larger, slower ejecta will rain down upon the outpost, rendering berms or screens ineffective". [MEASURED angles; MODELLED velocity] — [Metzger, Immer & Lane, "ISRU Implications for Lunar and Martian Plume Effects" (AIAA 2009; arXiv 2021)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.06248)
- Trajectory modelling with rarefied/compressible flow for Altair-class thrust: the previously reported 3° trajectory limit "can be exceeded by up to about five times"; particles starting 1 cm above the surface at an outer crater rim reach ≥5°, particles starting 10 cm up reach up to 15°. [MODELLED] — [Lane, Metzger & Carlson 2021, arXiv 2104.06332](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.06332). A search snippet of the same paper reports that a 2 m berm placed 30 m from the lander "does not stop particles" and that particles of all simulated diameters were ejected at >10° in one case. [SNIPPET-ONLY]
- Maximum ejecta velocity for the finest dust equals the propellant exhaust exit velocity: ~4,500 m/s for H2/O2 versus ~3,100 m/s for the Apollo LM's Aerozine/NTO. Smaller particles go faster (drag/inertia ~1/d). For a 41.3 t single-engine lander (67 kN) thrusting to 30 cm above the surface, the model assumes ejecta uniformly distributed in a 1–3° cone. [MODELLED] — [Metzger & Mantovani 2023, arXiv 2305.12234](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.12234)
- Plume-accelerated dust can exceed 2 km/s; roughly 10% of dust in the 1.7–2.3 km/s band falls back to the surface, "much of it near the landing site"; most sub-micron dust is removed by solar wind and electric fields; hazard "depends on lander mass and distance from the landing site". [MODELLED] — [Wittal, Phillips, Metzger et al., AAS 20-511 (2020), NTRS 20205003590](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20205003590)
- N-body simulation of near-escape ejecta from north pole, south pole and equator: particles near escape speed take "several days to weeks" to re-impact; equatorial ejecta at speeds as low as 2.142 km/s can stay aloft 30 days; south-pole ejecta could strike Gateway near periselene at 2.142 km/s. [MODELLED] — [Rivera, UCF M.S. thesis 2021](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/etd2020/749/)

**Ejecta mass and erosion per landing**
- Lane & Metzger (2015, Acta Geophysica 63(2):568–599) measured ~2.6 t total erosion for an Apollo LM landing from the descent imagery; the 2023 model coefficient was tuned so Roberts'-equation erosion reproduces "the same 2.6 tons total erosion". For a 40 t single-engine H2/O2 lander descending at 1 m/s with cutoff 1.5 m above the surface, the modelled erosion rate at 1.5 m altitude is 19,238 kg/s. [MEASURED 2.6 t; MODELLED 19,238 kg/s] — [Metzger & Mantovani 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.12234)
- Lane & Metzger (2015) found plume ejecta mass "scales as vehicle thrust to the 2.5 power"; LM landing mass ~5 t ejected ~2.6 t; a 40 t landing mass (HLS class delivering 9 t payload from LLO) would eject (40/5)^2.5 = 181× more, i.e. ~470 t as a worst-case bound (likely an overestimate because compacted deeper regolith resists erosion). Erosion for a 40 t lander occurs within a ~12 m radius (shear-stress calculation with nozzle 5–20 m up); if 470 t were removed as a bowl of radius 12 m at 2000 kg/m³ it would be 1.04 m deep at centre, with a lip exit angle of 9.87°. Ejection velocities "scale as the logarithm of lander mass" (particle sizes 1 µm–2 mm, nozzle 5 m above soil). [MODELLED, anchored on Apollo measurement] — Metzger, Britt, Cannon, Kinzel, Fontes & Kring, LPSC abstract "Lander Plume Effects at Outpost on Shackleton Crater Rim" (full PDF reviewed during this research; canonical URL could not be resolved — see Gaps). The 2.6 t and thrust^2.5 items are independently confirmed by [Metzger & Mantovani 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.12234).
- Chandrayaan-3 Vikram (Aug 2023): ISRO/NRSC compared Chandrayaan-2 OHRC images before and after landing and estimated ~2.09 t of "epiregolith" ejected over a 108.4 m² area (an irregular bright "ejecta halo"). [MEASURED] — [The Week, 27 Oct 2023](https://www.theweek.in/news/india/2023/10/27/chandrayaan-3-isro-says-vikram-landers-soft-landing-ejected-209-tonnes-of-lunar-regolith.amp.html). Vikram's visibly small dust plume was attributed by its team to the absence of a central engine (lower thrust during descent) and diagonal engine placement. [SNIPPET-ONLY] — [Space.com](https://space.com/india-chandrayaan-3-lunar-lander-moon-dust)
- Chang'e-5 landing: simulation constrained by landing photos gives erosion depth ~0.2 cm, total ejected mass ~335.95 kg and maximum particle incline angle 4.16°. [MODELLED] — [Aerospace (MDPI) 2022, DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/16cce4e58db24bc2b58f3b75f699262c)
- Chang'e-5 disturbance mapped from orbit: plume impingement evolved in four stages over ~50 s; final disturbed surface = central main zone ~2,300 m² plus marginal diffuse zone ~15,300 m²; the ascent blast zone (two sub-areas) is "nearly twice larger" than the landing zone (the listing gives "3,400 km²", almost certainly a units error for m²); phase-ratio analysis indicates the plume destroys the micro-porous structure of the uppermost regolith; future landers such as Starship "may cause significant lunar surface disturbances over an area of square kilometers". [MEASURED areas; MODELLED Starship projection] — [Qiao et al., JGR Planets 128, e2022JE007730 (2023)](https://buaa.globalimpact.cn/zh/publications/extensive-lunar-surface-disturbance-at-the-change-5-mission-landi/)
- Blue Ghost Mission 1 (Mare Crisium, 2 Mar 2025): SCALPSS 1.1 produced the "first dedicated in-situ measurements for plume-surface interaction at the lunar surface": a post-landing DEM beneath the lander; terrain under the north RCS thrusters is flat to within ~20 mm; one localized area is 60–80 mm deeper than surroundings but it is not under an RCS pair and whether it is plume-induced is still being assessed; a quantified erosion volume had not been published as of the paper. [MEASURED, preliminary] — [Tyrrell et al., AIAA SciTech 2026, NTRS 20250010922](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250010922/downloads/2026_SciTech_SCALPSS_PostLandingDEM_FINAL.pdf); ~1,800 images acquired during descent/landing — [SCALPSS mission overview, NTRS 20250010973](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250010973/downloads/2026_SciTech_SCALPSS_MissionOverview_FINAL.pdf)
- Metzger's 2024 erosion-rate theory (part 1) finds the Apollo-era disagreement over whether erosion scales with shear stress, kinetic-energy flux or thrust^2.5 unresolved for decades; the new theory says erosion rate scales with kinetic-energy flux at the bottom of the laminar sublayer and that lifted grains do not change the primary erosion rate; predictions for larger landers are deferred to "part 2: benchmarking and predictions". [MODELLED] — [Metzger 2024, arXiv 2403.18583](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.18583)
- NASA's own position (2023): Apollo flight experience "cannot be extrapolated" to Human Landing Systems for PSI; new ground tests (inert ethane and hybrid motors, 2024–2025) and modelling are required. [SNIPPET-ONLY] — [NASA PSI poster, NTRS 20230011539](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230011539/downloads/IPPW_Poster_KorzunChambersPSI_vSTRIVES.pdf)

**Damage evidence: Apollo 12 / Surveyor III**
- Apollo 12's LM landed ~160 m from Surveyor III; returned parts showed a thin layer scoured away (permanent "shadows"), hundreds of pits, and pit analysis indicated impacting particle velocities between 300 m/s and 2 km/s. [MEASURED] — [Metzger, Immer & Lane 2009/2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.06248). (A 2023 Frontiers paper gives the separation as 180 m [SNIPPET-ONLY] — [Frontiers Mech. Eng. 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/mechanical-engineering/articles/10.3389/fmech.2023.1116330/pdf); the NASA ALSJ gives no distance.)
- NASA SP-284 (1972): exhaust-accelerated dust "literally sandblasted" Surveyor III, removing most of the camera's 31-month discoloration except in shielded areas; sharp shadow edges imply dust "traveling in excess of 100 m/sec" with lunar gravity only slightly curving its path. [MEASURED] — [NASA ALSJ, "Sandblasting Surveyor III"](https://nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/static/history/alsj/UL12Sandblast.html)
- A modified Roberts' erosion theory predicts ~3 divots/cm² on Surveyor hardware, "very favorably" matching observed divot density. [MODELLED vs MEASURED] — [Metzger, arXiv 2104.05198](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.05198)
- Re-analysis of Surveyor III coupons: the LM plume removed more dust than it deposited; several mechanisms proposed. [MEASURED] — [Lane, Trigwell, Hintze & Metzger 2023, arXiv 2306.01087](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2306.01087)
- Pre-mission planners targeted ~500 ft (150 m) separation "to minimize the amount of sandblasting"; a retrospective comment notes "500 ft wasn't nearly enough". [MEASURED outcome; informal source] — [collectSPACE forum](https://www.collectspace.com/ubb/Forum29/HTML/001900.html)
- Apollo-era ascent dust: Apollo 11's EASEP passive seismometer (17 m from the LM per snippet) "greatly overheated by over 50 degrees F above its maximum top planned temperature" because LM ascent exhaust threw dust onto its gold surfaces; it failed after 21 Earth days. [MEASURED] — [O'Brien, "EASEP and ALSEPs"](https://www.brianjobrien.com/easep-and-alseps). Snippets of the Apollo Lunar Surface Journal state that this led to the Apollo 12 ALSEP being deployed 130 m from its LM and that "generally, 100 meters to the west of the Lunar Module was seen as adequate"; on Apollo 15 an ascent-blown blanket flew ~110 m at ~20 m/s and landed 11 m from the ALSEP central station; NASA's lesson was to "ensure that landing areas are sufficiently far from experiment sites and structures". [SNIPPET-ONLY; the ALSJ pages now redirect] — [ALSJ Apollo 12 ALSEP deployment](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/static/history/alsj/a12/a12.alsepdep.html), [ALSJ WOTM Apollo 15 blanket flight](https://www.nasa.gov/history/alsj/WOTM/WOTM-A15BlanketFlight.html)
- Blue Ghost M1 lessons: landing near a crater "combined with regolith accumulation on radiating surfaces caused temperature exceedances over the course of the lunar day", forcing longer quiescent periods and use of the antenna as a sunshade. [MEASURED, operational] — [Tackett et al., Firefly, LPSC 2026 #1958](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2026/pdf/1958.pdf)

**Standoff / keep-out guidance that exists**
- NASA 2011 recommendations for U.S. heritage sites: a 2.0 km Descent/Landing (D/L) exclusion radius around Apollo and Surveyor lander sites and 0.5 km around impact sites; approach trajectories must stay tangential to the boundary (no overflight); touchdown targeted "no less than 2.0 km" including the dispersion ellipse. Rationale: gas-flow-code analysis shows a "nearly flat sheet of blowing material... between 1 and 3 degrees elevation", particle ejection velocities "between 300 and 2000 m/s", and negligible atmosphere so "particles continue at that velocity until striking the lunar surface far away. Some particles travel almost all the way" around the Moon. Heritage lander hardware is elevated and "exposed to high-velocity particle impacts", hence the larger radius than for buried impact sites. [POLICY] — [NASA, "Recommendations to Space-Faring Entities", Rev A, Oct 2011](https://www.nasa.gov/pdf/617743main_NASA-USG_LUNAR_HISTORIC_SITES_RevA-508.pdf)
- Metzger (a member of the 2011 group) on the 2 km figure: "It's just a number we made up because we couldn't do any better at the time." The 2024 erosion paper says the author and a colleague "selected the 2 km distance arbitrarily". [POLICY/PLACEHOLDER] — [NPR via KUNC, Feb 2019](https://www.kunc.org/npr-news/2019-02-21/how-do-you-preserve-history-on-the-moon); [arXiv 2403.18583](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.18583). NBC: the recommendations are voluntary; artifact boundaries are 75 m (Apollo 11) and 225 m (Apollo 17). — [NBC News 2012](https://www.nbcnews.com/id/wbna47557457)
- NASA KSC Swamp Works (Gelino, Earth & Space 2026): "Distance reduces risks due to diminished ejecta flux and early drop out of larger particles"; "No safe scientific or operational standoff distances have been defined for various landers". Their low-fidelity LUNEXT sim assumes ejecta at 1 km/s and 3°. [DESIGN ASSUMPTION] — [NTRS 20260002816](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260002816/downloads/329%20-%20Site%20Plan%20and%20Bulk%20Presentation.pdf)
- Mars analogue (unprepared site, ~40 t human-class lander): 3 mm particles travel farthest (~700 m) and hit at ~43 m/s; "a minimum blast radius of 1 km should be sufficient to protect hardware around the landing vehicle. However, this radius should be increased by some safety factor"; shielding hardware could reduce the radius. Note this is a Mars (atmospheric drag) result; on the Moon drag is absent, so the same particles go much farther. [MODELLED] — [Metzger, Immer & Lane 2009/2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.06248)
- Constellation-era outpost study: the Shackleton-rim landing zone was 2.2 km from the habitation/power zone and 290 m lower; for a 40 t lander, particles would reach the habitation zone only if ejected upward at ≥10.5° (1 µm), 12.4° (10 µm), 16° (40 µm), 21° (100 µm) and >50° (>1 mm); the uphill slope gives "some natural protection" but crater formation modifies the ejection angle "permitting some damage to the outpost. Thus, additional mitigation may be needed." [MODELLED] — Metzger et al., LPSC abstract "Lander Plume Effects at Outpost on Shackleton Crater Rim" (URL unresolved; see Gaps)
- Terrain: a 2014 NASA presentation concluded "terrain plays a major role in trajectory mitigation and should be considered at candidate landing sites". [SNIPPET-ONLY] — [NTRS 20140017853](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20140017853/downloads/20140017853.pdf)
- 2020 Decadal white paper (Watkins, Metzger, Mehta et al.) reviews PSI knowledge and gaps and recommends descent imagers on every surface mission and open PSI data. — [arXiv 2102.12312](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.12312)

**Large-lander (Starship/Blue Moon) statements**
- Starship IFT-1 (Earth, April 2023): concrete debris was thrown up to 10 km at ~90 m/s; Metzger: in lunar gravity such pad-failure debris "could travel distances up to 6 kilometers"; a lunar liftoff pad failure "would be loss of vehicle or loss of crew". SpaceX "intends to position the lander's thrusters higher on the vessel to reduce the dust and debris stirred up on touchdown and takeoff". [MEASURED Earth event; MODELLED lunar extrapolation; company statement] — [Aerospace America, 29 Jan 2024](https://aerospaceamerica.aiaa.org/researchers-starship-pad-destruction-could-hold-lessons-for-lunar-landings/)
- Modelling of Starship landings near PSRs: a single landing could generate >10 t of water/icy exhaust products; four landings could add as much contamination as existing PSR surface frost; Apollo-class landers would add <1%. No SpaceX or NASA response. [MODELLED] — [Popular Science, 9 Jul 2024](https://www.popsci.com/science/starship-moon-ice)
- A 2023 ICES paper (JPL with Blue Origin inputs) simulated Blue Moon descent-engine plumes in the final metres; principal concern is dust deposition on thermal radiators, sensors, optics and solar arrays and visibility loss. [SNIPPET-ONLY; full text behind 403] — [TTU repository](https://ttu-ir.tdl.org/items/34d39d31-f93c-4859-8778-e0450ab29090)
- Purdue pad design paper cites an analysis that ejecta from a 40-ton lander's plume in loose regolith "could escape lunar gravity and threaten cislunar vehicles"; Metzger & Mantovani predict order 2,000–10,000 impacts/m² on Gateway per pass through the sheet (sub-hypervelocity, "slight" damage) but potentially extensive damage to a spacecraft in low lunar orbit. [MODELLED] — [Mount et al., Purdue, NTRS 20250011152](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250011152/downloads/Lunar%20Landing%20Pad_2025-12-06.pdf); [Metzger & Mantovani 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.12234)

### Inferences
- **Scaling to 3 t / 15 t / 100 t landers (no source gives these directly).** Applying Lane & Metzger's thrust^2.5 mass scaling with the LM anchor (5 t landing mass → 2.6 t ejected) gives rough worst-case ejecta masses of ~0.7 t (3 t lander), ~40 t (15 t lander), ~470 t (40 t) and ~4,600 t (100 t). Metzger explicitly calls the 40 t figure "likely an overestimate" because deeper regolith is more compacted, so the 100 t number should be treated as an upper bound, not an estimate. Velocities change little with lander size (log scaling): dust still moves at km/s, and the maximum is capped by exhaust exit velocity (3.1 km/s hypergolic LM; 4.5 km/s H2/O2; methalox would be intermediate).
- **Ranges on a flat surface** (g = 1.62 m/s², no drag): the published 600 m (1 cm gravel at 30 m/s) and 60 m (10 cm rocks at 10 m/s) are v²/g maxima, i.e. the worst-case 45° ramped trajectory, not the nominal 1–3° sheet. For the nominal sheet, dust at 1 km/s and 3° lands ~65 km away after ~65 s; at 2 km/s it travels hundreds of km (curvature dominates). Sand at 70–1000 m/s has a 45° worst-case range of 3 km to >600 km. So at any practical base radius (hundreds of metres to a few km) an unshielded rover in the sheet's line of sight is hit by dust and sand regardless of distance; what distance buys is (a) escape from gravel/rock impacts beyond ~0.6 km (LM-class; farther for bigger landers), (b) lower flux per m² (geometric 1/r), and (c) coverage by terrain shadowing.
- **South pole specifics**: the Constellation Shackleton analysis shows that topography (habitat ~290 m above a landing zone 2.2 km away) screens the low-angle sheet, leaving only ejecta ramped to >10° as a threat; the Lunar Innovation Park work shows the opposite philosophy (land ~20 m apart behind 2.5 m berms). Both imply the controlling variable is line-of-sight blocking of a 1–6° sheet, not raw distance. Choosing a pad on a down-slope or behind a crater rim relative to the base is the cheapest "standoff".
- The literature's effective rules of thumb: ~100–130 m was judged adequate against Apollo LM *ascent* dust for unshielded instruments (and 17 m was not); 150–160 m was *not* enough to prevent sandblasting of Surveyor III by an LM *descent*; 2 km is a placeholder for heritage sites; 1 km (plus safety factor) is Metzger's minimum for an unprepared Mars site with a 40 t lander. For the HLS/Starship class there is no published "safe" distance, only the statement that effects will be far greater than Apollo.

### Gaps
- No source gives ejecta range/velocity explicitly for 3 t, 15 t or 100 t landers at the south pole; the numbers above are scalings from LM and 40 t cases. Metzger's "part 2: benchmarking and predictions" erosion paper (2024/25) was not located within the search budget.
- The LPSC abstract "Lander Plume Effects at Outpost on Shackleton Crater Rim" (Metzger, Britt, Cannon, Kinzel, Fontes, Kring) was read from a PDF copy but its canonical USRA/NTRS URL could not be resolved before the search budget ran out; its numbers should be re-cited from the primary URL.
- No validated damage-threshold distance (flux or impact energy vs. distance) for unshielded rovers was found in any source; the Mars momentum/energy-vs-distance scatterplots in arXiv 2104.06248 are the only published attempt and are not transferable to the Moon.
- SCALPSS erosion volume/mass for Blue Ghost had not been published (as of the 2026 SciTech papers); IM-1/IM-2 and Blue Ghost results from NASA's 2024–2025 PSI test campaign (NTRS 20260004959) were not opened.
- A "consistent correlation between blast zone area and lander dry mass" was reported in a search snippet without an attributable source; not verified.

## Key Question 2: What does the literature say about the trade between pad-to-base distance (hauling cost) and plume damage risk? Has anyone optimised it?

### Takeaway
No published study optimises pad-to-base distance against hauling/cabling cost. The two documented design philosophies bracket the trade: NASA KSC's Lunar Innovation Park (2026) deliberately lands ~20 m from existing assets behind regolith berms "to reduce mission costs and logistics complexity" and minimise cable runs, while Constellation-era planning put the Shackleton landing zone 2.2 km from the habitation/power zone and NASA's heritage guidance uses 2 km; Metzger & Autry's 2022 cost model optimises pad construction mass/time/cost but treats pad radius, not pad location, as the variable.

### Cited Findings
- Lunar Innovation Park (NASA KSC Swamp Works, Gelino, Earth & Space 2026): "Bulk regolith infrastructure (e.g., berms, prepared surfaces) and landing aids enable extreme proximity landings (~20 m)"; layout is a "hub and spokes" industrial area beside a reusable launch/landing pad, designed as a function of landing accuracy, lander geometry, VSAT geometry, plume geometry, ejecta erosion of berms and secondary ejecta trajectories; it "minimizes cable run distances" and "allows for an incremental build up of collocated assets protected from PSI ejecta". Uses ~625 kg medium-scale CLPS landers; single-use landing zone 15 m diameter (5 m landing accuracy + 5 m lander), landing-zone centre spacing 19.9 m (= LZ diameter + 8 m standoff from ejecta-impacted berm face to lander + 4.9 m berm base width); LZ excavated 0.8 m to balance cut/fill, give "surrounding region some protection from ejecta" and reach densified regolith. Berm: 2.5 m high (assumed lander height with VSAT stowed, "may be overly conservative for 3° ejecta angle"), 1 m crest, 52° side slopes, 50% relative density (1.4 g/cm³). Reusable pad: 20 m diameter foundation 1.6 m below grade compacted to 80% RD, 15 m durable surface "built using TBD technology", a large berm protecting the whole industrial area and a shorter semicircular berm protecting the surroundings from 6° ejecta; two 9 m wide, 5°-slope accessways. On a 4° slope berm volume rises 2.6×. Open items: ejecta effects on berms, secondary ejecta, lander flyover/ascent trajectories "to ensure compatibility with the Park layout". [DESIGN ASSUMPTION] — [NTRS 20260002816](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260002816/downloads/329%20-%20Site%20Plan%20and%20Bulk%20Presentation.pdf)
- Lunar Innovation Park storyboard (Hilburger & Gelino, NASA STMD/KSC, 1 Apr 2026): goal to "land in extreme proximity to reduce mission costs and logistics complexity"; Mission 1 (mid-2028) deploys VSAT and an IPEx excavator that maps the site and builds a berm plus an adjacent flat landing site; "the distance between landing sites is ~20 m"; Mission 2 (late 2028) "lands while the VSAT is stowed, and the excavation robot surveils the PSI effects... The berm protects the first lander from PSI ejecta"; later missions cut through berms to pull power cables (first lunar power grid, 2030), bury an ARMADAS shelter under 1 m of regolith (2031), and build a reusable pad foundation and berms (2032). Related publications on site layout, construction-operations analysis and mission design are "pending". [DESIGN ASSUMPTION/CONCEPT] — [NTRS 20260002824](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260002824/downloads/Lunar_Inn_Park_Final_Storyboard_STRIVES_2.pdf)
- Metzger & Autry (New Space, Nov 2022 / arXiv 2022): landing pads are needed because without atmosphere "debris will travel vast distances with no loss of energy" and severity "decreases with distance"; the trade study optimises equipment mass versus "program delay cost" (the lost value of an outpost delayed by slow pad construction) and transportation cost; the most important variables are transport cost to the lunar surface and program delay cost. Pad location/distance from base is not a model variable. [MODELLED economics] — [arXiv 2205.00378](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.00378); journal version [New Space 10.1089/space.2022.0015](https://www.liebertpub.com/doi/10.1089/space.2022.0015)
- Metzger (2009/2021): the blast "may damage ISRU hardware and... ISRU hardware may be essential in controlling the blast"; berms are economical because ejecta is low-angle, but scour holes ramp ejecta higher and make berms/screens ineffective, so "a complete solution to the pluming problems on the Moon probably requires surface stabilization"; conservatism argues for berms *and* stabilised pads; "Trade studies are needed to definitively answer this question." — [arXiv 2104.06248](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.06248)
- Constellation outpost on Shackleton rim: landing zone 2.2 km from and 290 m below the habitation/energy zone; the slope limits exposure to small ejecta on high-angle trajectories, but "additional mitigation may be needed". [MODELLED] — Metzger et al., LPSC abstract (URL unresolved; see Gaps)
- Heritage guidance: 2.0 km landing exclusion, chosen as a placeholder (see Q1). [POLICY] — [NASA 2011](https://www.nasa.gov/pdf/617743main_NASA-USG_LUNAR_HISTORIC_SITES_RevA-508.pdf); [KUNC/NPR](https://www.kunc.org/npr-news/2019-02-21/how-do-you-preserve-history-on-the-moon)
- Masten FAST (NIAC 2020): building the pad during descent would "allow landings close to existing surface assets without pre-built infrastructure" and remove dedicated pad-building missions estimated to cost >$120 M each. [CONCEPT claim] — [NASA NIAC](https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/spacetech/niac/2020_Phase_I_Phase_II/Instant_Landing_Pads_for_Artemis_Lunar_Missions)
- Purdue (Dec 2025): a landing/launch pad should be sized/constructed considering "equipment needs, costs to transport" and other factors; design example is a slab for 50 t landers; no siting trade. — [NTRS 20250011152](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250011152/downloads/Lunar%20Landing%20Pad_2025-12-06.pdf)
- UCF CLASS summary: researchers' consensus is that pads are needed "for lunar missions that repeatedly visit an outpost"; options listed are an impermeable heat-resistant pad centre, gravel/polymer surrounds, berms or fences, and local blast barriers; gravel is ineffective at the pad centre and interlocking tiles can crack or be flipped by gas. — [UCF CLASS, "Do We Need Landing Pads?"](https://sciences.ucf.edu/class/?p=2214)

### Inferences
- The field has effectively replaced "distance" with "line-of-sight blocking plus surface stabilisation" as the primary control. Once a berm (2.5 m) and a prepared surface exist, NASA KSC considers ~20 m spacing acceptable for ~625 kg landers; the equivalent for 15–100 t landers has not been designed, but the same deck says the reusable pad's outer berm must handle a 6° ejecta angle, i.e. twice the nominal 3°.
- The hauling-cost side of the trade is quantified only indirectly: Metzger & Autry's Table 6 shows pad construction itself costs $130–548 M under Artemis constraints and that program-delay cost dominates, which argues for building pads early and close (short cable/road runs) rather than far; the Innovation Park storyboard makes the same argument qualitatively ("reduce mission costs and logistics complexity"). A formal distance optimisation (transport energy per tonne-km vs. expected plume damage vs. berm volume) does not appear to exist in the open literature and is a genuine research gap.
- For an unprepared landing zone the only available distances are placeholders (1 km Mars minimum with safety factor; 2 km heritage), and both were set for ~5–40 t landers; neither has been re-derived for 100 t-class vehicles.

### Gaps
- No paper optimising pad-to-base distance against hauling/cabling/road cost was found; the "pending" NASA KSC publications (site layout, construction-operations analysis, mission design) may fill this but were not yet on NTRS.
- Goudarzi (2026) "Optimization of Lunar Materials for Launch and Landing Pads: NASA STTR Phase I Final Report (80NSSC24PB220)" (Blueshift/Outward Technologies) is cited for the 8 m berm standoff but is a manuscript in preparation; not available.
- The newspaceeconomy.ca "future of lunar spaceports" overview and the X-Arc 2010 "lunar outpost risk" ASCE deck surfaced in search but were not opened.
- ESA LUNAR ISLANDS (TU Dresden-led) berm/pad effectiveness results: abstract only, no numbers public. — [IAC-23 D3.2B.80355](https://iafastro.directory/iac/archive/browse/IAC-23/D3/2B/80355/)

## Key Question 3: What are the construction options and costs/timelines for landing pads, and how much do they reduce ejecta?

### Takeaway
The best-documented options are microwave sintering, in-situ-baked pavers, polymer infusion, gravel "breakwaters" and regolith berms, with Metzger & Autry's 2022 economic model putting an Artemis-era 27-m-radius two-zone pad at ~$130–548 M (appropriated cost, excluding technology maturation) and 192–270 days of robotic construction with ≤50 kW of power; hardware demonstrations to date are Earth-based (PISCES basalt-paver pad 2015–16, ICON's 3D-printed "Lunar PAD" hot-fire 2021, Astroport SBIR/STTR furnaces 2021–24, Masten FAST concept 2020). No source quantifies the fraction of ejecta a pad removes; the literature treats a stabilised pad as eliminating erosion inside the outer radius (by design: shear stress below the erosion threshold) and berms as blocking the residual low-angle sheet, with the caveat that pad failure can create worse, platy ejecta.

### Cited Findings

**Pad geometry and design basis**
- Two-zone pad: inner zone must survive stagnation temperature/pressure at touchdown and the ignition shock at launch; outer zone must stop erosion over a larger radius. Illustrative radii: inner 12 m, outer 27 m, adapted from van Susante & Metzger's 5 m / 20 m for a 40 t lander with clustered engines, plus 7 m (2 m engine offset + 5 m landing-accuracy margin). The outer radius is where plume shear stress drops below the erosion threshold of any lunar soil (Roberts' equations). Thicknesses used: 7.62 cm inner, 2.54 cm outer. [MODELLED/DESIGN] — [Metzger & Autry 2022, arXiv 2205.00378](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.00378)
- Purdue structural design for up to 50 t landers (9 m footprint, 1 m footpads): a microwave-sintered slab needs ~14 in (0.36 m) thickness to avoid flexural cracking under a single-leg touchdown; punching shear is satisfied; worst-case launch plume surface temperatures 3400 °C at 0.7 m radius falling to 2100 °C at ~1 m; thermal penetration is confined to the top ~80 mm; model pad 18 m diameter. An earlier FE study had proposed a 1.0 m thick pad. Lander classes tabulated: 10 t, Blue Moon (45 t), 100 t. [MODELLED] — [Mount et al., Purdue, Dec 2025, NTRS 20250011152](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250011152/downloads/Lunar%20Landing%20Pad_2025-12-06.pdf)
- NASA KSC reusable pad concept: 20 m diameter compacted foundation (80% RD) 1.6 m below grade; 15 m durable surface by "TBD technology"; berms protect industrial area and surroundings (6° ejecta); teleoperated RASSOR 2.0 berm test reached 61% relative density by repeated trafficking (Black Point-1 simulant, Earth ambient). [DESIGN; LAB] — [NTRS 20260002816](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260002816/downloads/329%20-%20Site%20Plan%20and%20Bulk%20Presentation.pdf)

**Construction methods, mass, energy, time (Metzger & Autry 2022)**
- Non-optimised process figures (Table 1): grading inner pad 0.05 d / 0.6 t; grading outer 0.21 d; compacting inner 0.10 d, outer 0.43 d / 0.5 t; microwave sintering inner 4.1 d, 19.7 MWh, 4.7 t hardware, 4 rovers, 200 kW; sintering outer 14.1 d, 67.8 MWh; pavers inner 39.5 d, 41.3 MWh, 1.8 t; pavers outer 21.8 d, 55.9 MWh; gravel/rock outer 35.4 d, 0.81 MWh, 1.6 t; polymer outer 0.56 d, 0.011 MWh, 7.6 t (7.2 t is polymer). Inner-zone pavers need ~84 kg grout from Earth and ~55 h of grouting. — [arXiv 2205.00378](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.00378)
- Economic optimum with $300K/kg transport and high delay cost: sinter-sinter (SiSi) with 5.3 t hardware: 1.0 d grade/compact, 5.0 d inner, 17.3 d outer. Fully optimised (Table 5, $160B outpost, 4× value): $1M/kg → SiSi, 2 rovers, 1.64 t sintering hardware, 91 kW, 41.6 d, total $4.46B (appropriated $2.38B); $100K/kg → sinter+polymer (SiPo), 155 kW, 6.8 d, $1.50B ($1.11B); $10K/kg → 429 kW, 2.5 d, $292M ($143M); $2K/kg → 764 kW, 1.4 d, $181M ($81M). — [arXiv 2205.00378](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.00378)
- "Programmatic realism" Artemis case (Table 6: 270-day schedule, 50 kW power cap, yearly cadence): $1M/kg → SiSi, 1 rover (100 kg), 316 kg sintering hardware, 18 kW, 11 d grading + 58 d inner + 201 d outer = 270 d, appropriated cost $548M; $300K/kg → same hardware, $229M; $100K/kg → 445 kg sintering hardware, 25 kW, 192 d, $130M; $10K/kg (120 d, 50 kW) → $47.4M in 95 d; $2K/kg (30 d, 100 kW) → SiPo with 7.2 t polymer, 19.5 d, $27.5M. Estimates exclude maturation from TRL 3/4 to TRL 6. Microwave sintering is currently most favourable for the inner zone; the outer zone switches from sintering to polymer infusion when transport drops below ~$110K/kg. — [arXiv 2205.00378](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.00378); summarised in [UCF News](https://www.ucf.edu/news/methods-for-building-lunar-landing-pads-may-involve-microwaving-moon-soil/) and [Space Settlement Progress, Feb 2023](https://spacesettlementprogress.com/lunar-landing-pad-trade-study/)
- Pad construction time interacts with the lunar night: non-optimised paver+polymer took 40.85 d; surface operations halted at sunset (every 29.5 d) delay to next sunrise; the fiducial program-delay cost of a 40.85 d build was $576M versus $77M for a 5.45 d sinter+polymer build. — [arXiv 2205.00378](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.00378)

**Hardware demonstrations and programmes**
- PISCES / NASA KSC Swamp Works ACME project (Hawaii): 100 kiln-sintered Hawaiian-basalt interlocking pavers laid by the tele-operated Helelani rover (controlled from KSC) to form a landing-pad "bullseye", completed December 2015 after grading/compaction to meet a 30 Sept NASA milestone; durability to be checked with a simulated rocket-plume test. [LAB/field demo] — [PISCES, 5 Jan 2016](https://pacificspacecenter.com/2016/01/05/pisces-completes-lunar-landing-pad-bullseye/); project launch — [PISCES, Jun 2015](https://pacificspacecenter.com/2015/06/01/pisces-launches-robotic-space-construction-project/)
- Sintered basalt blocks (2018 PISCES study) withstood the "high velocity and high temperature of a solid fuel rocket motor" but the interlocking design "caused the blocks to crack and separate during the rocket test"; recommendations for improvement given. [LAB] — [ASCE Earth & Space 2022](https://ascelibrary.com/doi/10.1061/9780784484470.018)
- ICON "Lunar PAD" (Lunar Plume Alleviation Device, student concept from a 2019 NASA proposal course, 10 universities): subscale pad 3D-printed in cement (~7 h printing + 14 h infill) with a roof, petal-like exhaust channels venting at the edges, a central cone, and a perimeter wall "to capture lunar dust"; instrumented for temperature, strain and flow; hot-fire at Camp Swift, TX, "performed true to design". No ejecta-reduction figure. [LAB] — [3D Printing Industry, 22 Mar 2021](https://3dprintingindustry.com/news/icon-and-nasa-bring-lunar-infrastructure-closer-with-worlds-first-3d-printed-rocket-pad-187323/)
- ICON Project Olympus: $57.2 M NASA SBIR Phase III (Nov/Dec 2022) to develop a regolith 3D-printing construction system; includes a lunar-gravity simulation flight and testing Apollo regolith samples; research will "inform future lunar construction approaches" including landing pads, blast shields and roads. A search snippet gives the contract term as running through 2028. — [3D Printing Industry, Dec 2022](https://3dprintingindustry.com/news/icon-awarded-new-57-2m-nasa-contract-to-develop-system-than-can-3d-print-lunar-structures-218125/); ICON's DARPA LunA-10 deck assumes a sintered-regolith, low-CTE ceramic pad material — [DARPA ICON LunA-10](https://www.darpa.mil/sites/default/files/attachment/2024-12/icon-luna-10.pdf)
- Astroport Space Technologies (San Antonio): 2021 NASA STTR Phase I with UTSA for an induction furnace/nozzle to form and place regolith bricks for landing pads — [UTSA Today, May 2021](https://www.utsa.edu/today/2021/05/story/astroport-partnership-moon-construction.html); 2023 SBIR Phase I for the "Lunatron" furnace nozzle and an STTR on electrostatic regolith flow (XArc/Astroport total ~$1.3 M) — [SatNow 2023](https://satnow.com/news/details/1348-xarc-and-astroport-receive-1-3-million-in-multiple-nasa-sbir-sttr-awards-for-2023); 2024 STTR Phase II 80NSSC24CA021 "bulk regolith handling technologies for landing/launch pad site preparation and blast shield construction", up to ~$844,773 — [HigherGov](https://www.highergov.com/contract/80NSSC24CA021/)
- Masten FAST (in-Flight Alumina Spray Technique), NIAC Phase I 2020 (M. Kuhns): engineered particles injected into the descent plume coat the regolith into a hardened layer before touchdown; claimed to reduce ejecta abrasion and deep cratering and to avoid dedicated pad-building missions (>$120 M each); pad size, mass and time not given. [CONCEPT] — [NASA NIAC 2020](https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/spacetech/niac/2020_Phase_I_Phase_II/Instant_Landing_Pads_for_Artemis_Lunar_Missions); related Masten/UCF/MTU cratering work — [Kuhns et al., Earth & Space 2021](https://digitalcommons.mtu.edu/michigantech-p/15789)
- Berm alternatives: inflatable/deployable blast screens have a mass-per-metre cost but are quick to erect; excavators built for ISRU oxygen production could double as berm builders. — [arXiv 2104.06248](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.06248)
- Pad material hazards: in a Swamp Works test hot gas cracked a tile, built up pressure beneath it and caused "catastrophic failure"; gravel does not work at pad centre; interlocking tiles can be flipped by gas. — [UCF CLASS](https://sciences.ucf.edu/class/?p=2214). Pad failure would create "larger, possibly platy-shaped ejecta with large surface-to-mass ratio" that are blown more easily than natural rocks. — [arXiv 2104.06248](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.06248)

**Ejecta reduction by pads/berms**
- Design logic rather than measurement: the outer pad radius is set where plume shear stress falls below the erosion threshold, so a successful pad produces no erosion inside ~20–27 m; the inner zone exists to survive heat/pressure. — [arXiv 2205.00378](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.00378)
- Berms: justified by the 1–3° sheet ("a modest berm of lunar soil" suffices for a flat surface and single engine) but defeated when scour holes ramp ejecta to 7–11° or >22°; NASA KSC therefore sizes its outer berm for 6° and notes 2.5 m "may be overly conservative for 3°". — [arXiv 2104.06248](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.06248); [NTRS 20260002816](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260002816/downloads/329%20-%20Site%20Plan%20and%20Bulk%20Presentation.pdf)
- ESA LUNAR ISLANDS (TU Dresden, TU Berlin, TU Braunschweig, Glasgow, ONERA, ESTEC) is assessing "the effectiveness of in-situ manufactured structures on the containment of high-velocity particles" with cold-gas, hot-fire and numerical tests; only qualitative results public (regolith structures melt above ~1300 K). — [IAC-23](https://iafastro.directory/iac/archive/browse/IAC-23/D3/2B/80355/)

### Inferences
- Realistic Artemis-era pad cost ($130–548 M) is comparable to one CLPS delivery, and build time (6–9 months at ≤50 kW) spans several lunar nights, so a pad must be started at least a year before the first large lander it is meant to protect. Build time collapses to days only with hundreds of kW, which no near-term surface power plan provides.
- None of the demonstrated hardware (PISCES pavers, ICON cement Lunar PAD) has been tested at HLS-class heat flux or on the Moon; the only flight-relevant "pad" data remain Apollo/Surveyor/Chang'e/Blue Ghost on unprepared regolith.
- Quantitatively, pads convert the problem from "hundreds of tonnes of ejecta at km/s" (unprepared, 40 t class) to "residual gas-only plume plus whatever the pad surface sheds"; the open risk is pad failure, which the sources say could be worse than no pad.

### Gaps
- No measured ejecta-mass or flux reduction factor for any pad or berm design was found; ESA LUNAR ISLANDS and NASA's PSI test campaign are the likely future sources.
- The Lunar PAD motor class/thrust and the PISCES Class-M motor test results were not quantified in the sources opened.
- Astroport's current TRL and any pad-scale demonstration results were not found.
- The DARPA LunA-10 ICON deck was not opened beyond the search snippet.

## Key Question 4: Are there recommended operational windows around a landing (rovers parked behind berms, time to clear dust)?

### Takeaway
No published "no-drive window" or dust-clearance time for rovers was found. The only explicit operational protocols are (a) NASA's 2011 heritage rules (approach tangential to a 2 km boundary, no overflight, touchdown ≥2 km), (b) NASA KSC's Lunar Innovation Park sequence in which the neighbouring lander's vertical solar array is stowed and the excavation robot stands by behind a 2.5 m berm while a new lander touches down ~20 m away, and (c) Apollo practice of siting unshielded instruments ≥100–130 m from the LM to survive ascent dust. Physics sets the window: with no atmosphere, ejecta is ballistic, so the dust/sand sheet reaches a rover 1 km away within ~1 s of ejection and nearly all sub-escape ejecta is back on the ground within ~1–2 minutes of engine cutoff; only a sparse near-escape fraction returns over days to weeks.

### Cited Findings
- NASA 2011: visiting-vehicle trajectory "should remain tangential to the D/L boundary to ensure no overflight"; overflight risks un-burned propellant deposition and collision; touchdown/impact points "targeted to a distance of no less than 2.0 km" including the dispersion ellipse; the exclusion also covers "system failure during descent" (instantaneous impact point in the trajectory plane). [POLICY] — [NASA 2011 Rev A](https://www.nasa.gov/pdf/617743main_NASA-USG_LUNAR_HISTORIC_SITES_RevA-508.pdf)
- Lunar Innovation Park: "Mission 2 lands while the VSAT is stowed, and the excavation robot surveils the PSI effects"; "The berm protects the first lander from PSI ejecta"; berm height 2.5 m equals the lander height with VSAT stowed; the large berm around the reusable pad "protects all Park assets including VSATs in extended condition"; lander flyover and ascent/descent trajectories must be designed "to ensure compatibility with the Park layout". [DESIGN/CONCEPT] — [NTRS 20260002824](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260002824/downloads/Lunar_Inn_Park_Final_Storyboard_STRIVES_2.pdf); [NTRS 20260002816](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260002816/downloads/329%20-%20Site%20Plan%20and%20Bulk%20Presentation.pdf)
- Metzger & Mantovani: protection of orbiting spacecraft "may be provided through timing the landings relative to positions of the orbiting spacecraft (COLlision Avoidance, or COLA)"; the ejecta sheet persists for "several" Gateway passes before dispersing. [MODELLED] — [arXiv 2305.12234](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.12234)
- Ballistic nature: "In the vacuum of the Moon, motion of all particles is completely ballistic." [SNIPPET-ONLY] — [NTRS 20090022233](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20090022233/downloads/20090022233.pdf). Near-escape particles re-impact after "several days to weeks". [MODELLED] — [Rivera 2021](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/etd2020/749/). About 10% of 1.7–2.3 km/s dust falls back, much near the landing site. [MODELLED] — [Wittal et al. 2020](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20205003590)
- Apollo instrument siting against *ascent* dust: EASEP ~17 m from LM overheated and failed; later ALSEPs ~100–130 m away (see Q1 findings). — [O'Brien](https://www.brianjobrien.com/easep-and-alseps); [ALSJ snippets](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/static/history/alsj/a12/a12.alsepdep.html)
- Dust deposition consequences for a lander's own systems (and by extension nearby rovers): Blue Ghost's radiators accumulated regolith at landing and overheated during the lunar day. [MEASURED] — [LPSC 2026 #1958](https://www.hou.usra.edu/meetings/lpsc2026/pdf/1958.pdf). Blue Moon analyses focus on dust deposition on radiators, sensors, optics and solar arrays and on visibility loss during landing. [SNIPPET-ONLY] — [TTU 2023](https://ttu-ir.tdl.org/items/34d39d31-f93c-4859-8778-e0450ab29090)
- Apollo descent visibility: Conrad reported dust from ~300 ft altitude on Apollo 12 and Bean warned of dust at 50 ft; dust clouds caused visibility issues and sensor damage during Apollo landings and Apollo 15 landed tilted after the ejecta cloud obscured a crater. [MEASURED] — [NASA ALSJ](https://nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/static/history/alsj/UL12Sandblast.html); [NTRS 20140017853 (snippet)](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20140017853/downloads/20140017853.pdf)
- Sandblasting degrades "optics, solar cells, thermal control surfaces, and moving joints on mechanisms" — the rover subsystems most at risk. — [UCF CLASS](https://sciences.ucf.edu/class/?p=2214)

### Inferences
- **Exposure window, not settling time.** Because there is no atmosphere, there is no suspended-dust phase to wait out. For a rover at distance r in line of sight of the pad, exposure begins when the lander's plume first erodes the surface (Apollo: dust visible from ~30–90 m altitude, i.e. the last ~30–60 s of descent) and ends ~r/v after engine cutoff (≈1 s at 1 km for 1 km/s dust); higher-angle, slower ejecta from crater ramping (e.g. 100 m/s at 20°) lands within ~40 s and ~4 km. Hence a conservative "no-drive / shelter" window is: from ~2 minutes before planned touchdown until ~2 minutes after engine cutoff, and the same again around any ascent. Only the sparse near-escape fraction (days–weeks) persists, and it is a global, low-flux background rather than an operational constraint.
- **What to do in the window.** The documented mitigations are positional, not temporal: park behind a berm or terrain (2.5 m berm for a ~625 kg lander; the KSC deck implies taller for larger landers or ≥6° ejecta), stow or orient deployables (VSAT stowed during the neighbour's landing), and keep optics/radiators facing away from the pad. A rover beyond the gravel range (~0.6 km for LM-class, larger for HLS-class) but in line of sight still receives dust/sand at km/s, so parking out of line of sight matters more than distance.
- **Trajectory discipline** (approach tangential, no overflight of assets, ascent/descent azimuths designed against the base layout) is the one operational rule that appears in both the 2011 NASA guidance and the 2026 KSC concept, and should be treated as a baseline requirement for any base with a co-located pad.

### Gaps
- No source defines a numeric no-drive/no-EVA window, a required rover shelter geometry, or a post-landing inspection/cleaning interval; the inferences above are derived from ballistic physics and the cited protocols, not from published operations guidance.
- NASA Artemis Base Camp documents giving pad-to-habitat distances or landing-day operations rules were not found; a search for "Artemis Base Camp landing zone distance" returned only general layout principles.
- Lane & Metzger 2012 ("Ballistics of lunar ejecta") and 2015 (Acta Geophysica) were not opened directly; their time-of-flight and range tables would replace the back-of-envelope numbers here.
- Whether CLPS/Artemis rover operators (e.g., for a rover already on the surface when a second lander arrives nearby) have formal keep-out procedures could not be confirmed; the web-search budget was exhausted before this could be pursued.
