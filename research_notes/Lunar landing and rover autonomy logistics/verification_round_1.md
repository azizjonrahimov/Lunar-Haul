## 1. Grogan & de Weck 2012, "Federated Simulation and Gaming Framework for a Decentralized Space-Based Resource Economy"

**What was unverified.** The novelty note knew the paper only by title via ref. [84] of the Ho 2024 review and asked whether it "already simulates priced landing/transport services among independent actors", which would pre-empt the landing-pad slot-pricing contribution.

**What I found.** Full citation: Grogan, P. T., and O. L. de Weck, "Federated Simulation and Gaming Framework for a Decentralized Space-Based Resource Economy," in *Earth and Space 2012*, pp. 1468-1477, American Society of Civil Engineers, 2012, DOI 10.1061/9780784412190.156. Author's final manuscript is open access (CC BY-NC-SA 3.0) at http://hdl.handle.net/1721.1/84055; the PDF was retrieved from the DSpace REST endpoint https://dspace.mit.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/621fa1b9-f14c-4faa-b701-1dcc6d8dcf51/content (the handle page and the `/bitstreams/.../download` route returned HTTP 429 on every attempt; the ASCE page returned 403). Citation counts: 7 (Semantic Scholar, https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1061/9780784412190.156), 4 (OpenAlex). The paper is ten proceedings pages, roughly 4,000 words, with no results section; it is a framework proposal.

Abstract, verbatim: "Future human space exploration will require large amounts of resources for shielding and building materials, propellants, and consumables. A space-based resource economy could produce, transport, and store resource at distributed locations such as the lunar surface, stable orbits, or Lagrange points to avoid Earths deep gravity well. Design challenges include decentralized operation and management and socio-technical complexities not commonly addressed by modeling and simulation methods. This paper seeks to tackle these challenges by applying aspects of military wargaming to promote effective communication between decision-makers. A software architecture for federated simulation based on IEEE-1516 (HLA-Evolved) is presented in the context of multiple lunar in-situ resource production processes, resource depots, and intermediate transportation. The federation-level framework identifies interfaces between simulation models (federates), focusing on persistent assets (elements) and resources exchanged. Future work will develop the federated resource economy model and evaluate with decision-makers playing the roles of competing and collaborating players."

What it does, with the load-bearing quotes:

- Economic actors and money: "Since a player may command more than one element under a particular organization, a player object maintains the account balance measured in monetary resources. Any monetary resources exchanged by a players elements are transferred to the account balance. Some scenarios may also use an environment object, controlled by a non-player federate, to identify attributes external to the resource economy boundary, such as market prices, resource concentrations, or natural effects."
- Transactions: "Interaction models must be negotiated between players as a two-step request and response process. One player requests an interaction, and only if the other responds affirmatively, does the interaction take place. The element transport interaction describes the transportation of elements to a destination by a carrier, such as performed by a launch vehicle, in exchange for resources. The resource exchange interaction describes the simultaneous exchange of resources between two co-located elements. For example, a resource exchange at a propellant depot may trade propellant for money."
- Research questions it poses (not answers): "What phases of build-up (staged deployment) can contribute to stable intermediate forms of a resource economy? Are there key technologies, cost levels, or incentives that provide a tipping point for the viability of a resource economy? How do availability and reliability of in-situ processing plants influence the function of a resource economy?"
- Application case (Figure 3): launch vehicle, LEO, in-space transportation, EML1 resource depot, LLPO, "Ascent-descent", "In-situ Production". Table 1 lists "Descent/ascent vehicle" and "Surface vehicle" as component types, but the surface is a single node in the application case.
- Status of the evaluation: "Beta testing with volunteer subjects and a simplified scenario based on the lunar in-situ resource economy is targeted for spring 2012, followed by more detailed scenario development and evaluation using representative players in the summer and fall 2012."
- Lineage: builds on SpaceNet ("SpaceNet is a discrete event simulation tool for space exploration logistics analysis (Grogan, 2010)") and on de Weck's five-process/five-operand classification.

What it does not do: the words "auction", "bid", "pricing", "landing pad", "pad", "slot", "schedule", "plume", "dispersion", "rover", "dispatch" and "surface logistics" do not occur. Prices are either exogenous environment attributes ("market prices") or implicit in one-to-one negotiated exchanges; there is no price-formation or allocation mechanism, no congestion or capacity on any resource, no landing-site or surface-transport model, and no simulation results.

**Status.** Verified (full text read). Related items not readable in this session (HTTP 429 on DSpace handles): the companion MIT ESD working paper "Strategic Engineering Gaming for Improved Design and Interoperation of Infrastructure Systems" (ESD-WP-2012-11, hdl 1721.1/102926, which per its record contains "a prototype game built on a space-based resource economy") and Grogan's 2014 MIT PhD thesis (hdl 1721.1/87081). Grogan's later satellite work (search hit: "Sealed bid reverse auction pricing mechanisms for federated satellite systems", TUM portal) does contain an auction mechanism, but for federated satellite services, not lunar surface assets.

**Consequence for the novelty claims.** Grogan & de Weck 2012 does not pre-empt "landing-pad slot pricing in a surface dispatch marketplace". It should be cited as the antecedent for multi-owner, decentralized, monetary-account space-economy simulation (federation of players and elements, negotiated bilateral transport/exchange interactions), with the explicit distinction that it (i) proposes a framework rather than reporting a model or results, (ii) treats prices as exogenous or bilaterally negotiated rather than mechanism-determined, and (iii) has no landing or surface-logistics layer. Novelty item 5 in `novelty_prior_art.md` can move from "medium until read" to "defensible, with Grogan & de Weck 2012 cited as framework precedent and Grogan's satellite reverse-auction work cited as the nearest mechanism precedent outside the lunar surface domain".

## 2. NASA ADD Rev B, Rev C and the 2025 Architecture Update: objective LI-5, the landing radius and pad-to-habitat distance

**What was unverified.** The report says "No numeric CLPS cargo-lander accuracy requirement found; ADD Rev B objective LI-5 'defined radius' not read (file too large)"; the sigma sweep needs the governing requirement.

**Sources fetched and read (text-extracted in full).**
- ADD Rev B.1: NTRS 20240015571, https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240015571/downloads/ESDMD-001%20-%20ADD%20Rev%20B1%20Final.pdf (12.96 MB; designation "ESDMD-001 Rev-B.1"; "This second revision (Rev-B) of the ADD, developed to support NASA's 2024 Architecture Concept Review (ACR)"; NTRS lists publication date 1 April 2025).
- ADD Rev C: NASA/TM-20250010956, "ESDMD-001: Moon to Mars Architecture Definition Document | Revision C - 2025", December 2025, https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250010956/downloads/ADD%20Revision%20C%20-%2020260115.pdf (35.5 MB).
- 2025 Architecture Update: https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/2025-architecture-update-for-publication.pdf (13.2 MB, graphics-heavy, 31 kB of text).
- NASA 2023 white paper "Safe and Precise Landing at Lunar Sites", NTRS 20230016301, https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230016301/downloads/Safe%20and%20Precise%20Landing%20at%20Lunar%20Sites.pdf.
- 2022 Moon to Mars Objectives as reproduced in a NASA briefing to the National Academies ASEB: https://www.nationalacademies.org/cdn/materials/9fba0a13-4513-49be-8b93-f5908ec7d586.

**What I found.**

(a) The objective itself. "LI-5L: Demonstrate precision landing capabilities in support of continuous human lunar presence and a robust lunar economy." (ASEB briefing copy of the 2022 Objectives; the ADD writes the ID as "LI-05 L"). Rev B p. 59 (objectives table) carries the same wording, and the Rev B narrative reads: "Expansion of the power (LI-1), communications/position/navigation/timing (LI-2, LI-3), transportation (LI-5, LI-6), mobility (LI-6), ISRU (LI-7), infrastructure (LI-4, LI-8), and utilization (LI-9) sub-architectures builds toward the LI goal".

(b) The "defined radius" wording is not the objective but its characteristic/need, Rev B p. 59: CN-T-401 L, "Demonstrate the capability for lunar landers to reliably and safely land within a defined radius around an intended location." (Mars twin CN-T-401 M: "Demonstrate the capability for Mars landers to safely land within a defined radius around an intended location.") The decomposition below it, Rev B p. 283: use cases UC-T-401 L "Landing of crew lander(s) at specific pre-defined locations" and UC-T-402 L "Landing of cargo lander(s) at specific pre-defined locations"; functions FN-T-401 L "Provide precision landing for crew transport to the lunar surface", FN-T-402 L "Provide precision landing for cargo transport to the lunar surface", FN-T-403 L "Enable landing on the lunar surface under all lighting conditions". FN-T-402 L is mapped to the Lunar Surface Cargo Lander element (Rev B section 3.2.4.11.3, p. 127).

(c) Rev B gives no number. The only quantitative hook is technology gap ESDMD #1101 "Lunar Precision Landing and Hazard Avoidance for Human Exploration" (Rev B p. 434): "Advancement in precision landing and hazard avoidance (PL&HA) is needed to safely and reliably aggregate surface elements at lunar South Pole explorations sites." Current state of the art: "Systems capable of soft-touchdowns on illuminated landing sites at the lunar South Pole." Performance target: "Enable crew and cargo to be landed at lunar south pole exploration sites in various illumination conditions with TBD accuracy." Impact: "Without gap closure, the ability to land crew and cargo in close proximity to other surface elements and in low-visibility conditions is reduced." Traceability: "UC-T-401 L -- All FN; UC-T-402 L -- All FN".

(d) Rev C (December 2025) fills in the TBD. Gap 1101, Rev C p. 251, performance target: "Enable crew and cargo to be landed at lunar South Pole region exploration sites in various illumination conditions with 50m landing accuracy for FE segment with <10 minute initialization time to confirm position within 25m." Current state of the art: "Systems capable of soft-touchdowns on illuminated landing sites at the lunar South Pole region." For comparison the Mars gap 1102 (Rev C p. 252) states "Landing accuracy on order of 100 m. Detect and avoid obstacles on order of 1 m in diameter/depth." and recalls "The Perseverance Terrain Relative Navigation system enabled landing accuracy to within 5 m of the final designated landing target." Rev C also adds two data gaps traced to LI-5: DN-017 L "In situ measurement of particle velocity during lunar plume surface interaction (PSI) phenomena" and DN-018 L "In situ measurement of landing site alteration imaging at small scale on the lunar surface", both "Objective SE-07 LM, LI-05 L, TH-03 L" (Rev C pp. 280-281).

(e) NASA's 2023 white paper gives the only prose definition of the number: "Precision landing generally refers to the process of navigating a spacecraft to a safe landing location in close proximity to a specified target. For example, a precision landing could be qualified as a requirement to safely touch down within 50 to 100 meters of a given target on the lunar surface." It also states the logistics rationale the paper needs: "Implementing PL&HA systems on a spacecraft enables the landing of multiple assets within a targeted surface region or landing zone while avoiding collisions and limiting damage to existing surface assets. This mitigates the risk of unsafe touchdown for new landers and reduces post-landing travel distances between surface assets." and "As an example, landing a logistics module closer to a habitat module would ease the transfer of items from one module to the other. Keeping assets within extravehicular activity walking distances or within rover mobility distances enables efficiency of time spent on science and utilization." and "As the number of government and commercial landers on the surface increases, there will be a critical need to ensure that associated keep-out zones are respected."

(f) Pad-to-habitat distance: no ADD revision states one. Rev B section 3.2.4.3.2 (p. 117): "As stated in NASA's 2024 'Lunar Mobility Drivers and Needs' white paper, several factors may drive NASA to select habitation points that are separated from landing sites. This means the architecture will need to provide capabilities for elements, including the initial surface habitat, to move away from landers once on the surface, either using independent or integrated mobility systems." The numbers (tens of metres for shadowing, >1,000 m for blast ejecta, up to 5,000 m to habitation zones) live only in the ACR24 white paper already cited in `lander_descent_numbers.md`. The Rev C cargo-lander element (section 2.3.9, p. 59) says only: "The lunar surface cargo lander delivers assets to the Moon, much like the HDL or CLPS elements. However, the anticipated capability of the element is much smaller than HDL, delivering moderate amounts of cargo to the lunar surface." The 2025 Update mentions landing accuracy only as a data-gap rationale ("to increase confidence in landing accuracy and mobility system navigation") and introduces the Lunar Utility Rover, "a robotic mobility system to move logistics around the lunar surface".

**Status.** Verified (documents read) and Corrected (attribution of the "defined radius" wording; Rev B has no number; Rev C has 50 m / 25 m).

**Consequence for the simulation parameters.** The governing NASA number for the paper's dispersion sweep is now "50m landing accuracy for FE segment" (Rev C gap 1101, December 2025), with a 50-100 m band from the 2023 white paper and 100 m commercial targets (Blue Moon MK1, Griffin). NASA does not state whether 50 m is 1-sigma, 3-sigma, CEP or a hard radius; the sweep should therefore run sigma from ~10 m (SLIM-class, demonstrated) through 50 m and 100 m to the km-scale legacy ellipses, and report results against each interpretation. The pad-to-habitat distance remains a swept parameter anchored on the ACR24 white-paper bands; the ADD should be cited for the policy statement (habitation "separated from landing sites") and for DN-017/DN-018 linking PSI data gaps to LI-5, which supports presenting the keep-out radius as an acknowledged open NASA data gap.

The same text is reprinted at https://cummingsresearchpark.com/?p=10529 (Dec 4, 2020). This is the source Wikipedia's Blue Moon article cites for "as little as 8.9 kN (2,000 lbf)".

No lander masses.
- Secondary mass figures: fueled mass 21,350 kg (Wikipedia, citing Klotz, *Aviation Week*, 17 July 2023, pp. 32-35; not fetched); Gunter's Space Page "Mass: < 21350 kg", "Payload: Cargo variant: 3.6 tons to surface" (the 3.6 t is the 2019 pre-MK1 design figure), https://space.skyrocket.de/doc_sdat/blue-moon.htm, updated 15 Apr 2026; nextspaceflight lists 21,715 kg without a source. Dry mass: not published by Blue Origin, NASA, SpaceNews (Aug 2024), Payload (Mar 2024: "The cargo lander can transport 3,000 kg to the lunar surface"), Gunter's or Wikipedia. Isp: Wikipedia's 460 s is uncited; NewSpaceEconomy's "~453 s (est.)" is an estimate; Blue Origin says only "high specific impulse".

**Resolution of the throttle conflict.** Blue Origin's own figure is 2,000 lbf = 8.9 kN, i.e. 20 % of 10,000 lbf; this agrees with the Wikipedia infobox "20-100 %". The "1,000 lbf / 10 % / ~4.4 kN" figure appears only in NewSpaceEconomy (15 Nov 2025) with no source and should be dropped. Use 44.5 kN maximum (10,000 lbf = 44.48 kN; the "40 kN" in the 2020 release is a rounding).

**Derived landed-mass bound (Modelled, to be labelled Assumed).** From 21,350 kg fueled and 3,000 kg payload, with Isp 440-460 s and g0 = 9.80665 m/s^2 (rocket equation only, no margins):

| Launch profile assumed | Delta-v [m/s] | Landed mass [kg] (Isp 440 / 460) | Dry mass without payload [kg] | Hover thrust at 1.622 m/s^2 [kN] |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| New Glenn delivers MK1 to TLI; MK1 does LOI + descent | 2,800 | 11,158 / 11,477 | 8,158 / 8,477 | 18.1 / 18.6 |
| same, higher losses | 3,000 | 10,652 / 10,979 | 7,652 / 7,979 | 17.3 / 17.8 |
| MK1 does TLI itself from LEO | 5,900 | 5,440 / 5,773 | 2,440 / 2,773 | 8.8 / 9.4 |
| same, higher losses | 6,200 | 5,074 / 5,401 | 2,074 / 2,401 | 8.2 / 8.8 |

The LEO-start case implies a 2-3 t dry mass for an 8 m LH2/LOX lander, which is implausible, so the true profile is probably intermediate (a high-energy injection orbit); the landed mass is therefore in the 6-11 t band. At T/W = 1 in lunar gravity the 8.9 kN floor supports 5,487 kg and the 44.5 kN maximum 27,435 kg. Consequently MK1 can hover continuously on one BE-7 in every plausible case; the report's concern ("whether a 3 t-class lander can hover on one BE-7 or must pulse") conflated the 3 t payload with the landed mass.

**Status.** Throttle floor, maximum thrust, 100 m accuracy and 3 t payload: Verified (Blue Origin's own text via Wayback). Dry/landed mass: Still open (derived band only). Isp: Still open (no primary figure; use 440-460 s as an Assumed band, the range of comparable LH2/LOX expander-cycle engines - background knowledge, not fetched).

**Consequence for the simulation parameters.** For the MK1-class lander: landed mass sweep 6-11 t, single engine 8.9-44.5 kN (throttle 20-100 %), Isp 440-460 s (Assumed), hover T/W from 0.8 to 4 across the sweep, target accuracy 100 m. The 3 t-class vehicle in the lander sweep should be modelled on Nova-C/Blue Ghost-class landers, not on MK1.

## 4. Lane & Metzger 2012 and 2015: ballistic range and time-of-flight tables

**What was unverified.** "Lane & Metzger 2012/2015 range and time-of-flight tables not opened"; they "would replace back-of-envelope ranges".

**What I found.**

(a) The "2012 ballistics" paper is Lane, J. E., and P. T. Metzger, "Ballistics Model for Particles on a Horizontal Plane in a Vacuum Propelled by a Vertically Impinging Gas Jet", NASA KSC-2010-164, NTRS 20100031187, https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20100031187/downloads/20100031187.pdf (12-page draft preprint "sent to a journal"; the journal version appeared in *Particulate Science and Technology* 30(2), 2012 - venue from memory, not fetched). It is a trajectory-shape model, not a range model. Abstract: "A simple trajectory model has been developed and is presented. The particle trajectory path is estimated by computing the vertical position as a function of the horizontal position using a constant horizontal velocity and a vertical acceleration approximated as a power law." and "The last term on the right is the gravity term, which for a particle with velocities less than escape velocity will eventually bring the particle back to the ground. The parameters b, so, and in some cases vo, are taken from an interpolation of similar parameters determined from a CFD simulation matrix, coupled with complete particle trajectory simulations." Body (paraphrase of a garbled OCR line; numbers exact): when the horizontal velocity "is very large, from 100 to 2400 (escape velocity) m/s", the gravity term (g = 1.622 m/s^2 on the lunar surface) "has minimal effect within a radius of 20 to 30 m" for particles "less than 100 um in diameter". Conclusion: "This term is generally the < 3 deg trajectory angle." There is no table of range or time of flight; Table 1 lists fit parameters (b, c, vy). The fitted horizontal velocities for the Apollo-LM-class CFD case (Figure 2) are the only speed numbers: 1 um particles starting 0.89, 1.09, 2.0, 3.7, 4.5 and 6.7 m from the nozzle centre: vy = 1997, 1770, 640, 414, 480 and 346 m/s; 1000 um particles starting 0.89, 1.33 and 2.0 m: 133.6, 116.3 and 88.7 m/s. For a 67 N thruster at 5 ft (Figure 3): 1 um at 0.89/2.4/6.7 m: 1983, 529, 191 m/s; 1000 um: 93.3, 15.8, 0.09 m/s.

(b) The 2015 paper is Lane, J. E., and P. T. Metzger, "Estimation of Apollo lunar dust transport using optical extinction measurements", *Acta Geophysica* 63(2):568-599 (2015), arXiv 1503.00154, https://arxiv.org/pdf/1503.00154 (25 pp.). It is an erosion-rate method, not a ballistics paper. Key verbatim numbers: "The total eroded mass, using the parameter values chosen, equals 2594 kg."; "The computed value of mT ~ 2600 kg, and as shown in Table 3, is well within the range of other estimates of Apollo 12 total mass erosion."; Table 3: Scott (1975) Apollo 12 4500 to 6400 kg; Metzger et al. (2008) Apollo 12 2400 kg; Metzger et al. (2010) Apollo average 1200 kg; present work 2600 kg. Table 2 gives the time history over the last 50.7 s of descent (LM altitude 36.6 m to 1.83 m, erosion radius a0 from 68 m to 5 m, erosion rate from 0.000734 to 1.26 kg s^-1 m^-2), with "the majority of the mass weighted erosion takes place in the last 20 s." Velocity model: an empirical maximum-velocity fit u(h, D) (Eq. 7, Fig. 5) reduced by "v(D) = xi sin(alpha) u(h, D), where alpha is the plume propelled dust angle, equal to approximately 3 degrees (Immer et al. 2011b)" with xi = 1/8 ("a value of xi = 1/8 is used as an approximation throughout the descent and erosion analysis"). On thrust scaling the paper argues linearity: "According to Roberts' plume analysis theory, shear stress everywhere on the surface is proportional to thrust of the rocket, indirectly implying that the relationship is linear" and "Erosion rate and shear stress were found to be linearly related". The "thrust^2.5" ejecta-mass scaling is not in this paper; it belongs to Metzger's later LPSC/SSERVI abstracts and Metzger & Mantovani 2023, which the plume note already cites. The note's phrase "Lane & Metzger (2015) found plume ejecta mass 'scales as vehicle thrust to the 2.5 power'" should be re-attributed.

(c) The closest published range information is Lane, Metzger & Carlson, "Lunar Dust Particles Blown By Lander Engine Exhaust in Rarefied and Compressible Flow" (Earth & Space 2010; arXiv 2104.06332, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.06332). Figure 3 is a plot of "the down range distance (in m) of the trajectory" against particle diameter for the Altair descent case ("the proposed Altair lunar lander descent thrust equal to approximately 33.7 kN, a factor of 5.4 greater than the Apollo lander thrust"); the plot was not digitised here. Text: "Note that escape velocity, vE = sqrt(2 gL RL) ~ 2.37 km/s, where gL = 1.622 m/s^2 and RL = 1737 km, is reached in some cases of the smallest particles." Abstract: "Particles that originate at a height of 1 cm above the surface from an outer crater rim can be propelled to angles of 5 degrees or more. Particles that start at 10 cm above the surface can be ejected with angles of up to 15 degrees." Summary: "Previous work showed most particles ejected from a rocket jet impingement on the lunar surface are in the range of 3 deg or less. A 2 m high berm located at 30 m should be effective in stopping particles with trajectory angles of up to 4 deg. Based on these simulations, some larger particles will be ejected at angles of up to 15 deg near the rocket nozzle. These larger particles have relatively low velocities, but due to the lack of any drag impediment, these particles can travel long distances before raining down to the ground." and "Obviously, there will be a very large reduction in the number of particles hitting an outpost structure located at 1 km due to spherical spreading."

(d) Because no published table exists, the following was computed here (vacuum ballistics; g = 1.622 m/s^2; R = 1,737.4 km; flat-Moon closed form R = v^2 sin 2theta / g and T = 2 v sin theta / g; spherical-Moon values by RK4 integration of the two-body problem to re-impact). Mark as Modelled.
