# Multi-robot traffic management and provable safety for Lunar ATC: literature review (current to 2026-10-27)

Scope: what the literature says about (1) multi-agent path finding and shared-road traffic, (2) traffic management with many operators (UTM, U-space), (3) runtime assurance and provable safety layers, (4) formal verification and statistical safety claims, and (5) lunar specifics (plume keep-out, safety zones, rover speeds and braking, crew, communications). It ends with concrete rules of the road and a test plan for the LunarHaul simulator, and a novelty check.

## 1. Multi-agent path finding (MAPF) and shared-road traffic

### Takeaway

Centralised optimal MAPF (CBS) is exact but scales to tens or low hundreds of agents; bounded-suboptimal (ECBS, EECBS) and prioritised planning reach hundreds; PIBT and LaCAM reach thousands to 10,000 agents on grids in seconds to minutes. For shared roads, two results matter more than raw scale. First, a plan computed on a discrete grid is safe on real robots only if an execution layer enforces the plan's ordering of who passes first (Hönig's action dependency graph); this costs little throughput and removes the need for synchronous clocks. Second, throughput in lifelong (continuous-task) operation rises with fleet size up to a peak and then falls because of congestion; the peak moves with the planner and with road layout. Deadlock is a real failure mode in every family: prioritised planning is incomplete, PIBT's guarantee needs every edge to lie on a cycle (a dead-end single-lane road violates this), windowed planners deadlock when the window is short, and the Dresner–Stone reservation intersection deadlocked at coarse granularity. The standard remedy for narrow roads is a "well-formed" layout: every vehicle can wait somewhere that blocks no one else, and a vehicle only enters a single-lane segment when its exit is guaranteed.

### Cited findings

- **Conflict-Based Search (CBS).** Two-level search: a high level branches on conflicts between pairs of agents and adds a constraint to one of them; a low level replans one agent at a time. Optimal for sum of costs. — Sharon, Stern, Felner, Sturtevant, "Conflict-based search for optimal multi-agent pathfinding", *Artificial Intelligence* 219 (2015) 40–66, doi:10.1016/j.artint.2014.11.006. **High** (method; numbers not quoted).
- **ECBS / EECBS.** Bounded-suboptimal CBS with a focal list; cost ≤ w × optimal. — Barer, Sharon, Stern, Felner, "Suboptimal variants of the conflict-based search algorithm for the multi-agent pathfinding problem", SoCS 2014; Li, Ruml, Koenig, "EECBS: A bounded-suboptimal search for multi-agent path finding", AAAI 2021, 35(14):12353–12362. **High** (method).
- **Prioritised planning and reservation tables.** Agents plan one after another in a fixed priority order; each plan is written into a space-time reservation table that later agents must avoid (Cooperative A\*, WHCA\* with a rolling window). — Erdmann & Lozano-Pérez, "On multiple moving objects", *Algorithmica* 2 (1987) 477–521; Silver, "Cooperative pathfinding", AIIDE 2005, pp. 117–122. **High** (method).
- **Prioritised planning is incomplete, but a revised version is complete on a characterised class of maps.** Classical prioritised planning "has been shown to be effective in practice, but it is incomplete"; revised prioritised planning (RPP) provably solves a formally characterised class of instances, and an asynchronous decentralised variant inherits that property and ran up to twice as fast as the earlier synchronised decentralised method. The class is what the authors call well-formed infrastructures (every robot's start and goal are points where a parked robot blocks nobody). — Čáp, Novák, Kleiner, Selecký, "Prioritized planning algorithms for trajectory coordination of multiple mobile robots", *IEEE Trans. Automation Science and Engineering* 12(3) (2015) 835–849, doi:10.1109/TASE.2015.2445780; arXiv:1409.2399. **Medium** (abstract and summaries; the "well-formed infrastructure" wording from memory of the paper, not re-read).
- **Lifelong MAPF with well-formed endpoints.** For continuous pickup-and-delivery, Ma et al. prove that their decoupled algorithms complete all tasks if the instance is well-formed: there are enough "endpoints" where agents can rest without blocking any other agent's path. — Ma, Li, Kumar, Koenig, "Lifelong multi-agent path finding for online pickup and delivery tasks", AAMAS 2017, pp. 837–845. **Medium** (from memory of the paper; not re-read this session).
- **PIBT.** Each agent gets a unique priority every timestep; a lower-priority agent in the way "inherits" priority and is pushed, with backtracking if it cannot move. Fully decentralisable. Guarantee: all agents reach their goals in finite time if every pair of adjacent nodes lies on a simple cycle (for example, biconnected graphs). — Okumura, Machida, Défago, Tamura, "Priority inheritance with backtracking for iterative multi-agent path finding", IJCAI 2019, pp. 535–542; journal version *Artificial Intelligence* 310 (2022) 103752; arXiv:1901.11282. **High** for the guarantee (IJCAI abstract); **Medium** for the journal details.
  - Consequence for lunar roads: a dead-end spur or a single-lane corridor between two bays has edges on no cycle, so PIBT's guarantee does not apply there. PIBT alone is also described in the later LaCAM papers as a greedy method that can deadlock or livelock.
- **LaCAM and Engineering LaCAM\*.** LaCAM is a complete search that uses PIBT to generate successors; LaCAM\* is eventually optimal. In Okumura's AAMAS 2024 evaluation on the MAPF benchmark (32 grid maps × 25 instances = 800 instances, maximum agents per scenario, "mostly a thousand agents", 30 s limit), the original LaCAM solved all 800; the enhanced version failed one (maze-32-32-4); LNS2, the only other method able to attempt these, failed 290 (36%). Optimal and bounded-suboptimal methods and prioritised planning "largely falter" on these instances. A 10,000-agent test on warehouse-20-40-10-2-2 (25 instances, 5-min limit) was run; such instances were "unsolvable in a realistic timeframe" for search-based MAPF before LaCAM. A swap-style reordering of PIBT's candidates was added specifically to stop the search stalling where agents must exchange places in narrow passages. — Okumura, "Engineering LaCAM\*: Towards real-time, large-scale, and near-optimal multi-agent pathfinding", AAMAS 2024, arXiv:2308.04292; Okumura, "LaCAM: Search-based algorithm for quick multi-agent pathfinding", AAAI 2023, 37(10):11655–11662. **High** (primary text read).
- **Benchmark definitions** (vertex, edge, swap and following conflicts; sum-of-costs vs makespan): Stern et al., "Multi-agent pathfinding: Definitions, variants, and benchmarks", SoCS 2019. **High**.
- **Lifelong MAPF in warehouses (RHCR), quantitative.** Rolling-horizon collision resolution: resolve conflicts only for the next w timesteps, replan every h steps. Fulfilment map 33×46 (16% obstacles), w = 20, h = 5, 5,000 timesteps per run. Throughput (tasks per timestep) with 60 / 100 / 140 agents: RHCR 2.33 / 3.56 / 4.55, versus 6.8% / 6.3% / 4.3% lower for "holding endpoints" and 6.0% / 4.2% / 1.1% lower for "reserving dummy paths"; RHCR runtime 0.33 / 2.04 / 7.78 s per call versus ≤ 0.17 s for the baselines. Sorting centre 37×77: with PBS and w = 5, throughput rose from 12.27 (400 agents) to 27.95 (1,000 agents); with w = ∞, PBS solved only up to 700 agents. "Small values of w speed up RHCR by up to a factor of 6 without compromising the throughput." The paper warns that "using too small a value for the time horizon may generate deadlocks" (its Example 2 with w = 2 has agents waiting forever) and adds a potential-function rule that grows w until enough agents progress; RHCR remains incomplete. Up to 1,000 agents occupy 38.9% of empty cells. — Li, Tinka, Kiesel, Durham, Kumar, Koenig, "Lifelong multi-agent path finding in large-scale warehouses", AAAI 2021, 35(13):11272–11281; arXiv:2005.07371. **High** (tables read via ar5iv).
- **Throughput peaks with density.** For lifelong MAPF there is a "peak agent density, beyond which adding more agents decreases throughput due to increasingly severe congestion"; congestion-avoiding guidance (penalising edges by predicted traffic flow) moved the peak by about 2,000 agents on the Warehouse map and 200 on the Sortation map. — Chen, Harabor, Li, Stuckey, "Traffic flow optimisation for lifelong multi-agent path finding", AAAI 2024, 38(18):20674–20682, doi:10.1609/aaai.v38i18.30054. **Medium** (extended abstract and summaries; full tables not read). A 2026 follow-up reports experiments with up to 16,000 agents (Lin, Chen, Dowe, Harabor, AAMAS 2026). **Low** (search snippet).
- **Executing MAPF plans on real robots: action dependency graph (ADG).** The plan is turned into a graph whose edges record which robot must finish an action before another starts (for example, who goes first through a narrow passage). A robot enters a cell only after the previous robot has completely left it, so the effective safety distance is one cell. Only finished actions need to be reported, so communication is light and robot dynamics can be arbitrary. A plan that needs exact synchronous motion (four robots rotating in a 2×2 block) shows up as a cycle in the ADG and can be rejected. Warehouse simulation in Amazon's HARMONIES simulator ("small_1": 50 robots, 600 shelves, 8 stations; ECBS-TA with suboptimality 2; replanning took 15 s on average but overlapped execution): zero unnecessary wait time; a synchronous baseline (all robots wait for the slowest at every step) reached station utilisation u(500) = 0.09, "over 2.5 times lower". Mixed-reality run: 12 robots (6 physical iRobot Create2), an unforeseen obstacle and an imposed speed change; no collisions. — Hönig, Kiesel, Tinka, Durham, Ayanian, "Persistent and robust execution of MAPF schedules in warehouses", *IEEE Robotics and Automation Letters* 4(2) (2019) 1125–1131, doi:10.1109/LRA.2019.2894217. **High** (primary text read).
- **Kiva / Amazon warehouses.** Drive units lift and move shelving pods on a grid; "a large installation may require 500 or more vehicles". Coordination is centralised (a job manager allocates tasks and space). — Wurman, D'Andrea, Mountz, "Coordinating hundreds of cooperative, autonomous vehicles in warehouses", *AI Magazine* 29(1) (2008) 9–20, doi:10.1609/aimag.v29i1.2082. **Medium** (abstract only; PDF hosts unreachable this session, so the reservation mechanism inside Kiva is not quoted).
- **Autonomous intersection management (AIM), quantitative.** Vehicles request a time-space reservation from an intersection manager, which simulates the trajectory on a grid of n × n tiles and accepts if no tile is double-booked. Design rules stated in the paper: the system "should be safe in the event of total communication failure"; "If a message is sent but not received ... the worst thing that can happen is additional delay. No collisions can occur due to lost messages", because a vehicle never assumes a reservation it has not had confirmed. Buffers: static buffers waste space, time buffers scale with speed, and a hybrid (time buffer plus a small static buffer) is recommended; extra buffers on the exit-edge tiles prevent rear-end collisions after the intersection. Results: the reservation system "can reduce delay by two orders of magnitude"; at a vehicle spawn probability of 0.02 per step, all traffic lights (periods 10, 30, 50 s) were past capacity while the granularity-2 system added less than 0.1 s to average travel time (runs of 1,000,000 steps ≈ 5.5 h simulated). Granularity 1 (whole intersection as one tile) deadlocked in heavy traffic (spike near spawn probability 0.013); odd granularities did worse than the even number below them (2×2 beat 3×3, 4×4 beat 5×5), because opposite-direction vehicles compete for the middle tiles. The policy can emulate a traffic light or stop sign, so it subsumes them. — Dresner & Stone, "A multiagent approach to autonomous intersection management", *JAIR* 31 (2008) 591–656, doi:10.1613/jair.2502. **High** (primary text read). The earlier AAMAS 2004 paper claimed delay "two to three hundred times better than traffic lights" (Medium, abstract).
- **Deadlock theory.** A deadlock needs four conditions at once: mutual exclusion, hold-and-wait, no preemption, circular wait (Coffman, Elphick, Shoshani, "System deadlocks", *ACM Computing Surveys* 3(2) (1971) 67–78). Breaking any one prevents deadlock; for roads the practical lever is circular wait (acquire road segments in a fixed order, or only enter a segment whose exit is already reserved). **High** (textbook).

### What transfers to the Moon

Lunar traffic is low-density compared with warehouses (tens of vehicles at most), so raw MAPF scale is not the problem; the problems are long single-lane segments, very different vehicle speeds and stopping distances, intermittent communication, and multiple owners. The transferable pieces are: reservation tables over road segments with time buffers (Silver; Dresner), an ADG-style precedence layer that makes plans robust to slow or late vehicles (Hönig), well-formed layouts with passing bays and parking spots (Čáp; Ma), and the measurement practice of plotting throughput and delay against fleet size and finding the congestion peak (Li; Chen).

## 2. Traffic management with many operators: NASA UTM and EU U-space

### Takeaway

UTM is the closest existing template for robots from different owners sharing space without a single controller. Its core ideas are: every operation is filed in advance as an "operational intent" (4D volumes: space plus start and end times); service suppliers that serve different operators check each new intent against all others (strategic deconfliction), with first-come-first-served precedence and priority for public-safety operations; vehicles are monitored for conformance to their filed intent, and a nonconforming or contingent operation is broadcast to everyone nearby. The EU U-space regulation makes the "no intersection in space and time" rule explicit for flight authorisations. NASA's field tests measured the federated message exchange: average 189 ms, 95% of messages within 531 ms, but 95th percentile 3.9 s for negotiation messages. What transfers to the Moon is the structure (filed intents, federated deconfliction, conformance monitoring, published constraints such as a closed landing zone); what does not transfer is the reliance on terrestrial internet and cloud servers.

### Cited findings

- **UTM ConOps principles.** "Two primary mantras: (1) flexibility where possible and structure where necessary (2) a risk-based approach". The operator "submits an operation plan with waypoints or airspace volumes, start and end times, vehicle information"; the service supplier checks static constraints, then dynamic constraints "such as weather, conflicts with other plans" and returns approval, rejection or notifications; when new constraints arise the operation is re-checked "and potentially be terminated or rerouted"; contingencies such as geofence breach or loss of command-and-control link are handled by contingency procedures. De-confliction "could be as simple as first-come-first-serve notification with prioritization for public safety, or emergency operations", and cooperation between suppliers "cannot extend to de facto management of the airspace by third party entities". Technical capability levels (TCL) were staged on four risk metrics (people and property on the ground, manned aircraft nearby, density of UAS operations), one TCL every 12–18 months. — Kopardekar, Rios, Prevot, Johnson, Jung, Robinson, "Unmanned Aircraft System Traffic Management (UTM) Concept of Operations", 16th AIAA Aviation Technology, Integration, and Operations Conference, 2016, AIAA 2016-3292 (NTRS 20190000370). **High** (primary text read). The DOI 10.2514/6.2016-3292 follows AIAA's pattern but was not resolved this session (Medium).
- **TCL dates and contents** (NASA slides by Kopardekar): TCL1 (Aug 2015) reservation of airspace volume over unpopulated land; TCL2 (Oct 2016) beyond visual line of sight, tracking, "procedures and rules-of-the-road"; TCL3 (Jan 2018) moderately populated areas, vehicle-to-vehicle and vehicle-to-UTM links; TCL4 (Mar 2019) urban, higher density, "large-scale contingencies mitigation". Principles slide: "UAS stay clear of each other", "Public safety UAS have priority over other UAS". — NASA UTM overview slides, NTRS 20160006553. **High** (primary slides read).
- **TCL4 federated network performance** (Reno, 17–28 June 2019; Corpus Christi, Aug 2019). More than 1.1 million USS-to-USS message samples; success criteria average latency < 1,000 ms (minimum) and < 400 ms (target). Overall average 189.4 ms; 95% of exchanges ≤ 531 ms; 95th percentile 471 ms for position messages and 3,908 ms for negotiation messages (the only type that missed the minimum criterion). Observed operational impact came from "downed servers, inappropriately high http-request rates, or misimplemented specifications", not from latency. Target densities "were often missed" because of orchestration of many stakeholders. — Rios et al., "UAS Service Supplier Network Performance: Results and analysis from flight testing multiple USS providers in NASA's TCL4 demonstration", NASA/TM-2020-220462, Jan 2020, https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20200000531. **High** (primary text read). A secondary summary adds that 117 of 219 landings (~53%) were within 3 m of the planned point or inside the declared polygon (**Medium**, not located in the extracted text).
- **ASTM F3548-21 (USS interoperability).** Covers strategic conflict detection, "aggregate operational intent conformance monitoring", constraint management and processing, and "conformance monitoring for situational awareness" when operations are nonconforming or contingent. A revision (work item WK85414) is adding conflicts allowed where regulation permits, priority and preemption, and negotiation. — ASTM F3548-21, *Standard Specification for UAS Traffic Management (UTM) UAS Service Supplier (USS) Interoperability*; https://www.astm.org/workitem-wk85414. **Medium** (scope pages; normative text is paywalled and was not read). The operational-intent state names (Accepted, Activated, Nonconforming, Contingent) are as implemented in the open-source InterUSS project; **Low** (not confirmed against the standard this session).
- **EU U-space.** All UAS operations in U-space airspace receive at least four mandatory services: network identification, geo-awareness, UAS flight authorisation, and traffic information (Article 3(2)); weather and conformance monitoring are additional services. Recital 20: the flight authorisation service "should ensure that authorised UAS operations are free of intersection in space and time with any other notified UAS flight authorisation within the same portion of U-space airspace." Manned aircraft under air traffic control and UAS are kept segregated by "dynamic reconfiguration" of the airspace (Article 4). — Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2021/664 of 22 April 2021 on a regulatory framework for the U-space, OJ L 139, consolidated version of 22 Feb 2026, https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg_impl/2021/664/oj/eng. **Medium** (EUR-Lex could not be fetched; wording from EASA Easy Access Rules excerpts and search snippets).

### What transfers to the lunar surface

| UTM / U-space element | Lunar ATC counterpart | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Operational intent (4D volumes) | Each robot files its route as a list of road segments with entry and exit time windows | Strategic check before departure |
| Strategic deconfliction, "free of intersection in space and time" | Segment reservations must not overlap in space and time (with buffers) | Same rule as Dresner's tiles |
| FCFS with priority for public safety | FCFS with a fixed priority ladder (crew, landing, emergency first) | See rule R8 below |
| Constraint management (temporary flight restrictions) | Published landing-zone closures, crew work areas, broken-down vehicles | Artemis Accords "safety zones" are the policy hook |
| Conformance monitoring | Each robot compares neighbours' broadcast positions with their filed intents | Nonconforming robot loses forward reservations |
| Federated suppliers, no single controller | Each owner's robots run the same safety program; no owner controls others | OTPS notes partners may want centralised coordination once ≥ 3 actors operate |
| Cloud/internet transport | Local radio mesh (LTE or 802.11-class), with gaps | Earth link (2.6 s round trip plus relay) is too slow for tactical safety |

## 3. Runtime assurance (RTA) and provable safety layers

### Takeaway

All mainstream safety-layer designs share one pattern: an unverified high-performance controller (a person, a learned policy, an optimiser) proposes an action, and a small verified component either passes it, modifies it minimally, or switches to a verified backup controller, so that the system stays inside a set of states from which safety can always be maintained. Simplex (Sha) and ASTM F3269 describe the switching form; control barrier functions (Ames) give the minimal-modification form as a quadratic program; RSS (Shalev-Shwartz) gives closed-form safe distances and a responsibility rule for cars; shields (Alshiekh) give the discrete, temporal-logic form for reinforcement learning; Hamilton–Jacobi reachability computes the safe set itself. KeYmaera X proofs for ground robots show which safety notion is achievable with moving obstacles: "passive safety" (the robot is stopped if a collision happens), not absolute collision freedom, unless the other agents also obey rules. Papers measure the cost of the safety layer in a small set of ways: task performance with and without the filter (reward, throughput, completion time), how often and how much the filter changes the command, conservatism of the safe distance against naturalistic data, and computation time. A paper that reports zero violations without reporting this cost is incomplete, because a layer that always stops is trivially safe.

### Cited findings

- **Simplex architecture.** A high-performance controller and a high-assurance controller run side by side; a decision module switches to the high-assurance controller when the system would otherwise leave a region from which the safety controller can recover. The design premise is "using simplicity to control complexity": reliability comes from a simple, verifiable core that guarantees critical functions. — Seto, Krogh, Sha, Chutinan, "The Simplex architecture for safe online control system upgrades", ACC 1998, pp. 3504–3508; Sha, "Using simplicity to control complexity", *IEEE Software* 18(4) (2001) 20–28, doi:10.1109/MS.2001.936213. **Medium** (secondary descriptions; primary not re-read).
- **ASTM F3269.** "Standard Practice for Methods to Safely Bound Behavior of Aircraft Systems Containing Complex Functions Using Run-Time Assurance" (F3269-21; first edition F3269-17 scoped to unmanned aircraft flight behaviour). Architecture: a safety monitor watches the inputs and outputs of the complex function and switches to a matching recovery function; only one recovery active at a time. The motivation is that complex functions are too hard to verify with conventional software assurance (DO-178C, IEC 61508). — https://store.astm.org/f3269-21.html. **Medium** (scope page and secondary descriptions).
- **RTA tutorial and taxonomy.** Explicit vs implicit (whether the backup's future trajectory is simulated online) and switching vs optimisation-based filters; Auto-GCAS (F-16 automatic ground collision avoidance) is the running example of an implicit Simplex design. — Hobbs, Mote, Abate, Coogan, Feron, "Runtime assurance for safety-critical systems: An introduction to safety filtering approaches for complex control systems", *IEEE Control Systems Magazine* 43(2) (2023) 28–65, doi:10.1109/MCS.2023.3234380; arXiv:2110.03506. **High** (abstract; journal pages from memory, Medium). Auto-GCAS lives saved: an AFRL article gives 13 lives and 12 aircraft; other sources give 8–15 at different dates (**Low**, inconsistent secondary sources).
- **Cost of four RTA filter types (computation).** Spacecraft docking (Clohessy–Wiltshire, distance-dependent speed limit), LQR primary controller, 100 runs: computation time relative to explicit switching = 1.00× (explicit switching), 3.25× (explicit optimisation), 11.31× (implicit switching), 13.33× (implicit optimisation); implicit switching showed "chattering" between primary and backup. Fuel and intervention frequency were not reported. — Dunlap, Hibbard, Mote, Hobbs, "Comparing run time assurance approaches for safe spacecraft docking", arXiv:2110.00447 (2021). **High** (text read via ar5iv; venue not confirmed).
- **Control barrier functions (CBF).** For a safe set {x : h(x) ≥ 0}, require ḣ(x, u) ≥ −α(h(x)); solving min ‖u − u_nom‖² subject to this linear-in-u constraint (a quadratic program) gives the smallest change to the nominal command that keeps the set forward-invariant. Multi-robot "safety barrier certificates" apply this pairwise between robots and were run on the Robotarium. — Ames, Xu, Grizzle, Tabuada, "Control barrier function based quadratic programs for safety critical systems", *IEEE TAC* 62(8) (2017) 3861–3876, doi:10.1109/TAC.2016.2638961; Ames et al., "Control barrier functions: Theory and applications", ECC 2019, pp. 3420–3431; Wang, Ames, Egerstedt, "Safety barrier certificates for collisions-free multirobot systems", *IEEE T-RO* 33(3) (2017) 661–674, doi:10.1109/TRO.2017.2659727. **High** (method; standard results).
- **Responsibility-Sensitive Safety (RSS).** Safe longitudinal distance for a rear car (speed v_r) behind a front car (speed v_f), response time ρ, rear car accelerating at most a_accel,max during ρ then braking at least a_brake,min, front car braking at most a_brake,max:
  d_min = [ v_r ρ + ½ a_accel,max ρ² + (v_r + ρ a_accel,max)² / (2 a_brake,min) − v_f² / (2 a_brake,max) ]₊.
  Lateral and opposite-direction versions exist. Five "common sense" rules: do not hit someone from behind; do not cut in recklessly; right of way is given, not taken; be careful in areas with limited visibility; if you can avoid an accident without causing another one, you must. A vehicle is "responsible" for an accident only if it violated the rule's proper response. — Shalev-Shwartz, Shammah, Shashua, "On a formal model of safe and scalable self-driving cars", arXiv:1708.06374 (2017). **High** for the definitions (standard form; the search snippet confirmed Definition 1 and the start of Lemma 2). The authors later argued RSS removes "almost all" accidents "at a mild cost" to throughput, with no numbers in the abstract (arXiv:1901.05022, **Medium**). Critics report RSS as conservative; a 2022 review notes RSS rules can sacrifice "comfort, progress, and fuel efficiency" (arXiv:2206.03418, **Medium**).
- **Shields for reinforcement learning.** A reactive shield is synthesised from a temporal-logic safety specification (a safety game on an abstraction). Preemptive shield: before the agent acts, it receives the list of safe actions. Post-posed shield: the shield corrects the chosen action "only if the chosen action causes a violation of the specification". Conditions are given under which the shield preserves the learner's convergence. — Alshiekh, Bloem, Ehlers, Könighofer, Niekum, Topcu, "Safe reinforcement learning via shielding", AAAI 2018, 32(1); arXiv:1708.08611. **High** (abstract).
- **Hamilton–Jacobi reachability.** Computes the backward reachable set of states from which, whatever the other agent does, a collision cannot be avoided; its complement (with the optimal safe control on its boundary) is the safe set. Exact in principle, grid-based, exponential in state dimension (practical to about 4–5 dimensions per pair). — Mitchell, Bayen, Tomlin, "A time-dependent Hamilton–Jacobi formulation of reachable sets for continuous dynamic games", *IEEE TAC* 50(7) (2005) 947–957, doi:10.1109/TAC.2005.851439; Bansal, Chen, Herbert, Tomlin, "Hamilton–Jacobi reachability: A brief overview and recent advances", CDC 2017, pp. 2242–2253. **High** (method).
- **Verified ground-robot navigation.** Four safety notions proved in differential dynamic logic (KeYmaera): static safety (stationary obstacles), passive safety (no collision while the robot is moving, with moving obstacles; the robot is stopped if one hits it), passive-friendly safety (the robot also leaves room for the obstacle to stop), and passive orientation safety (limited sensor field of view). Proofs hold under sensor uncertainty and actuator disturbance; liveness proofs show the robot can still reach waypoints and pass intersections. — Mitsch, Ghorbal, Vogelbacher, Platzer, "Formal verification of obstacle avoidance and navigation of ground robots", *IJRR* 36(12) (2017) 1312–1340, doi:10.1177/0278364917733549; arXiv:1605.00604. **Medium** (abstract and summaries).

### How papers measure the cost of the safety layer (summary)

1. Task performance with and without the layer on the same scenarios: reward (shields), throughput or delay (AIM, MAPF execution: Hönig's 2.5× utilisation gap between ADG and synchronous execution is a cost-of-safety-mechanism comparison).
2. Intervention statistics: fraction of steps where the command is changed, and size of the change ‖u − u_nom‖ (CBF-QP), or switch count and chattering (Simplex).
3. Conservatism of the safe envelope against observed behaviour: for RSS, how often naturalistic drivers violate d_min without any crash (a false-alarm rate), and calibration of ρ and the accelerations.
4. Computation time per decision (Dunlap et al.).
5. Liveness side-effects: deadlocks or freezing introduced by the layer (the "frozen robot" failure of CBF and reachability filters in dense crowds; RHCR deadlocks at small w).

## 4. Formal verification of rules of the road and protocols; statistical safety claims

### Takeaway

Two kinds of evidence are needed and they answer different questions. Exhaustive verification (model checking or theorem proving) proves that the rules, as modelled, never allow a violation and never deadlock; it applies to a discretised or abstracted model, and AWS's experience shows that such models find deep bugs (a 35-step trace in DynamoDB) that testing misses. Simulation and statistical model checking then measure how the implemented system behaves on the continuous dynamics, including the parts the model abstracted away. The statistics of "zero violations in n runs" are simple: the exact one-sided 95% upper bound on the per-run violation probability is 1 − 0.05^(1/n) ≈ 3/n (the rule of three), so 10⁶ clean encounters bound the rate at 3.0 × 10⁻⁶ per encounter, and 10⁷ at 3.0 × 10⁻⁷. That bound is valid only for independent runs drawn from the stated encounter distribution; it says nothing about encounters the generator never produces, which is why stratified encounter types, importance sampling and an exhaustive check on the discrete model are needed alongside it.

### Cited findings: statistics of rare events

- **Rule of three.** With 0 events in n independent trials, the 95% upper confidence limit for the event probability is approximately 3/n. — Hanley & Lippman-Hand, "If nothing goes wrong, is everything all right? Interpreting zero numerators", *JAMA* 249(13) (1983) 1743–1745, doi:10.1001/jama.1983.03330370053031. **High**. Exact form [DERIVED]: p_u = 1 − α^(1/n); for α = 0.05, n = 10³, 10⁴, 10⁵, 10⁶, 10⁷: 2.99 × 10⁻³, 3.0 × 10⁻⁴, 3.0 × 10⁻⁵, 3.0 × 10⁻⁶, 3.0 × 10⁻⁷; at 99% confidence multiply by 4.61/3.00. With k > 0 events use the Clopper–Pearson interval (Clopper & Pearson, *Biometrika* 26(4) (1934) 404–413).
- **Estimation bound used in statistical model checking.** To estimate a probability within ±ε with confidence 1 − δ, n ≥ ln(2/δ)/(2ε²) (Okamoto 1959 / Hoeffding); ε = 0.01, δ = 0.05 gives n = 18,445 [DERIVED]. This additive bound is useless for rare events (ε must be far below the rate), which is why zero-failure bounds or sequential tests (Wald SPRT, used by Younes & Simmons) are used instead. — Legay, Delahaye, Bensalem, "Statistical model checking: An overview", RV 2010, LNCS 6418, pp. 122–135; Younes & Simmons, "Statistical probabilistic model checking with a focus on time-bounded properties", *Information and Computation* 204(9) (2006) 1368–1409. **High**.
- **Why testing alone cannot show high reliability.** Demonstrating with 95% confidence that a fleet's fatality rate is below the human rate of 1.09 per 100 million miles needs about 275 million failure-free miles, about 12.5 years for 100 vehicles driving continuously. — Kalra & Paddock, "Driving to safety: How many miles of driving would it take to demonstrate autonomous vehicle reliability?", *Transportation Research Part A* 94 (2016) 182–193, doi:10.1016/j.tra.2016.09.010; RAND RR-1478. **Medium** (figures via secondary summaries; RAND page returned 403). The 275 million is the rule of three: 3 / (1.09 × 10⁻⁸ per mile) = 2.75 × 10⁸ miles [DERIVED], which confirms the figure.
- **Importance sampling for rare encounters.** Sampling dangerous lane-change encounters from a skewed distribution and reweighting gave estimates of conflict, crash and injury rates accelerated by "around 2,000 to 20,000" times relative to naturalistic sampling (1,000 simulated miles standing for 2–20 million real miles). — Zhao, Lam, Peng, Bao, LeBlanc, Nobukawa, Pan, "Accelerated evaluation of automated vehicles safety in lane-change scenarios based on importance sampling techniques", *IEEE T-ITS* 18(3) (2017) 595–607, doi:10.1109/TITS.2016.2582208. **High** (abstract). Related: O'Kelly, Sinha, Namkoong, Duchi, Tedrake, "Scalable end-to-end autonomous vehicle testing via rare-event simulation", NeurIPS 2018 (cross-entropy adaptive sampling). **Medium**.

### What a credible verification claim looks like

1. State the property in logic (e.g. STL or invariant form) and the model it was checked on, including what the model abstracts (grid size, time step, sensing error).
2. Exhaustive check on the discrete model: reachable state count, properties proved (safety invariants, deadlock freedom, bounded liveness), and the counterexamples found and fixed during development.
3. Make the checked code the running code: the checker imports the same rule functions the simulator uses, so the check tests the implementation of the rules as well as the model.
4. Simulation evidence per encounter type: n_k runs of type k, violations v_k, Clopper–Pearson or rule-of-three bound, near-miss counts and the minimum robustness margin. Report the encounter generator and its parameter ranges, and the seeds.
5. Independence: one encounter per independent seed (or a correction for clustering); do not count 10⁶ timesteps of one scenario as 10⁶ trials.
6. The cost of safety measured on the same seeds (common random numbers): delay, throughput, intervention rate.
7. A statement of what is not covered: encounter types not generated, sensor failure modes outside the model, non-compliant agents.

## 5. Lunar specifics

### Takeaway

The lunar surface policy layer is notification and coordination, not exclusion: Artemis Accords safety zones are areas where "nominal operations ... or an anomalous event could reasonably cause harmful interference", sized by "commonly accepted scientific and engineering principles", temporary, and other actors must notify and coordinate before entering. NASA's OTPS report adds three facts that shape a traffic controller: transit corridors near the south pole may be only 20–40 m wide in places; landing standoff distances are uncertain enough that NASA experts suggested simple standard distances; and with three or more actors some centralised coordination "may become necessary". Physical numbers for the rules: plume ejecta standoff of order 1–2 km (Apollo heritage boundary 2 km; NASA logistics guidance ">1,000 m"; particles of 1–10 cm reached about 1.5 km); rover design speeds 10–15 km/h on 20° slopes, realised averages lower; and, important for separation rules, lunar braking distance is much longer than on Earth because braking force scales with gravity (Apollo 16 stopped from about 10 km/h in roughly twice the distance of the 1-g trainer). No published Artemis requirement for a crew keep-out radius around autonomous rovers was found. Surface communications are immature: the only cellular network flown (Nokia on IM-2) ran for about 25 minutes without completing a call, and CADRE's three-rover mesh has not yet flown (IM-3 now NET Q1 2027).

### Cited findings: policy

- **Artemis Accords, Section 11 (primary text).** §11.7: "the Signatories intend to provide notification of their activities and commit to coordinating with any relevant actor to avoid harmful interference. The area wherein this notification and coordination will be implemented ... is referred to as a 'safety zone'. A safety zone should be the area in which nominal operations of a relevant activity or an anomalous event could reasonably cause harmful interference." (a) size, scope, notice and coordination "should reflect the nature of the operations being conducted and the environment"; (b) size and scope determined "in a reasonable manner leveraging commonly accepted scientific and engineering principles"; (c) zones change with the operation and "will ultimately be temporary, ending when the relevant operation ceases"; (d) prompt notification of establishment, alteration or end, to each other and the UN Secretary-General. §11.8: on request, provide "the basis for the area". §11.10: respect reasonable safety zones "including by providing prior notification to and coordinating with each other before conducting operations in a safety zone". §11.11: respect "the principle of free access to all areas of celestial bodies". §11.5: share information on location and nature of activities if they may cause harmful interference or a safety hazard. — *The Artemis Accords: Principles for Cooperation in the Civil Exploration and Use of the Moon, Mars, Comets, and Asteroids*, signed 13 Oct 2020, https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/Artemis-Accords-signed-13Oct2020.pdf. **High** (primary text read).

## What this means for our simulation

### A. Architecture to test

The neural planner proposes an action (next segment, speed, wait or go). The safety program on each vehicle holds a local copy of the shared reservation table and the published constraints (landing closures, crew zones), and accepts the proposal only if it keeps every rule below satisfied for the current reservations; otherwise it substitutes the safe fallback (slow to the safe speed, hold at the current safe point, or stop). Reservations are acquired by message; a reservation that is not confirmed is never assumed (Dresner's rule), so a lost message can only add delay. This is a Simplex / post-posed shield design and should be described as such.

### C. Encounter types to test

1. Following on the flat and on 10°/15°/20° downhill, leader brakes at maximum.
2. Head-on on a single-lane segment, both with and without a passing bay within reach; with a third vehicle queued behind each.
3. Overtaking on a two-lane stretch, ending before a single-lane segment.
4. Crossing and merging at a junction; four-way contention for one junction (Dresner granularity test).
5. Cyclic wait: three or four vehicles each holding a segment the next needs (tests R3; the checker should find this if R3 is weakened).
6. Landing announced while a vehicle is inside R; while a vehicle has a filed route through R; landing delayed (window extended) and waved off (window cancelled and re-filed).
7. Crew on foot crossing a road; crew emerging from behind a boulder or crater rim (limited visibility); crew walking toward a stopped robot.
8. Communication dropout mid-negotiation (one side receives the grant, the other does not); 10–50% message loss; mesh partition between two groups.
9. Nonconforming vehicle: slip on a slope makes it late; navigation drift makes it off-route; a vehicle stops dead (fault) inside a single-lane segment.
10. Multi-owner mix: different speed classes (HAUL 4 km/h, LTV 10 km/h) on the same segment; one owner's planner that always bids aggressively (an adversarial planner: the shield must still hold).
11. Charger and pad contention at the base with 2×, 4×, 8× the nominal fleet.
12. Planner fuzzing: random and adversarial proposals from the neural planner at every step (the safety program must hold whatever the planner says).

### D. Metrics

- **Violations (hard):** separation below R1 d_min; any vehicle inside a closed landing zone; any vehicle inside R_crew,stop; any entry to a single-lane segment without a reservation. Target: zero.
- **Near misses:** separation < 2 × d_min, or time-to-collision < 10 s; crew distance < 2 × R_crew,stop; zone exit with less than 5 min margin.
- **Minimum separation and STL robustness:** distribution over encounters (minimum, 1st percentile), per encounter type.
- **Delay cost of safety:** extra travel time per delivery versus (a) the same planner without the safety layer and (b) a centralised reference plan (prioritised planning or CBS on the road graph); intervention rate (fraction of planner actions modified) and mean modification; throughput (deliveries per day) against fleet size, to locate the congestion peak.
- **Deadlock and livelock:** count and duration of states in which a set of vehicles all wait for each other longer than T_dead (e.g. 10 min); livelock = vehicles moving without reducing distance-to-goal for longer than T_live.
- **Fairness across owners:** mean and 95th-percentile delay per owner; share of yields given versus received.
- **Communications:** message latency percentiles (compare UTM TCL4: 95% ≤ 531 ms), reservation grant latency, fraction of time each vehicle was disconnected.

### E. The statistical argument for safety claims

- Claim per encounter type k: "zero violations in n_k independent encounters; one-sided 95% upper bound p_k ≤ 1 − 0.05^(1/n_k) ≈ 3/n_k." With n_k = 10⁵ per type and 12 types (1.2 million encounters), each type is bounded at 3.0 × 10⁻⁵.
- For a claim over the whole operational mix with weights w_k: p ≤ Σ w_k p_k at joint confidence ≥ 1 − Σ α_k (Bonferroni); use α_k = 0.05/12 per type for a 95% joint statement, which raises each bound from 3.0/n_k to about 5.5/n_k (since −ln(0.05/12) = 5.48).
- A flat "1,000,000 encounters, zero violations" supports p ≤ 3.0 × 10⁻⁶ per encounter (95%) only for the encounter distribution generated; state that distribution and its parameter ranges.
- Independence: one encounter per seed; when encounters are extracted from long multi-vehicle runs, count encounters but bound with the number of independent runs, or use a cluster bootstrap.
- Rare types (crew emerging from occlusion during a landing closure) should be sampled at boosted rates and reweighted (importance sampling; Zhao et al. report 2,000–20,000× acceleration), with the weights reported.
- Pair every bound with near-miss rates and the minimum robustness margin, so a reader sees how close the system came.
- The simulation bound covers the implementation on continuous dynamics; the exhaustive check below covers the rules on the discrete model. Neither covers real sensors.

### F. Formal check feasible on a laptop (8 GB shared)

An explicit-state model checker written in Python, in the style of TLC or UPPAAL, over a discretised encounter model:

- **Model.** A small road graph with typed edges (two-lane, single-lane) and nodes (junction, bay, parking, pad boundary): for example 12–16 nodes including one single-lane segment of 4 cells with a bay at each end and one junction. 2–3 vehicles, each with state (node or cell, heading, mode ∈ {drive, wait, yield, stopped}, held reservations). One landing process with phases {open, notice, closed, reopen}. One crew agent moving on 4–6 cells. One link state per vehicle {up, down}. Time is discrete (one step = one cell transit at the slowest class).
- **Transitions.** Interleaving of vehicle moves, crew moves, landing phase changes and link drops, all nondeterministic; the planner is modelled as fully nondeterministic (any proposal), so the proof covers any planner. Each vehicle's move goes through the same `safety_check()` function the simulator uses (import it; one source of truth).
- **Properties.** Safety invariants checked on every reachable state: no two vehicles in the same cell or within the separation cells; no vehicle in a closed zone; no vehicle in a crew cell or adjacent cells. Deadlock freedom: every reachable non-goal state has an enabled transition that is not "all wait". Bounded liveness: from every reachable state, a state where all vehicles have reached their goals is reachable within K steps under fair scheduling (backward BFS from goal states; any reachable state outside that set is a liveness counterexample). Livelock: strongly connected components (Tarjan) among non-goal states with no exit under fairness.
- **Size and memory.** Upper bound for 3 vehicles on 12 cells × 2 headings × 3 modes: 72³ ≈ 3.7 × 10⁵ vehicle combinations, × 4 landing phases × 7 crew positions × 8 link states ≈ 8.4 × 10⁷ [DERIVED], too many for a Python set. Use symmetry reduction (vehicles of the same class are interchangeable: sort them, ÷ 6), drop link states for the safety-only run, and pack each state into one integer. Reachable sets are usually far below the bound; keep a hard cap (e.g. 3 × 10⁶ states ≈ 300–500 MB in a Python set of ints) and abort with a message if exceeded. 2-vehicle models without link states (bound 72² × 4 × 7 ≈ 1.5 × 10⁵ states) run in seconds.
- **Mutation testing of the checker.** Remove one rule at a time (R3 exit reservation, R5 entry check, R7 hold) and confirm the checker produces a counterexample each time; a checker that never fails proves nothing. Report each counterexample trace length (AWS reports 35-step traces; ours will be short).
- **Optional cross-checks.** The same model in UPPAAL (free academic licence; `A[] not collision`, `A[] not deadlock`) or TLA+ (needs Java), run once, to show the home-made checker agrees.

## Novelty check

What exists:
- Warehouse and grid MAPF, lifelong MAPF, and robust execution (CBS, PBS/RHCR, PIBT/LaCAM, ADG): single owner, central planner, dense grids, no landing closures, no crew, no proofs over an unknown planner.
- Autonomous intersection management (Dresner & Stone) and its formal models (UPPAAL theses): one junction, cars, cooperative V2I.
- UTM / U-space for drones: multi-operator strategic deconfliction and conformance monitoring in airspace, with cloud connectivity and no ground-road topology.
- Runtime assurance, CBFs, RSS, shields: single vehicle or homogeneous fleets; RSS is for road cars on Earth.
- Lunar: Artemis Accords and OTPS give policy (notification, coordination, temporary safety zones, narrow corridors, standard standoff distances) without operating rules or numbers; GLEX 2025 (Kim, Li, Wang) argues for lunar rules of the road and proposes maritime rules, qualitatively; an AMOS 2024 poster covers observation architecture; Agent Mars (arXiv 2602.13291) has a scripted "ATC" landing-slot scenario without physics; lunar multi-rover planning papers (e.g. DRL plus potential fields, *Aerospace* 11(4):253, 2024) treat a single owner and no shared rules.

Searches run for the gap: "lunar surface multi-rover traffic rules right of way formal verification runtime assurance shared roads multiple operators"; "lunar rover traffic management collision avoidance multi-agent path finding south pole landing pad keep-out" (arXiv 2025–2026); earlier searches recorded in `novelty_prior_art.md`. None returned a quantitative rule set for lunar surface traffic.

What appears new, if we deliver it:
1. A concrete, parameterised rule set for lunar surface traffic (separation from lunar braking physics, single-lane give-way with exit reservations, time-windowed landing closures, crew keep-clear, comms-loss behaviour, conformance), shared by robots of different owners.
2. A proof on the discrete model, by exhaustive search over a fully nondeterministic planner, that the rules give safety and deadlock freedom, with mutation tests showing each rule is needed.
3. A measured cost of safety on lunar terrain (LOLA 5 m Connecting Ridge road graph): delay, throughput versus fleet size, and the congestion peak, with and without the safety layer and against a centralised reference.
4. Per-encounter statistical bounds with importance sampling, in the form described above.

Limits of this check: the GLEX 2025 full paper and the ASTM F3548 normative text were not available; company-internal rover autonomy work (Astrolab, Lunar Outpost, Intuitive Machines) is not public; and CADRE's flight software design for multi-rover coordination was not examined in detail.
