Rover lab: drive a leg of the base

Pick two sites on real Moon terrain (NASA LOLA elevation data), who drives and the lighting, then drive the route in your browser. It is the same model the logistics simulation takes its driving times from: slope limits, wheel slip, glare when driving towards the low sun, shadows, the Earth–Moon signal delay, energy use and interventions (a person on Earth stepping in). Drive it yourself to feel what a four-second delay does.

The drive in 3D

The 3D view in words

In a race, the yellow ring is the scripted expert and the orange one the neural network; the pale outline ahead of a rover driven from Earth is where the command sent now takes effect. Cyan ribbon: the planned route. Short posts beside the leg: Earth-link sample points about 100 m apart along the route for this lighting, cyan while any part of Earth is above the terrain for a 2.5 m antenna there and red when none is (SPICE and LOLA horizons). Red pins: interventions. Rocks are representative: LOLA cannot resolve them and the driving model does not see them.

Sun bearings are grid bearings: at the pole “north” is the map's +y direction. The sun is only 1.5° above the horizon, so shadows are long; they are cast by terrain inside this map tile only.

What the driver sees and does

A driving policy gets a 32 × 32 grid around the rover, 5 m per cell, rotated with the rover (forward is up; the outlined cell is the rover), plus five numbers. It answers with two: a steering rate and a throttle. These are the exact inputs the learned driver gets; when it drives, the wheel and the throttle below show the network's outputs.

The learned driver

A convolutional network that imitates the scripted expert from the grid and the five numbers above. It is fetched only when you choose it in the Driver menu (the network and the in-browser runtime are about 15 MB). The logistics experiments in the paper use the expert's driving tables, not this driver.
