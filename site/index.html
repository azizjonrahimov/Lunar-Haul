Cargo logistics at a lunar south-pole base, on real terrain

A discrete-event simulator of how landed cargo gets from the pads to where it is needed, with learned landing and driving models on NASA terrain of the Connecting Ridge.

Terrain near the lunar south pole from NASA's Lunar Orbiter Laser Altimeter, lit by a sun that stays within 1.5° of the horizon and circles once per lunar day; long shadows cross the ridges and low ground the sun cannot reach stays black. Shadows are cast only by terrain inside this tile.

Version 2's comparison of dispatch rules still holds: pooling the fleets of several owners cuts the delay of urgent cargo.

What it is

A simulator of surface cargo movement at a south-pole base on real terrain: lander manifests and landing outcomes, rover fleets owned by several organisations, the lunar night, Earth–Moon control latency, and four ways of dispatching the vehicles, from each owner using its own rovers to a pooled marketplace. The landing and driving outcomes it samples come from controllers flown and driven in their own physics models, and the same physics runs in Python and in your browser.

How it works

What is new

Distance is what costs. At the typical fleet load, each kilometre between pad and base adds to the delay of urgent cargo, most of it in the first kilometre. A plume keep-out radius and a stand-down after each landing add no measurable delay at these loads: the fleets absorb the evacuations. Landing dispersion adds per e-fold of the mean miss, only through landings that miss the pad: a 100 m pad absorbs today's 50 m accuracy, a 25 m pad does not.

A learned lander comes close when it sees what the guidance law sees. Imitation of the classical law, given the altimeter's recent history, lands of dispersed landings in the touchdown box, against for the law itself; reinforcement learning on the same rewards trades touchdown accuracy for reward.

Rover autonomy stands in for fleet size. Two autonomous rovers deliver urgent cargo about as fast as six teleoperated ones.
