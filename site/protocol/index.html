Our design, being tested in the simulation

The Lunar Rover Interaction Protocol

Rovers of several companies working one base have to agree on where they are, what the ground looks like and what time it is, talk to each other over a radio the hills keep cutting, share jobs, and keep working when Earth is out of sight. Here are the eight problems, one at a time: the question, our answer, how we test it, who we would work with, and one figure to try.

1 · Positioning

Where am I?

“GPS does not work on the Moon. What replaces it?”

Our answer

A rover counts wheel turns and tracks the ground with cameras, which drifts by about ( on loose soil without cameras), and it resets that drift with absolute fixes: matching what it sees to orbital maps (), radio ranges to the base's beacons ( within ) and, from about 2030, the LunaNet navigation satellites (, available about ). Every position report carries its own horizontal error (one sigma and, since LRIP v0.2, a 95 % bound; no covariance is carried), and the rules of the road widen a rover's safety margin when that error is large.

2 · A shared map and coordinate system

How does a returning rover catch up on the map?

“How do you synchronise after a long time out of contact?”

Our answer

The base keeps one map of versioned tiles and every rover carries a full copy it can drive on alone; after time out of contact the two compare a short digest of tile versions and send only the changed tiles, hazards on the rover's route first. By calculation from the assumed change rates (experiment E52), after a day away that is for the the routes cross, of a full resend of every layer (); at 16 kbit/s the hazard layer is current after , against oldest first. The design has every coordinate name its frame, because reading it in the wrong lunar frame moves a south-pole base by about . In LRIP v each sender declares the frame, datum and time scale of everything it sends, in its heartbeat (sent at least every ) and in its signed manifest, and a receiver refuses a position from a sender that has no authenticated declaration.

Method

Experiment E52 (hypothesis H12) brings rovers back after hours to days out of contact and counts the bytes and the time until their hazard map is current, against a full resend. The bytes, and so H12's share of a full resend, follow from the assumed change rates: a design calculation, not a test. The hazards a rover meets on its stale map after contact are counted: at most with hazards first and with the oldest change first, at any outage and link rate. 3 · Time

What time is it on the Moon?

“How do rovers synchronise clocks?”

Our answer

Rovers agree on one lunar time scale and exchange timing messages in both directions, so the signal delay cancels; a clock on the Moon runs fast against one on Earth, so lunar time cannot simply copy UTC. Between resyncs each rover runs on its own oscillator: a crystal drifts about , a chip-scale atomic clock about . That is enough for position time stamps () but not for one-way radio ranging, where 1 ns is . 4 · Communication

Can it hear me behind the hill?

“Which channels? And what happens behind a hill?”

Our answer

Wi-Fi and LTE near the base, a narrow-band channel everywhere, and mesh relaying between rovers on the same radios, with messages stored and forwarded (the Bundle Protocol, about per message) when no path exists. Behind a hill the link is lost rather than weakened (a ridge costs –), so the remedy is geometric: relay through a rover or a mast that sees both sides, or store the message and wait.

5 · The protocol itself

What does a rover say, byte by byte?

“An ‘MCP for rovers’: what are the messages, and how does a rover say what it can do?”

Our answer

LRIP v has message types (position, status, intent, alert, the task and reservation messages, capability, map notice and a heartbeat), each with a version number and an authentication code; a position report is of content and before link framing and coding ( in a Proximity-1 frame). Since v0.2 the report carries a 95 % horizontal bound beside its one-sigma error, in six bits that were spare, so it did not grow; no covariance is carried. Each sender declares the frame, datum and time scale of its positions and time stamps in its heartbeat (HELLO, now instead of , sent at least every ) and in its capability manifest ( more), bound to its current key: a receiver refuses a position from a sender with no authenticated declaration. A v0.1 packet still decodes; only its positions are refused, since v0.1 cannot declare. Twenty rovers reporting once a second need about by note 03's estimate (ten times a second, ; E53's calculated load is below). Each rover also publishes a signed capability manifest (about ), the idea behind tool descriptions in the Model Context Protocol, so a job can be matched to any maker's vehicle.

6 · Coordination as a team

A rover breaks down: who takes over?

“How are tasks shared, and what happens when a rover fails?”

Our answer

Neighbours notice the silence by missed heartbeats (suspect after , lost after ), the job goes back to auction under a higher epoch number, and the silent rover's road reservation stays reserved: after its timeout and a grace period the segment is marked unconfirmed, and another vehicle may enter it only at a speed from which it can stop within its own sensor range, reporting what it finds. A planner on the Moon gave the same urgent wait as a full-information marketplace in our runs (), while a dispatcher on Earth that stops when Earth sets cost of extra wait.

Method

The Phase 5 engine (experiments E40 to E43) with the lost-link ladder of E54; a failure decision left to Earth would add about of expected delay at our base. NASA's CADRE elects a new leader within .

In E54's message replay at loss per hop, the lost-link rule keeps the base's picture of a vehicle old on average ( for the worst vehicle), and it sends a silent vehicle back along the path it drove times a seed; the replay does not drive those returns, and no delay figure contains them. A job of two vehicles gets an award for each, so awards a seed carry jobs, of them team jobs. The age and the returns were corrected after the same code review (the age had a floor of half a step, and the code's return rule was not the one the papers state): before, ( for the worst vehicle) and returns a seed.

7 · Reliability and security

Is that position report true?

“What if another rover reports a wrong position, by fault or on purpose?”

Our answer

Every message carries a counter and a keyed authentication code, so forged messages are rejected, and so are replayed ones, except a copy of a message the receiver missed that arrives while still fresh; a rover that is wrong anyway is caught by comparing its report with your own radio range to it. If the normalised squared difference exceeds on 3 of the last 5 reports, the rover is treated as suspect and the others are told; an error of about is caught 90 % of the time.

The persistence is what makes the textbook threshold usable: tested one report at a time, it raises false alarms per pair-day on our simulated streams; at 3 of the last 5, . Every fixed threshold fails out of distribution (noisier sensors, smaller faults): false alarms per pair-day for the rule and for the single test. Values many rovers must agree on are not averaged among the rovers: on our sparse mesh the trimmed mean keeps liars from pushing the shared value out of the honest range, but the honest rovers agree in at most of runs ( with the base mast as one more node), so shared estimates are computed at the base.

Method

Experiment E55 (hypotheses H15 and H16) scores the rule as written, with no tuning, on the same simulated streams and faults as the learned detector of E56 (see the network), and tests the trimmed-mean rule for values many rovers must agree on, with and without the base mast. A second replay trial, added after the code review, replays recent messages instead of old ones: at loss of replays were accepted, and at of , all of them copies of a message the receiver had missed, still inside the freshness window, and of any other kind.

8 · Market and reality

Who has flown what?

“Who is doing this already, and who would pay?”

Our answer

Nobody has flown cooperating rovers on the Moon: NASA's CADRE (three rovers, one owner) is built and waits for its lander, and the first robots of several makers on one site are planned for 2028 to 2029. Nobody we found sells a coordination layer for vehicles of different makers, and in practice the customer is NASA's Moon Base programme and its contractors.

Method

A survey of flown and planned missions, current to 11 November 2026; no experiment.

Who we would work with

First: the JPL CADRE team, the Lunar Surface Innovation Consortium and LOGIC. Then the OmniLRS simulator authors (Luxembourg and Tohoku), DFKI, DLR, and the ESA and DLR LUNA test hall.
