Phase 5 · our design, being tested in the simulation

Lunar ATC

Around 2030 a south-pole base will have rovers, hopper drones and robots from several companies at work at once. Today each company drives its own vehicles from its own control room on Earth, and Earth sinks below the ridges for days at a time. Lunar ATC is a network on the Moon itself: the robots talk to each other directly, share jobs, roads and chargers, team up on loads too heavy for one, and keep working when Earth is out of sight. One rule holds it together: the AI suggests, the safety program decides.

Lunar ATC is a design this project is testing, not a system any agency or company runs. Where a number below comes from the simulation it says so, with its source file; parts still being measured are marked as such.

1. Three parts

The safety program, on every robot

A small program that checks every move before the robot makes it: keep your distance, never enter a one-lane stretch without a reservation, stay out of a landing zone while a lander comes down, give crew on foot the right of way, stop safely if the radio goes quiet. It is deliberately simple, so that it can be checked exhaustively, and it has the last word.

The job market, shared by every company

When cargo arrives, the job goes on one board for all the companies' robots. Each robot offers a price: its cost to do the job, from its distance, battery and other work. A small learned correction, one weight for each thing the robot knows locally, nudges those offers (section 4 shows it), and the cheapest offer wins. Heavy loads can go to a team of two robots from different companies.

The base computer, with a spare

A computer at the base runs the board and keeps the ledger of who did what. It has two boards, so either can take over; if the whole computer fails, the robots elect one of themselves to run the board until it is back, the way NASA's CADRE rovers pick a leader.

The AI suggests, the safety program decides

The planner and the learned bids are fast and clever, but nothing proves they are always right. So they only suggest: a route, a speed, a job to take. The safety program on the robot decides: it lets the move through, trims it, or holds the robot where it is. Whatever the AI says, the rules hold.

2. What Earth still does

  • Sets the rules and the prices. The rules of the road and the price list are agreed on Earth and uploaded; the robots apply them, they do not invent them.
  • Publishes the landings. Landing times close the pads and their surroundings; the schedule comes from Earth well in advance.
  • Settles the money later. The base computer keeps the ledger; when Earth is back in view, the companies' payments are settled and the ledger is audited.
  • Handles what robots should not. Disputes between companies, faults that need a person, and anything involving crew.
  • What Earth no longer has to do is pick who carries each load, minute by minute, which is exactly what stops when Earth sinks below the ridges.

5. The safety program at work

To test the safety program, the simulation threw vehicles at each other in twelve kinds of encounter, each drawn at random many times: following, meeting head-on on a one-lane road, junctions, crew on foot, radio dropouts, landing closures, misbehaving vehicles and planners that try to cause a crash. Every vehicle's every move, ten times a second, went through the safety program (experiment E44, task B-78).

Loading the results…

A violation is any contact, a gap to the vehicle ahead below the 5 m margin, a vehicle inside a closed landing zone, a moving vehicle too close to crew, two vehicles meeting inside a one-lane road, or an over-full pad or junction. The bound is the 95 % upper limit on the chance of a violation per encounter of that type, corrected for testing twelve types (Bonferroni). Emergency brake: the share of encounters in which, at least once, no gentler move kept every rule and the safety program braked fully.
