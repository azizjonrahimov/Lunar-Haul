# Learned landers and rovers price lunar logistics

Extend LunarSim by treating the two learned models as **stochastic generators that feed the discrete-event simulation**, not as controllers running inside it: a PPO terminal-descent policy, trained in a custom 3-DoF Gymnasium environment parameterised from flown landers rather than in the toy `LunarLander-v3`, produces per-landing dispersion, propellant residual and plume exposure; a behavioural-cloning driver trained on PGDA LOLA 5 m terrain with a scripted expert produces per-leg speed, slip, energy and intervention rate; the DES samples both and reports delivery delay, service level and $/kg exactly as it does today. The defensible new result is the **first quantitative law linking landing dispersion σ, pad-to-base standoff D and plume keep-out radius R (with rover autonomy level) to surface delivery cost**, a relation NASA's 2023 and 2025 architecture papers state only qualitatively ("a reduction in distance means a reduction in transfer time and risk") and that no retrieved paper from 2000 to 2026 quantifies ([NASA 2023](https://www.lpi.usra.edu/lunar/strategies/resources/M2M-ACR2023_SafePreciseLunarLandings.pdf); [NASA 2025](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/iparch10-wp-logistics-mobility-cargo.pdf)). Every input can be anchored to a published number: the **100 m (3σ) HLS requirement** and **50 m SPLICE ground rule** against demonstrated accuracies from **10 m (SLIM, pre-divert) to 1.5 km (IM-1)**; NASA's **>1,000 m ejecta and up to 5,000 m aggregation** relocation bands; teleoperated rover speed falling from **3.24 to 1.76 km/h as latency rises from 0 to 8 s**; and **2,000-10,000 kg/yr** of cargo. The plume side must be swept parametrically (R from 0.2 to 3 km) because NASA itself calls PSI predictions "largely qualitative and uncertain" and the 2 km heritage standoff was, in its author's words, "just a number we made up". The recommended build is Three.js with NASA-AMMOS's `3d-tiles-renderer` over self-hosted PGDA LOLA tiles for the 3D site; FastAPI with SQLite (WAL) plus Litestream and an RQ worker on a ~€4.49/month Hetzner VPS with ORCID and GitHub sign-in for the backend; `gymnasium 1.4.0`, `stable-baselines3 2.9.0` and `torch 2.14.1` on CPU for the lander MLP, with the 4 GB GPU reserved for the rover CNN; and JSON weights with a hand-written NumPy/JavaScript forward pass (lander) plus ONNX Runtime Web (rover) so Python and browser produce identical numbers, continuing LunarSim's existing cross-implementation check. About twenty items remain unverified and must be closed before publication, chiefly Blue Moon MK1's dry mass and throttle floor, SCALPSS erosion volumes, the landing-radius objective in NASA's ADD Rev B, the Grogan & de Weck 2012 federated-economy paper, and Hetzner's current price.

## No published model prices landing dispersion or plume keep-out in delivery delay

The prior art splits into four traditions that never meet. Campaign-level space-logistics network models (SpaceNet, Ishimatsu's generalized multi-commodity network flow, the Ho group's reviews) treat "the lunar surface" as one node; Ho's 2024 review explicitly lists "surface vehicle routing and logistics" among topics it does not cover and names higher-fidelity validation of logistics models as "largely unexplored" ([Ho 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.01107v8); [Ishimatsu et al.](http://strategic.mit.edu/JSR_Final_Manuscript_Ishimatsu.pdf)). Plume and pad engineering (Metzger's group, NASA KSC, Purdue) stops at a static standoff geometry or a pad construction cost: Metzger and Autry optimise pad radius against transport cost and program-delay cost but never vary the pad's location ([Metzger & Autry 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.00378)); Anderson, Saxena and Metzger (2026) compute crater "umbrella zones" of roughly 100 m for an 8 m lander and 200 m for a 1 m rover and describe a rover sheltering during an ascent, but model no routes or schedules ([Anderson et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13263143/)). Rover autonomy and teleoperation work stops at the single vehicle: NASA JSC measured speed made good under latency but derived no fleet consequence ([Litaker et al. 2025](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240011392/downloads/Teleops%20IEEE%20Paper%20FY24%20FINAL.pdf)), and the 2026 Toronto/MDA cargo-rover field test moved 164 m of cargo autonomously in 8.6% of the teach time without modelling throughput or cost ([Krawciw et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.03371v1)). The young fourth tradition of 2025-2026 agent-based and DES base models includes rovers but not landers' dispersion or plume: the George Mason Mesa benchmark runs 4 astronauts and 2 rovers for 2,016 hours with shuttle resupply every 60 days and lists "logistics infrastructure" as future work ([Vera et al. 2026](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0348882)), and the Agent Mars LLM-agent simulation resolves overlapping landing slots by message passing with no physics or price ([Wang 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.13291v1)). LunarSim already sits in this fourth tradition; Phase 3 would be the first to wire the second and third into it.

Learned controllers in the space domain report only vehicle-level metrics. Gaudet, Linares and Furfaro's 6-DoF PPO Mars lander achieves **0.4 ± 0.7 m downrange and −0.1 ± 0.7 m crossrange over 10,000 Monte Carlo episodes with fuel 4% above the energy-optimal DR/DV law and 18% above the GPOPS open-loop optimum**, and their recurrent meta-RL variant holds **0.3 m mean miss under engine failure where DR/DV scatters to 144.9 m**, yet neither paper mentions surface operations ([Gaudet et al. 2018](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1810.08719); [Gaudet et al. 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1904.09865)). Rover-side learned work reports AUROC 0.874 for traversability from 500 sols of Perseverance logs, 2-4 cm zero-shot tracking error for an RL waypoint follower in a lunar analogue lab, or a 37% cost-of-transport cut on a 20° sand slope, never delivered cargo or energy per delivered kilogram ([Chiu et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.24952); [Orsula et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.11503); [Bouton et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.06790)). In terrestrial logistics, RL has been embedded in DES models as the dispatcher (haul trucks, taxis), never as a vehicle controller whose physical outcome distributions feed the DES ([Buildings 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/buildings16112274); [arXiv 2307.07508](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2307.07508)). The only RL in space logistics proper is Takubo, Chen and Ho's hierarchical RL for campaign design, where RL picks vehicles and ISRU deployment above a MILP, which Ho's review calls "one of the first studies that incorporated machine learning into space logistics" ([Takubo et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.08981)).

The strongest paper combines contributions 1, 2 and 3: the learned controllers supply the dispersion and intervention distributions, the DES supplies the coupling, and the law is the output. Each piece alone has a partial antecedent; the combination has none ([novelty map sources above](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.01107v8)). Two honest caveats shape the hypothesis. First, under NASA's own architecture assumptions a controller's residual dispersion of 1-100 m is small against relocation distances of 50 m to 5 km, so the law may turn out to act through thresholds rather than smoothly: dispersion sets pad diameter and pad mass scales with (D/2)², so halving dispersion from 100 m to 50 m cuts pad material fourfold and is "the difference between one and several Starship deliveries" ([Gelino et al. 2022](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220000334/downloads/In%20situ%20Lunar%20Launch%20and%20Landing%20Pad%20Construction%20with%20Regolith%20and%20Thermoset%20Polymers%20ASCE%20Earth%20and%20Space%202022%20Paper%20SUBMITTED.pdf)); off-centre landings inside the 100 m margin change which assets sit in the ejecta sheet, which Anderson et al. explicitly ask to be simulated ([Anderson et al. 2026](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44453-026-00045-w)); and tail events (IM-1's 1.5 km miss and tip-over) may dominate expected cost more than σ does. A finding of the form "dispersion matters through pad cost and tail risk, not through traverse distance" would itself be new and is reachable with the proposed architecture. Second, the propellant coupling is unexplored: nobody has converted a controller's saved propellant into delivered-payload mass, and Gaudet's 291 kg versus 250 kg optimum on a 2,000 kg lander shows the gap is of order 15-20% of descent propellant ([Gaudet et al. 2018](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1810.08719)). Validation hooks exist for both learned models: Chen et al.'s LOLA-based terramechanics gives **0.4-4.4 kWh round trips to 31 PSRs with slip sensitivity of +24% to +47%** ([Chen et al. 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/astronomy-and-space-sciences/articles/10.3389/fspas.2026.1764826/full)), and Litaker's 12% speed loss at 4 s latency is a measured target the driver model must reproduce ([Litaker et al. 2025](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240011392/downloads/Teleops%20IEEE%20Paper%20FY24%20FINAL.pdf)).

The state space at 2 km altitude and below has a consistent three-phase shape across Apollo, NASA's reference designs and the flown vehicles. At **2 km the vehicle still moves 50-300 m/s** with a flight-path angle between −30° and −90° (Chang'e-4 vertical at 85 m/s; IM-1 at 283.6 m/s and 1,934 m AGL; Apollo high gate at ~2.1 km) ([Karlgaard et al. 2025](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250007036/downloads/aasimndl.pdf); [Liu et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6760200/)). NASA's SPLICE robotic reference (2,000 kg, 300 kg payload, 5,000 N motor) targets **500 m slant range with −15 m/s vertical and 30 m/s horizontal velocity**, then 50 m directly over the site at −1.1 m/s and zero horizontal, then a vertical phase from 50 m to 2 m; the human-scale terminal descent is "nominally a vertical descent to the landing site from 30 m altitude to touchdown, at a velocity of 1 m/s downward" ([SPLICE PL&HA 2020](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20200002415/downloads/20200002415.pdf)). Apollo's low gate at 152 m carried 18.3 m/s forward and 4.9 m/s down at ~16° off vertical, with automatic vertical descent from 46 m at 0.9 m/s ([Bennett 1970](https://www.lpi.usra.edu/lunar/documents/Bennett_NASA-TM-X-58040.pdf)), and NASA's government-reference Artemis lander begins terminal descent at 200 m and cuts its engines at 1 m altitude and 1 m/s ([Lugo et al. 2020](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205003002/downloads/nav.pdf)). The control space is a main engine throttled across 10-60% (Apollo) or 20-100% (Chang'e, BE-7 per Wikipedia), gimbal of order ±6°, and RCS clusters of 4-250 N on small landers or pulsed main engines where deep throttle is unavailable (Griffin). Hazard detection belongs where the lidar envelope holds: **400-600 m slant range, 10-40 m/s descent rate, producing a 100 m-diameter DEM at 5 cm/pixel in 2 s** that must resolve 20 cm hazards and ~5° slopes ([Restrepo et al. 2019](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20190034132/downloads/20190034132.pdf)); the Navigation Doppler Lidar supplies velocity at 20 Hz with 0.2 cm/s resolution from >4 km ([Gragossian et al. 2020](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20200003107/downloads/20200003107.pdf)).

The touchdown box is the only part of the problem with fully published limits. SPLICE's multi-mission ground rules are **vertical <2.0 m/s, horizontal <1 m/s, attitude within ±6°, rates within 2°/s and delivery within a 50 m radius, all 3σ**, derived "from past human lunar missions" ([SPLICE PL&HA 2020](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20200002415/downloads/20200002415.pdf)); the HLS requirement HLS-R-0322 is **100 m (3σ)** and the uncrewed demo success criterion repeats 100 m (3σ) between 84°S and 90°S ([NASA HLS 2020](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20200001606/downloads/20200001606.pdf); [HLS SOW](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/80MSFC20C0034-P00010_Att_J-01_SOW_RIF_TAGGED.pdf)). HLS must also stand within **8° of vertical (threshold) or 5° (goal)** for surface operations ([NASA HLS 2020](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20200001606/downloads/20200001606.pdf)); Apollo's design envelope was 12° effective slope with 10 ft/s vertical and 4 ft/s horizontal, Apollo 11 touched down at roughly 0.5-0.6 m/s, and the "Doghouse" certification envelope is being re-created for HLS in 2026 ([Rogers 1972](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19720018253/downloads/19720018253.pdf); [NASA 2026](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260001760/downloads/Apollo%2011%20LM%20Touchdown%20Dynamics%20VnV%20HLS.pdf)). Apollo 15 rested at 11°, "very nearly violating the tilt limit for safe ascent", and ALHAT's manager put the ascent limit near 12° ([LSIC pads deck](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205009125/downloads/Lunar%20Landing%20&%20Launch%20Pads%20v1.pdf)). A defensible simulation box is therefore vertical ≤2.0 m/s (nominal ~1 m/s), horizontal ≤1.0 m/s, attitude ≤6° and rates ≤2°/s at contact; landed tilt beyond 8° counts as degraded and beyond ~12° as failure. Propellant margin has one published standard: Apollo added 85 fps "to assure 2 minutes of flying time in the landing phase, that is, below an altitude of 500 feet" plus 60 fps for landing-point redesignation, and Apollo 11 landed with about 45 s remaining ([Bennett 1970](https://www.lpi.usra.edu/lunar/documents/Bennett_NASA-TM-X-58040.pdf); [NASA SP-4029](https://history.nasa.gov/SP-4029/Apollo_11a_Summary.htm)); modern landers have flown far thinner, with SLIM's fault at ~98% of total propulsion use and ispace M1 hovering at 5 km until depletion ([JAXA 2024](https://www.isas.jaxa.jp/en/topics/files/SLIM-press-briefing-20241226.pdf); [ispace 2023](https://www.ispace-inc.com/2023/05/26/ispace-announces-results-of-the-hakuto-r-mission-1-lunar-landing/)).

Demonstrated precision versus requirement is the quantity the DES will sample. Only SLIM has deliberately landed under 100 m (~10 m before its divert); Blue Ghost sits at the 100 m boundary; both Nova-C and both ispace missions exceeded 250 m (ispace M2 impacted ~282 m south and ~236 m east of target at 42 m/s after its rangefinder first returned below 900 m instead of 3 km, per a search excerpt) ([SpaceNews](https://spacenews.com/laser-rangefinder-problems-blamed-for-second-ispace-lunar-lander-crash/); [Payload 2025](https://payloadspace.com/ispace-blames-faulty-hardware-for-failed-lunar-landing/)). NASA's own 6-DoF navigation study shows why: an IMU-only solution drifts over 3,000 km by touchdown, while DSN plus star tracker plus TRN brings the final position error below 4 m, and SPLICE's covariance analysis concludes "precision landing requirements cannot be satisfied without relative sensors" ([Lugo et al. 2020](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205003002/downloads/nav.pdf); [SPLICE PL&HA 2020](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20200002415/downloads/20200002415.pdf)). Every tip-over or crash since 2023 traces to the altitude-sensing chain rather than guidance or propulsion: IM-1's altimeter was never armed, IM-2's altimeter was disturbed by its own dust plume while optical navigation matched craters imaged from 100 km, ispace M1's filter rejected valid altimetry over a 3 km rim and estimated zero altitude at ~5 km, and ispace M2's rangefinder reported late by more than 2 km of altitude ([Karlgaard et al. 2025](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250007036/downloads/aasimndl.pdf); [Spaceflight Now 2025](https://spaceflightnow.com/2025/05/14/intuitive-machines-ceo-points-to-issues-that-prevented-upright-touchdown-during-im-2-moon-landing/); [ispace 2023](https://www.ispace-inc.com/2023/05/26/ispace-announces-results-of-the-hakuto-r-mission-1-lunar-landing/)). The RL environment should therefore model sensor dropout and bias below ~30 m (where Apollo crews lost visibility and IM-2's lidar was blinded) as the dominant uncertainty, which is also where the meta-RL literature shows learned policies beat classical laws ([LSIC pads deck](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205009125/downloads/Lunar%20Landing%20&%20Launch%20Pads%20v1.pdf); [Gaudet et al. 2019](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1904.09865)).

The physics-based RL literature also fixes the reward design and the comparison protocol a defensible paper must follow. Gaudet's per-step reward is α‖v − v_targ‖ with α = −0.01 (velocity-field tracking), β‖F‖ with β = −0.05 (control effort as fuel proxy), γ = −100 on attitude-limit violation (terminating), a constant η = +0.01 per step to stop the agent crashing early to escape negative shaping, and a terminal bonus κ = +10 gated on ‖r‖ < 5 m, ‖v‖ < 2 m/s, attitude < 0.2 rad and rates < 0.2 rad/s, with separate discount rates for the shaping and terminal terms because GAE is "not compatible with multiple discount rates" ([Gaudet et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/html/1810.08719)). The accepted protocol is a dispersed-initial-condition Monte Carlo of 5,000-20,000 episodes reporting mean, std, min and max of each touchdown component and propellant, an open-loop fuel optimum (GPOPS, indirect PMP or lossless convexification) from the same initial conditions, a closed-loop classical law (DR/DV, ZEM/ZEV or Apollo polynomial) under the same perturbations, and robustness sweeps over mass, thrust, Isp, engine failure and sensor bias ([Gaudet et al. 2018](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1810.08719); [Furfaro et al. 2020](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2003.02182)). Holt, Origer and Izzo's 2025 comparison warns that behavioural cloning of optimal trajectories is near-optimal nominally (0.30% residual) but collapses under stochastic initial conditions (20-22% convergence versus 100% for RL), so the lander must be RL, not imitation ([Holt et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.19535)). Izzo's review adds the sobering calibration that DRL training costs "three orders of magnitude" more compute than one optimal-control solve and is preferable only "where uncertainties dominate", which is precisely the sensor-dropout regime above ([Izzo et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/html/2305.13078)). Gaudet's 3-DoF policy converged about 70 times faster than 6-DoF (300,000 episodes), so a 3-DoF lunar PPO study is workstation-feasible and 6-DoF is not on the target laptop ([Gaudet et al. 2018](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1810.08719)).

## Plume ejecta travels kilometres, yet NASA has defined no safe standoff

The measured and modelled picture agrees on geometry and disagrees on how far to stand back. Apollo descent-video photogrammetry shows dust leaving at **1-3° above the horizon** with 10-15 cm objects visible in flight, and Apollo 15's angle rose to 7-11° because it descended over a shallow crater ([Immer et al. 2008](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20130012063); [Metzger, Immer & Lane 2009/2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.06248)). For an Apollo-LM-class lander (~5 t, 45 kN) dust reaches **1-3 km/s** (some above the 2.38 km/s escape speed), sand 70-1,000 m/s, **1 cm gravel ~30 m/s landing within ~600 m, and 10 cm rocks ~10 m/s within ~60 m** ([Metzger, GLEX 2021](https://p.iafastro.directory/iac/paper/id/62796/abstract-pdf/GLEX-2021,12,3,7,x62796.brief.pdf)). The maximum dust speed is capped by exhaust exit velocity, ~4,500 m/s for H2/O2 against ~3,100 m/s for Apollo's hypergolics ([Metzger & Mantovani 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.12234)). Ejecta mass scales as **thrust^2.5**: the LM eroded ~2.6 t (measured from imagery) and a 40 t lander would eject (40/5)^2.5 = 181 times more, **~470 t as a worst-case bound** that the authors call "likely an overestimate", with erosion confined to a ~12 m radius, while ejection velocity scales only with the logarithm of lander mass ([Metzger et al. 2019](https://www.lpi.usra.edu/exploration/publications/MetzgerEtAl_SSERVI%20ESF2019_LanderPlumeEjecta.pdf); [Metzger & Mantovani 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.12234)). Applying that scaling to the simulation's lander classes gives rough upper bounds of **~0.7 t (3 t lander), ~40 t (15 t) and ~4,600 t (100 t)**; no source gives these directly and the 100 t figure is a bound, not an estimate. Measured anchors from recent landings are Chandrayaan-3's **~2.09 t** of ejected regolith over 108 m² and Chang'e-5's **~2,300 m² main disturbed zone plus ~15,300 m² diffuse zone**, with the ascent blast zone nearly twice the landing zone ([The Week 2023](https://www.theweek.in/news/india/2023/10/27/chandrayaan-3-isro-says-vikram-landers-soft-landing-ejected-209-tonnes-of-lunar-regolith.amp.html); [Qiao et al. 2023](https://buaa.globalimpact.cn/zh/publications/extensive-lunar-surface-disturbance-at-the-change-5-mission-landi/)). Damage evidence remains Apollo 12's LM sandblasting Surveyor III at **155-160 m** with impact velocities of **300 m/s to 2 km/s** inferred from pits ([Metzger, Immer & Lane 2009/2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.06248); [NASA ALSJ](https://nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/static/history/alsj/UL12Sandblast.html)), and Apollo 11's EASEP seismometer 17 m from the LM overheating from ascent-blown dust, after which instruments were sited 100-130 m away ([O'Brien](https://www.brianjobrien.com/easep-and-alseps)).

The standoff numbers in circulation are placeholders, and the sources say so. NASA's 2011 heritage-site guidance sets a **2.0 km** descent/landing exclusion radius with tangential approach and no overflight ([NASA 2011](https://www.nasa.gov/pdf/617743main_NASA-USG_LUNAR_HISTORIC_SITES_RevA-508.pdf)); Metzger, who helped write it, said "It's just a number we made up because we couldn't do any better at the time" ([KUNC/NPR 2019](https://www.kunc.org/npr-news/2019-02-21/how-do-you-preserve-history-on-the-moon)). NASA KSC's 2026 Lunar Innovation Park work states flatly that **"No safe scientific or operational standoff distances have been defined for various landers"** and then lands ~625 kg CLPS landers **~20 m apart behind 2.5 m regolith berms** (landing-zone centre spacing 19.9 m, 15 m zones, berm sized for 6° ejecta) to "reduce mission costs and logistics complexity" ([Gelino, NTRS 2026](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260002816/downloads/329%20-%20Site%20Plan%20and%20Bulk%20Presentation.pdf); [Hilburger & Gelino 2026](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260002824/downloads/Lunar_Inn_Park_Final_Storyboard_STRIVES_2.pdf)). The opposite philosophy, Constellation's Shackleton outpost, put the landing zone **2.2 km from and 290 m below** the habitation zone, so only ejecta ramped above 10.5-21° could reach it ([Metzger et al. 2019](https://www.lpi.usra.edu/exploration/publications/MetzgerEtAl_SSERVI%20ESF2019_LanderPlumeEjecta.pdf)). The one distance-to-risk relation is Wittal et al.'s: for a 10 t lander's 1.7-2.3 km/s fraction, **~8 impacts per landing at 1 km (23.5 J each), a 37% chance of impact at 5 km and 10% at 10 km** for a fixed base footprint ([Wittal et al. 2020](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205003590/downloads/20_Wittaletal_LanderPlumeEjecta.pdf); [NASA OTPS 2022](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/05/nasa-otps-lunar-landing-and-operations-policy-analysis-final-report-2.pdf)). NASA's 2024-2025 mobility papers convert all this into planning bands rather than safety limits: **>50 m to escape lander shadow, >1,000 m for blast ejecta, up to 5,000 m to aggregate elements in habitation zones**, traverses of up to 20° slope, planned mobility of ~1,500 kg cargo, and demand of 2,000-10,000 kg/yr ([NASA ACR24](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/acr24-compiled-white-papers.pdf); [NASA 2025](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/iparch10-wp-logistics-mobility-cargo.pdf)). The controlling variable in both NASA KSC's and Constellation's layouts is line-of-sight blocking of a 1-6° sheet, not raw distance, so pad siting on a down-slope or behind a crater rim relative to the base is the cheapest standoff, and Anderson et al. formalise this as crater umbrella zones of ~100 m for an 8 m lander and ~200 m for a 1 m rover ([Anderson et al. 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13263143/)).

Pad facts are equally notional. The LSIC/KSC "to be reviewed" requirements from 2020 are terrain slope <5°, cleared of rocks >20 cm, 3,000 Pa shear stress, landing within 100 m of a point, and no overflight of the base ([Mueller, LSIC 2020](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20205009125/downloads/Lunar%20Landing%20&%20Launch%20Pads%20v1.pdf)). The Artemis pad diameter requirement was **200 m with an effort to reduce it to 100 m**, because 200 m "is not feasible with a single Starship payload" ([Gelino et al. 2022](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220000334/downloads/In%20situ%20Lunar%20Launch%20and%20Landing%20Pad%20Construction%20with%20Regolith%20and%20Thermoset%20Polymers%20ASCE%20Earth%20and%20Space%202022%20Paper%20SUBMITTED.pdf)). Metzger and Autry's two-zone pad (12 m inner, 27 m outer radius for a 40 t lander with a 5 m landing-accuracy margin) costs **$130-548 M appropriated and takes 192-270 days at ≤50 kW** under Artemis constraints, with microwave sintering favoured for the inner zone and polymer infusion for the outer zone once transport falls below ~$110K/kg; build time collapses to days only with hundreds of kilowatts ([Metzger & Autry 2022](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2205.00378)). No source quantifies the fraction of ejecta a pad or berm removes; the design logic is that the outer radius sits where plume shear drops below the erosion threshold, and a failed pad could shed platy debris worse than natural regolith ([Metzger, Immer & Lane 2009/2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2104.06248); [UCF CLASS](https://sciences.ucf.edu/class/?p=2214)). Hardware demonstrations are Earth-only: PISCES's 100-paver basalt pad (2015), ICON's 3D-printed Lunar PAD hot-fire (2021), ICON's $57.2 M Project Olympus (2022) and Astroport's SBIR/STTR furnaces ([PISCES 2016](https://pacificspacecenter.com/2016/01/05/pisces-completes-lunar-landing-pad-bullseye/); [3D Printing Industry 2021](https://3dprintingindustry.com/news/icon-and-nasa-bring-lunar-infrastructure-closer-with-worlds-first-3d-printed-rocket-pad-187323/); [3D Printing Industry 2022](https://3dprintingindustry.com/news/icon-awarded-new-57-2m-nasa-contract-to-develop-system-than-can-3d-print-lunar-structures-218125/)).

NASA states its open questions plainly, and the paper should quote them. Apollo experience "cannot be extrapolated" to Human Landing Systems for PSI ([NASA PSI poster 2023](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20230011539/downloads/IPPW_Poster_KorzunChambersPSI_vSTRIVES.pdf)); NASA "currently lacks direct in-situ measurements of PSI phenomena, leaving predictions largely qualitative and uncertain" ([NASA 2023](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/safe-and-precise-landing-at-lunar-sites.pdf)); the LSIC community expects the decision on landing near versus far from habitats by about 2027 ([LSIC](https://lsic.jhuapl.edu/Resources/files/The%20Path%20to%20an%20Enduring%20Lunar%20Presence.pdf)); the Innovation Park deck lists ejecta effects on berms, secondary ejecta and ascent trajectories as open items ([Gelino, NTRS 2026](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260002816/downloads/329%20-%20Site%20Plan%20and%20Bulk%20Presentation.pdf)); Metzger's 2024 erosion theory defers predictions for larger landers to an unpublished "part 2" ([Metzger 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2403.18583)); and the first dedicated in-situ PSI dataset, SCALPSS 1.1 on Blue Ghost, has produced a post-landing DEM with a 60-80 mm depression of uncertain origin but no published erosion volume ([Tyrrell et al. 2026](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20250010922/downloads/2026_SciTech_SCALPSS_PostLandingDEM_FINAL.pdf)). No source defines a numeric no-drive window; because the Moon has no atmosphere, ejecta is ballistic, dust at 1 km/s reaches a rover 1 km away within ~1 s and nearly all sub-escape ejecta is down within 1-2 minutes of cutoff, so a conservative shelter window of roughly two minutes before touchdown to two minutes after engine cutoff (and again at ascent) follows from physics, not from published operations guidance, and the documented mitigations are positional (berm, terrain, stowed arrays, optics facing away) rather than temporal ([NTRS 20090022233](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20090022233/downloads/20090022233.pdf); [Rivera 2021](https://stars.library.ucf.edu/etd2020/749/)). For the DES this means the plume module should be a keep-out radius R and a stand-down duration T, both swept parametrically, with the ejecta-mass scaling used only to order the lander classes.

## Earth teleoperation halves rover speed and nobody reports interventions per kilometre

The hardware-grounded lunar driving numbers are few. Apollo's LRV averaged **8-9 km/h while moving** (27.8 km in 3 h 02 min on Apollo 15; 35.9 km in 4 h 26 min on Apollo 17) and used **1.58-1.67 Ah/km at 36 V, about 57-60 Wh/km**, for ~700 kg of vehicle plus payload ([NSSDC](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/lunar/apollo_lrv.html); [ALSJ Apollo 17](https://www.apollojournals.org/alsj/a17/a17.clsout3.html)). Yutu-2, teleoperated, drove **1,613 m in about six years** with in-situ slip ratios of **10.5%, 13.0% and 19.7%** and ~10 mm sinkage ([Wikipedia: Yutu-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yutu-2); [Jilin Univ.](https://cjournal.hep.com.cn/1671-5497/CN/PDF/10.13229/j.cnki.jdxbgxb.20211193); [Nature Geoscience ESM](https://media.springernature.com/original/springer-static/esm/art%3A10.1038%2Fs41561-022-00905-6/MediaObjects/41561_2022_905_MOESM1_ESM.pdf)). For Earth teleoperation, NASA JSC's 2023 human-in-the-loop studies with a government-reference LTV (15 km/h, ±20° slopes) give **speed made good of 3.24 km/h at 0 s, 2.56 km/h at 4 s (12% slower, p = .028), 2.03 km/h at 6 s and 1.76 km/h at 8 s**, with operators settling into a 1-1.5 km/h cruise, more braking and higher workload under delay, and >95% of navigation at 6-8 s using camera pans to ground-truth hazards; speed without stops fell only from 3.80 to 3.55 km/h, so the latency penalty is mostly stopping time, and the authors call the result "a very conservative average" that nonetheless meets LTV-SYS-071 ([Litaker et al. 2025](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20240011392)). VIPER's concept of operations was slower still: 6-10 s latency, a stop about every 4.5 m, **0.36-0.72 km/h** travel speed, 447 kg roving mass, 5,420 Wh battery, 15° nominal slope, 15 cm obstacles, 50 h low-power survival ([NASA VIPER](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/viper/lunar-operations/); [Bluethmann 2024](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240013903/downloads/LSIC_2024-Tech%20Infusion_Panel-VIPER_Mob-HW.pdf)). No lunar rover has demonstrated supervised or full autonomy; the flight analogue is Perseverance AutoNav at ~0.12 km/h and the aspirational target is Endurance-A's "~10x Perseverance", mostly at night ([JPL 2021](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/nasas-self-driving-perseverance-mars-rover-takes-the-wheel/); [NASA SMD 2023](https://science.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/10/intrepid-endurance-inspire-comparison.pdf)).

The slip-slope relation is the piece most likely to be wrong if taken from Earth tests. Hu et al.'s Chrono continuum simulations, correlated against NASA Glenn SLOPE-lab single-wheel and MGRU3 rover tests on GRC-1 and GRC-3 simulants, show that the 73 kg MGRU3 climbs 30° at ~42% slip on Earth while the 440 kg VIPER in lunar gravity would see **~85% slip, past the 80% dig-in threshold**, that slope-vs-slip curves are invariant to wheel speed, and that Bekker-Wong parameters measured on Earth do not transfer because they are gravity-dependent; the authors conclude "a paradigm shift is justified when judging trafficability in different gravity fields" ([Hu et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.11001); [Unjhawala et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.05643)). A cargo rover with a 1,600 kg payload loads its wheels three to four times more than VIPER, so **20° is a hard planning limit and 15° the nominal design limit** for the driving model. The lighting study adds a cost no traversability model includes: at the 1.2° sun elevation used in the JSC simulator, up-sun legs force ±20-30° tacking, the rover's own shadow forces tacking down-sun, artificial lights are useless while lit terrain is in view, and some geometries must be excluded from route planning outright, all of which "would affect the accuracy and speed of any teleoperations from Earth" ([Litaker et al. 2025 lighting](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20240011393/downloads/Lighiting%20Study%20IEEE%20Paper%20FINAL-1.pdf)). East-west and north-south legs therefore have different time and energy costs, which the DES does not yet know.

The evaluation metric is where the behavioural-cloning tutorial must be rewritten. NVIDIA's PilotNet defines autonomy as **(1 − interventions × 6 s / elapsed time) × 100** and reports ~98% on a Holmdel drive and 10 miles with zero interventions ([Bojarski et al. 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.07316)). That 6 s assumes a co-located human; with a 5-10 s round trip an Earth-based intervention costs at least one RTT plus the tens-of-seconds ground-truthing stops the JSC data describe, so autonomy percentage must be recomputed with intervention cost ≥ RTT + stop/restart time, and interventions per kilometre becomes the logistics-facing quantity. No planetary study reports it: Mars work reports AUROC/F1 or ~15% slip-prediction error, LunaLab work reports tracking error (DreamerV3 2.3-3.6 cm; PPO 13-15 cm), and the JSC studies report speed made good ([Angelova et al. 2006](https://robotics.jpl.nasa.gov/media/documents/Angelova06LearningSlip.pdf); [Orsula et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.11503)). End-to-end BC on lunar rovers exists only in simulation theses with no intervention numbers ([Pisa ETD 2025](https://etd.adm.unipi.it/t/etd-05112025-215810)). Three gaps are therefore well supported: no study links a learned driving model to logistics outcomes; no learned driving model is trained or evaluated under south-pole lighting even though NASA's study shows it dominates speed and route feasibility (the SPICE-HL3 dataset of ~1.3 M images at 5 and 50 cm/s from dawn to night was built precisely for this scarcity); and slip-vs-slope for heavy cargo rovers in lunar gravity exists only as physics prediction ([arXiv 2506.22956](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.22956); [arXiv 2309.12397](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.12397)). The driving model should output, per leg, a speed drawn from the autonomy-level curve above with slope and lighting penalties, a slip ratio from the Hu curve, energy from the LRV baseline scaled by mass and slip, and an intervention count converted to time by RTT; it should be validated against the 12% loss at 4 s and the 0.4-4.4 kWh PSR round trips before it is allowed to drive the DES.

## LOLA 5 m tiles and NASA models are free; Artemis landers are not

The terrain is the one place where the site can be both scientifically exact and visually professional at no cost. NASA GSFC's Planetary Geodynamics Data Archive publishes **per-site 20 km × 20 km LOLA DEM fields at 5 m/px** for exactly the Artemis candidate regions (Site01 Connecting Ridge, Site04 Shackleton rim, Site11 de Gerlache rim, Site23 Malapert massif, Site20v2 Leibnitz beta plateau, i.e. Mons Mouton, plus Haworth, Nobile rims, Amundsen and others) as south polar stereographic GeoTIFFs with slope, count, uncertainty and 100 DEM "clones" for error propagation; stated accuracy is median RMS Z error 0.30-0.50 m and slope error 1.5-2.5°, with about 90% of 5 m pixels interpolated ([PGDA products/78](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/78)). For arbitrary windows the **3.3 GB 87°S-90°S 5 m mosaic** (`ldem_87s_5mpp.tif`, with 820 MB hillshade and 4.9 GB slope companions) was built from 97 such fields with 30-55 cm RMS residuals ([PGDA products/81](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/81)), and the lighter **2.6 GB cloud-optimised 20 m DEM** `LDEM_80S_20MPP_ADJ.TIF` comes with 20 m PSR rasters and shapefiles (506,349 PSRs; 1,531 above 1 km²), hillshade, error and roughness layers, all citable to Barker et al. 2023 with data DOI 10.60903/gsfcpgda-lola-spole ([PGDA products/90](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/90/)). Illumination ground truth comes from PGDA's average-visibility, PSR, Earth-visibility and sky-visibility maps at 60-240 m, computed hourly over an 18.6-year nutation cycle with a finite solar disc ([PGDA products/69](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/69)). A 20 km window at 5 m is a 4,000 × 4,000 crop; `gdal_translate -projwin ... -ot UInt16 -scale zmin zmax 0 65535 -of PNG` produces a browser heightmap, and NASA SVS's own convention of storing LOLA heights as uint16 half-metres with a +20,000 offset is a ready recipe ([GDAL](https://gdal.org/en/stable/programs/gdal_translate.html); [NASA SVS 4720](https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/4720)).

Three licensing traps matter. First, NASA imagery is "generally not subject to copyright in the United States" but the NASA insignia, logotype and seal are protected, no endorsement may be implied, and identifiable current astronauts cannot appear in commercial products ([NASA media usage](https://www.nasa.gov/nasa-brand-center/images-and-media/)); ESA and JAXA permit educational use with the prescribed credit but not commercial use without permission ([ESA terms](https://www.esa.int/ESA_Multimedia/Terms_and_conditions_of_use_of_images_and_videos_available_on_the_esa_website); [JAXA policy](https://global.jaxa.jp/policy.html)); SpaceX's Flickr licence has changed at least three times and is unverified today ([Gigazine 2019](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20191212-spacex-copyright-restrictions-photos)). Second, Cesium Moon is the fastest path to streaming 1 m NAC imagery on 5 m terrain but its terms require the "Cesium ion" logo on the main application window, forbid offline copies, and the free Community tier (10 GB storage, 15 GB/month streaming) excludes "funded educational research" and government projects, pushing a grant-funded lab to the $149/month Commercial tier ([Cesium pricing](https://cesium.com/platform/cesium-ion/pricing/); [Cesium ToS](https://cesium.com/legal/terms-of-service/)). Third, no official public 3D model of any Artemis-era lander or rover exists, so the legally clean route is parametric low-poly stand-ins built from public dimensions (9 m × ~50 m cylinder for Starship HLS, 3.08 m × 8.05 m for MK1, a generic four-wheel LTV) labelled "representative", lit and shadowed on the real terrain, with the NASA Apollo LM and RASSOR models converted to glTF where heritage vehicles appear ([NASA HLS](https://www.nasa.gov/humans-in-space/human-landing-system/); [Wikipedia: Blue Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blue_Moon_(spacecraft))). For the 20 km site the right texture is the 1 m NAC mosaic of the chosen region or a hillshade synthesised from the 5 m DEM; the CGI Moon Kit's 100 m-class WAC colour, with its polar fill, belongs only on the distant context globe ([NASA SVS 4720](https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/4720)).

## Three.js with NASA's 3D Tiles renderer, Distill typography, WCAG motion rules

Two stacks are realistic for lunar terrain plus vehicles plus time scrubbing, both permissively licensed and both used by NASA-adjacent teams. CesiumJS (Apache 2.0) with Cesium Moon gives a working time-dynamic viewer fastest: `Ellipsoid.MOON`, `Cesium3DTileset.fromIonAssetId(2684829)`, `PathGraphics` trails with `leadTime`/`trailTime`, a `SunLight` and an `Animation` widget with play/pause/reverse and a speed shuttle ring, at the cost of a single-body model, an Earth-oriented widget set and the ion terms above ([Cesium Moon](https://cesium.com/platform/cesium-ion/content/cesium-moon/); [Cesium PathGraphics](https://cesium.com/learn/cesiumjs/ref-doc/PathGraphics.html); [Cesium Animation](https://cesium.com/learn/cesiumjs/ref-doc/Animation.html)). Three.js (MIT, ~168 kB min+gz against Babylon's 1.4 MB) with NASA-AMMOS's `3d-tiles-renderer` (Apache-2.0, Caltech/JPL, "used on the Perseverance and Curiosity rover missions", with Cesium ion, WMTS and quantized-mesh plugins) gives full control of lighting, materials and overlays, can stream Cesium Moon or a self-hosted LOLA tileset, and matches LunarSim's existing hand-written JavaScript engine ([NASA-AMMOS/3DTilesRendererJS](https://github.com/NASA-AMMOS/3DTilesRendererJS); [LogRocket 2025](https://blog.logrocket.com/three-js-vs-babylon-js/)). Three.js is the recommendation because the site's core is replaying simulated trajectories on real terrain with custom overlays (slope, energy, keep-out radii, dispersion ellipses), and because self-hosting the PGDA tiles avoids the Cesium ion funding clause. The terrain recipe follows from Three.js's own documentation: a 16-bit heightmap `displacementMap` must be paired with a precomputed normal map because "the renderer can not recompute surface normals from the displaced vertices"; a `PlaneGeometry` of ~1,024² segments (about a million vertices) is fine for laptop GPUs, with 5 m detail only near the lander; and the grazing polar sun needs cascaded shadow maps (`SunLightShadow`, two cascades at 1,024² default, or `three-csm`) with tight shadow frusta and `normalBias`, plus a precomputed horizon or ray-marched height-field shadow for the far field, validated against PGDA's PSR rasters so Shackleton's floor stays dark ([three.js MeshStandardMaterial](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/MeshStandardMaterial.html); [three.js SunLightShadow](https://threejs.org/docs/pages/SunLightShadow.html); [PGDA products/90](https://pgda.gsfc.nasa.gov/products/90/)). NASA SVS's own south-pole renders are the visual reference: at the poles the Sun "is never more than 1.5° above or below the horizon" and shadows "reach as far as mountains 120 kilometers away", so the lighting is a scientific statement rather than a mood ([SVS 4893](https://svs.gsfc.nasa.gov/4893)).

The design language of credible scientific sites is measurable and quiet. Distill's stylesheet sets body text at 16 px rising to 1.06 rem with **line-height 1.7**, text colour **rgba(0,0,0,0.8)**, hairlines and rules at **rgba(0,0,0,0.1)**, h2 at 24-36 px, blockquotes at 0.6 alpha and 14 px code ([Distill d-article.css](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/distillpub/template/master/src/styles/d-article.css)). Fonts should be one OFL sans (Source Sans 3, Inter or Lato) self-hosted via `@font-face`, renamed if subset because WOFF conversion counts as modification under Reserved Font Names ([OFL FAQ](https://openfontlicense.org/ofl-faq/)), plus a tabular-figure monospace for telemetry. Palettes should be Paul Tol's Bright set (`#4477AA #EE6677 #228833 #CCBB44 #66CCEE #AA3377 #BBBBBB`) or Okabe-Ito for up to seven categorical series, with Crameri's perceptually uniform *batlow* or Tol's greyscale-safe Iridescent for elevation and slope, never rainbow/jet, and shape or direct labels alongside colour because ~6% of men are green-blind ([Paul Tol](https://sronpersonalpages.nl/~pault/); [Okabe & Ito](https://jfly.uni-koeln.de/color/); [Crameri](https://www.fabiocrameri.ch/colourmaps/)). Charts for a research audience need explicit uncertainty: Vega-Lite's `errorband` with `extent: "ci"` is "the 95% bootstrapped confidence interval of the mean" (default `stderr`), Observable Plot's `linearRegressionY` draws a `ci` 0.95 band at `fillOpacity` 0.1 and warns that regression "push[es] people to the wrong conclusions", and facets via `fx`/`fy` give Tufte's small multiples on shared scales ([Vega-Lite errorband](https://vega.github.io/vega-lite/docs/errorband.html); [Observable Plot regression](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/observablehq/plot/main/docs/marks/linear-regression.md); [Observable Plot facets](https://observablehq.com/plot/features/facets)). Every figure should expose SVG/PNG/CSV download as Vega-Embed's actions menu does, and serve its data alongside, which is what separates a research artefact from a marketing graphic ([vega-embed](https://github.com/vega/vega-embed); [Plotly export](https://plotly.com/javascript/static-image-export/)). The layout precedent is Earth Engine's 400 px vertical control panel beside a dominant map with small `bottom-right` overlays ([Earth Engine UI](https://developers.google.com/earth-engine/guides/ui_panels)); the telemetry minimum is SpaceX's T+, velocity and altitude trio for the lander, plus slope, energy and distance-to-goal for the rover, each traceable to a logged variable with units, since numbers not driven by simulation state are the research-site equivalent of chartjunk ([SpaceXtract](https://github.com/shahar603/SpaceXtract)). Camera presets plus a narrative step list (Cesium Stories' view-per-slide, Event Horizon's saved views at milestones, NASA Eyes' guided journeys) is the standard way to mix storytelling with free exploration ([Cesium Stories](https://cesium.com/cesium-stories); [Cesium blog 2026](https://cesium.com/blog/2026/09/22/blacknight-space-event-horizon-cesiumjs/); [JPL 2022](https://www.jpl.nasa.gov/news/explore-the-solar-system-with-nasas-new-and-improved-3d-eyes/)).

Accessibility and performance rules are concrete. WCAG 2.2 SC 2.2.2 (Level A) requires a pause/stop/hide mechanism for any auto-starting motion lasting over five seconds, and SC 2.3.3 (AAA) requires that interaction-triggered motion can be disabled unless essential; because CSS cannot gate canvas/WebGL animation, the page must read `window.matchMedia('(prefers-reduced-motion: reduce)')` and treat it as tiering (keep fades, remove displacement), not a kill switch ([W3C SC 2.2.2](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/pause-stop-hide.html); [W3C SC 2.3.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/animation-from-interactions.html); [Verdigris](https://design.verdigris.co/categories/animation/reduced-motion)). MDN states that canvas "content is not exposed to accessibility tools as semantic HTML is", so an always-present DOM telemetry table and plan-view 2D fallback must mirror the 3D state and double as the screen-reader alternative and the Tier-0 fallback ([MDN canvas](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/HTML/Reference/Elements/canvas)). Performance guidance from practitioners is to tier devices with `detect-gpu` (Tier 0 = no WebGL or <15 fps, provide a non-WebGL fallback; Tier 3 ≥60 fps), cap the drawing buffer at an absolute pixel budget of about 5.2 MP rather than a device pixel ratio, hold a steady 30 fps floor, keep the lit-material light count constant to avoid shader recompiles, bound the tile LRU cache (no new tiles load once `maxBytesSize` is hit), and avoid CSS blend overlays on the canvas, which cost 30-45 ms/frame in one case study ([detect-gpu](https://github.com/pmndrs/detect-gpu); [Codrops](https://tympanus.net/codrops/?p=119712); [3DTilesRendererJS](https://github.com/NASA-AMMOS/3DTilesRendererJS)). The site map that reads as credible follows the Nerfies/Academic-project-page lineage (title, authors, links row, abstract, figures, BibTeX) for `/paper`, Distill's rule that "weaknesses must be disclosed" for `/method`, Keep a Changelog for `/changelog`, versioned datasets with licences and checksums for `/data`, and shareable `/replay/<id>` permalinks, with the viewer, paper and data never gated behind login, as Earth Engine Apps are viewable "without needing to sign in" ([Academic Project Page Template](https://github.com/eliahuhorwitz/Academic-project-page-template); [Distill](https://distill.pub/journal/); [keepachangelog](https://keepachangelog.com/en/1.1.0/); [Earth Engine Apps](https://developers.google.com/earth-engine/guides/apps)). Reusing the Nerfies template verbatim makes the page CC BY-SA 4.0; borrowing only its structure in original HTML avoids the share-alike obligation ([nerfies.github.io](https://github.com/nerfies/nerfies.github.io)).

## One €4.49 VPS with SQLite, Litestream and ORCID login is defensible

The credible part of a research backend is its data model and DOI path, not its hosting brand, which lets the whole system run on one small machine. FastAPI's own documentation says heavy background computation belongs in "bigger tools like Celery" with a message queue rather than `BackgroundTasks`, so the second-to-minute simulation jobs need a separate worker: RQ 2.12.0 (Aug 2026, Redis ≥5 or Valkey ≥7.2, built-in cron since 2.5) is the smallest credible queue, a DB-polling worker on a `jobs` table is the zero-extra-service start, Celery 5.6.3 is defensible but heavier, and `arq` is "in maintenance only mode" ([FastAPI](https://fastapi.tiangolo.com/tutorial/background-tasks/); [PyPI rq](https://pypi.org/project/rq/); [PyPI arq](https://pypi.org/project/arq)). Progress streams to the browser over Server-Sent Events via `sse-starlette` 3.5.0 (Sep 2026, disconnect detection, 15 s ping), which is one-directional, CDN-friendly and auto-reconnecting, and `slowapi` cannot rate-limit WebSockets anyway ([PyPI sse-starlette](https://pypi.org/project/sse-starlette/); [slowapi](https://github.com/laurents/slowapi)). Django 6.0 is the fallback if auth breadth matters more than API ergonomics: its new Tasks framework "does not provide a worker mechanism" but `django-allauth` ships an ORCID provider that works "out of the box" with the public API and WebAuthn passkeys since 64.x ([Django 6.0 Tasks](https://docs.djangoproject.com/en/6.0/topics/tasks/); [allauth ORCID](https://docs.allauth.org/en/latest/socialaccount/providers/orcid.html)).

SQLite in WAL mode is enough by SQLite's own guidance: "any site that gets fewer than 100K hits/day should work fine", WAL lets readers and the single writer proceed concurrently provided "all processes using a database must be on the same host computer", JSON operators `->`/`->>` are built in since 3.38.0 and JSONB since 3.45.0 ([SQLite when to use](https://www.sqlite.org/whentouse.html); [SQLite WAL](https://www.sqlite.org/wal.html); [SQLite JSON](https://www.sqlite.org/json1.html)). Litestream 0.5 adds continuous replication to S3-compatible storage with point-in-time restore from "only a dozen or so files on average", and `litestream-vfs` 0.5.17 (Aug 2026, beta, Linux/macOS only) loads directly into Python's `sqlite3` ([Fly.io Litestream 0.5](https://fly.io/blog/litestream-v050-is-here/); [PyPI litestream-vfs](https://pypi.org/project/litestream-vfs/)). Authentication should follow the least-friction order for academics: ORCID (free Public API credentials "for non-commercial use by individuals", yielding the identifier to stamp on run records), GitHub, then email magic links, with Authlib 1.9.1 as the OAuth client in FastAPI and passkeys via `py_webauthn` later ([ORCID](https://info.orcid.org/documentation/integration-guide/registering-a-public-api-client/); [Authlib](https://docs.authlib.org/en/latest/)). If passwords exist at all, OWASP's minimum is **Argon2id with 19 MiB, 2 iterations, parallelism 1** via `argon2-cffi` 25.1.0; magic-link tokens must be cryptographically random, single-use, expiring, rate-limited and stored hashed ([OWASP password storage](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Password_Storage_Cheat_Sheet.html); [OWASP forgot password](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Forgot_Password_Cheat_Sheet.html)). Sessions should be opaque server-side IDs of ≥128 bits in `HttpOnly; Secure; SameSite` cookies, never JWTs in localStorage, which OWASP says are "accessible to any JavaScript executing in the origin" ([OWASP session management](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Session_Management_Cheat_Sheet.html)). The cross-origin consequence is decisive for the existing GitHub Pages frontend: `github.io` is on the Public Suffix List, so `<user>.github.io` and the API are different sites, cookies become third-party cookies that Safari, Firefox and Brave block by default (Chrome abandoned deprecation in April 2025 but users may still block), and bearer tokens would be forced; moving the page to `app.<domain>` via CNAME and the API to `api.<domain>` makes them same-site so `SameSite=Lax` cookies work, with CORS allow-listing the exact origin, Origin-header checks and a custom request header as the CSRF defence that OWASP calls "particularly well suited for AJAX or API endpoints" ([Atomic Object PSL](https://spin.atomicobject.com/public-suffix-list/); [MDN Set-Cookie](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/HTTP/Reference/Headers/Set-Cookie); [Privacy Sandbox 2025](https://privacysandbox.google.com/blog/privacy-sandbox-next-steps); [OWASP CSRF](https://cheatsheetseries.owasp.org/cheatsheets/Cross-Site_Request_Forgery_Prevention_Cheat_Sheet.html)). GitHub recommends verifying the custom domain before adding it to prevent takeover ([GitHub custom domains](https://docs.github.com/en/pages/configuring-a-custom-domain-for-your-github-pages-site/about-custom-domains-and-github-pages)).

The run record is what makes the backend "built in a new way" in a sense reviewers will respect. The platforms researchers already trust converge on the same provenance triple: W&B records the git commit and captures the diff patch of uncommitted changes so `wandb restore` can check out the exact code; OpenML runs carry flow plus hyperparameter "setups" and warn that "exactly reproducing all results may not always be possible" across library versions; Zenodo gives every file version its own DOI under a concept DOI and keeps records "for the lifetime of the repository" ([W&B restore](https://docs.coreweave.com/models/ref/cli/wandb-restore); [OpenML runs](https://docs.openml.org/concepts/runs/); [Zenodo versions](https://help.zenodo.org/docs/deposit/manage-versions/); [Zenodo policies](https://about.zenodo.org/policies/)). The proposed immutable `runs` row therefore stores engine semver, full git SHA, dirty flag and patch hash, lockfile hash, Python and platform versions, canonical scenario hash (sorted keys, fixed float format), policy-weights hash and format, one named seed per RNG stream with the algorithm name, timestamps and worker, and a SHA-256 of the output trace stored content-addressed under `blobs/<sha[:2]>/<sha>` in Git's object-store manner, giving deduplication and tamper evidence for free ([Pro Git](https://git-scm.com/book/en/v2/Git-Internals-Git-Objects)). A `POST /runs/{id}/replicate` endpoint that re-enqueues with identical inputs and compares output hashes turns replication into a machine-checkable badge, which LunarSim's existing Python-versus-JavaScript cross-implementation check already does in spirit. Connecting the repository to Zenodo's GitHub integration mints a version DOI per tagged release automatically, and the REST API's `prereserve_doi` lets a user deposit a run bundle (record JSON, scenario, seeds, trace, lockfile, `CITATION.cff`) from the site after testing on the sandbox ([Zenodo GitHub](https://help.zenodo.org/docs/github/enable-repository/); [Zenodo developers](https://developers.zenodo.org/)). Papers with Code shut down in July 2025, so hosting the leaderboard yourself with CC-BY export is now the realistic path; rank only runs with `git_dirty=false` on a DOI'd release and freeze snapshots per engine version ([hyper.ai 2025](https://hyper.ai/en/news/42900)). Abuse protection should budget the scarce resource, CPU-minutes per account per day enforced at enqueue, with `slowapi` per-route limits documented and returned as 429 with `Retry-After` the way ORCID and Zenodo publish theirs, Turnstile on sign-up and anonymous submission, and signed object-storage URLs for large traces ([slowapi](https://github.com/laurents/slowapi); [Turnstile](https://developers.cloudflare.com/turnstile/)).

`LunarLander-v3` is defined exactly by the Gymnasium source and should be treated as the pedagogical warm-up, not the research model. It is a Box2D world at `FPS = 50`, `SCALE = 30`, `MAIN_ENGINE_POWER = 13.0`, `SIDE_ENGINE_POWER = 0.6`, `INITIAL_RANDOM = 1000.0`, 600 × 400 viewport, default `gravity = -10.0` (bounded in (−12, 0)), with optional wind (`wind_power` 15.0, `turbulence_power` 1.5). The 8-D observation is `[(x − 10)/10, (y − helipad − 0.6)/6.667, vx·10/50, vy·6.667/50, angle, 20·ω/50, leg1, leg2]` with bounds ±2.5, ±2.5, ±10, ±10, ±2π, ±10, [0,1], [0,1]. The reward is the step difference of `shaping = −100·√(x²+y²) − 100·√(vx²+vy²) − 100·|angle| + 10·leg1 + 10·leg2`, minus `0.30·m_power` and `0.03·s_power` per frame, overwritten (not added) to −100 on crash or |x| ≥ 1 and +100 when the body falls asleep at rest; episodes are capped at 1,000 steps (20 s) and `reward_threshold` is 200 ([lunar_lander.py](https://github.com/Farama-Foundation/Gymnasium/blob/main/gymnasium/envs/box2d/lunar_lander.py); [envs/__init__.py](https://github.com/Farama-Foundation/Gymnasium/blob/main/gymnasium/envs/__init__.py)). Actions are `Discrete(4)` (noop, left, main, right) or `Box(−1, 1, (2,))` where the main engine fires above 0 at 50-100% throttle (`m_power = (clip(a0,0,1)+1)/2`) and side engines fire when |a1| > 0.5; a random dispersion of ±1/SCALE is added to the thrust point every step, so the dynamics are stochastic even without wind. v3 (Gymnasium 1.0.0a2, May 2024) fixed the world not being fully destroyed on reset and wind offsets not being re-randomised, and widened the observation bounds ([Gymnasium docs](https://gymnasium.farama.org/environments/box2d/lunar_lander/); [Gymnasium release notes](https://gymnasium.farama.org/gymnasium_release_notes/)). Two behaviours matter for the tutorial's "reward hacking" theme: because shaping is a potential difference, hovering accrues zero net shaping while paying up to 0.30 per frame, so pure hovering is already penalised; "float and never land" episodes arise instead because the +100 requires the Box2D body to fall asleep, which an agent that keeps firing never triggers, and the 1,000-step truncation arrives without penalty. SALSA-RL's appendix shows the inverse lever, removing landing bonuses and adding a +0.2 survival bonus to deliberately train hovering ([Li & Maulik 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.15512)). Gaudet's η = +0.01 constant and SALSA's +0.2 are the same knob pulled in opposite directions, and the two-discount-rate scheme is the published answer to balancing them ([Gaudet et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/html/1810.08719)).

The PPO configuration that solves it is RL Zoo's, identical for discrete and continuous: `n_envs: 16`, `n_timesteps: 1e6`, `MlpPolicy`, `n_steps: 1024`, `batch_size: 64`, `gae_lambda: 0.98`, `gamma: 0.999`, `n_epochs: 4`, `ent_coef: 0.01`, with SB3 defaults for `learning_rate` 3e-4 and `clip_range` 0.2 and no `VecNormalize` ([RL Zoo ppo.yml](https://github.com/DLR-RM/rl-baselines3-zoo/blob/master/hyperparams/ppo.yml); [SB3 PPO docs](https://stable-baselines3.readthedocs.io/en/master/modules/ppo.html)). It scores **242 ± 32 (discrete) and 271 ± 32 (continuous) at 1 M steps** in the Zoo benchmark (v2 ids, single run), the Hugging Face course reports 200.20 ± 20.80 in "approximately ~20min" on Colab, and community v3 cards show 260.6 ± 18.9 at 1 M and 278.8 ± 19.1 at 2 M steps against 82 ± 102 at 200k ([RL Zoo benchmark](https://github.com/DLR-RM/rl-baselines3-zoo/blob/master/benchmark.md); [HF course](https://huggingface.co/learn/deep-rl-course/unit1/hands-on); [HF model card](https://huggingface.co/asiful2/ppo-LunarLander-v3)). SB3 warns at setup that a non-CNN policy on GPU "is primarily intended to run on the CPU" and "the GPU utilization will be poor", so the lander MLP trains on CPU with `device="cpu"`, `SubprocVecEnv` needs the `if __name__ == "__main__":` guard on Windows because the start method is `spawn`, and evaluation uses `evaluate_policy` over 10-20 episodes with `deterministic=True` on a `Monitor`-wrapped env across several seeds ([SB3 on_policy_algorithm.py](https://github.com/DLR-RM/stable-baselines3/blob/master/stable_baselines3/common/on_policy_algorithm.py); [SB3 vec_envs](https://stable-baselines3.readthedocs.io/en/master/guide/vec_envs.html); [SB3 evaluation](https://stable-baselines3.readthedocs.io/en/master/common/evaluation.html)). No primary source gives CPU wall-clock on a laptop; it must be measured.

The export path to JavaScript needs no runtime for the lander. SB3's default `ActorCriticPolicy` is `net_arch=dict(pi=[64, 64], vf=[64, 64])` with `nn.Tanh`, so the discrete actor is `8 → 64 → 64 → 4`, **4,996 parameters** (the critic's 4,801 are not needed at inference); the deterministic action is `argmax(logits)` for `Categorical` and the clipped Gaussian mean for `DiagGaussian` (the learned `log_std` matters only for sampling) ([SB3 policies.py](https://github.com/DLR-RM/stable-baselines3/blob/master/stable_baselines3/common/policies.py); [SB3 distributions.py](https://github.com/DLR-RM/stable-baselines3/blob/master/stable_baselines3/common/distributions.py)). Dumping `model.policy.state_dict()` entries under `mlp_extractor.policy_net.{0,2}.{weight,bias}` and `action_net.{weight,bias}` to JSON (tens of kilobytes) and re-implementing two tanh linear layers plus argmax in about thirty lines of JavaScript and NumPy gives bit-identical behaviour in `sim.js` and the Python engine, provided the observation is built with exactly the scaling above and no `VecNormalize` was used (if it is added later, its running mean and variance must be exported too) ([SB3 export guide](https://stable-baselines3.readthedocs.io/en/master/guide/export.html)). The official ONNX route (`OnnxableSB3Policy` wrapper, `th.onnx.export(..., opset_version=17)`, "doesn't include the post-processing step" of clipping) plus `onnxruntime-web` 1.30.0 is the cross-check, at the cost of a 368 kB script and a 14.2 MB WASM download that a 5k-parameter MLP does not justify ([SB3 export guide](https://stable-baselines3.readthedocs.io/en/master/guide/export.html); [jsDelivr listing](https://data.jsdelivr.com/v1/packages/npm/onnxruntime-web@1.30.0?structure=flat)). The TensorFlow.js route is closed: tfjs 4.22.0 and its converter were last published in October 2024, the converter "does not support converting Keras models saved in the new format", and SB3's own guide calls it "not currently maintained" ([tfjs issue #8266](https://github.com/tensorflow/tfjs/issues/8266); [npm tfjs](https://www.npmjs.com/package/@tensorflow/tfjs)).

The research-grade lander is a custom `gym.Env`, not `LunarLander-v3`. Gymnasium's contract is `observation_space`, `action_space`, `reset(seed, options)` calling `super().reset(seed=seed)` and returning `(obs, info)`, `step(action)` returning `(obs, reward, terminated, truncated, info)`, registration with `gym.register(id="lunar/Descent-v0", entry_point=..., max_episode_steps=N)` and validation with `check_env`, which in 1.4.0 also insists that `reset`/`step` return fresh observation and info objects ([Gymnasium custom env](https://gymnasium.farama.org/introduction/create_custom_env/); [Gymnasium 1.4.0 release](https://github.com/Farama-Foundation/Gymnasium/releases/tag/v1.4.0)). A 3-DoF planar or point-mass terminal-descent environment should take its constants from the lander table above (1.62 m/s², variable mass with Isp 285-460 s, throttle floor 10-30% of a 1.5-3× hover maximum, ±6° gimbal or pitch-rate command, initial states drawn from the 500 m / −15 m/s / 30 m/s SPLICE gate down to the 30 m / 1 m/s terminal), keep actions symmetric in [−1, 1] and observations O(1) as SB3 advises, copy the potential-difference shaping with a ±100 terminal gated on the SPLICE touchdown box, and sample gravity, mass, thrust, Isp and sensor noise from `self.np_random` in `reset()` so domain randomisation is seed-reproducible; curriculum is a `BaseCallback` calling `training_env.env_method("set_difficulty", level)` as SB3's `set_mu` example does ([SB3 RL tips](https://stable-baselines3.readthedocs.io/en/master/guide/rl_tips.html); [SB3 vec_envs](https://stable-baselines3.readthedocs.io/en/master/guide/vec_envs.html)). No published Gymnasium lunar terminal-descent environment with variable mass and throttle limits exists; this is a synthesis to be written, and its outputs (touchdown position, velocity, attitude, propellant, time below 30 m as the plume-exposure proxy) are what the DES samples per landing.

The behavioural-cloning driver is the PilotNet recipe with a lunar twist. NVIDIA's DAVE-2 is nine layers (normaliser, five convolutions of 5×5 stride 2 ×3 then 3×3 ×2, three fully connected) with "about 27 million connections and 250 thousand parameters", trained on ~72 h of driving with shift and rotation augmentation whose labels are adjusted to return to lane centre within two seconds ([NVIDIA blog](https://developer.nvidia.com/blog/deep-learning-self-driving-cars/); [Bojarski et al. 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.07316)). The widely used Udacity implementation realises it as 24/36/48/64/64 filters, Dense 100/50/10/1 with ELU, 66 × 200 YUV input, dropout 0.5, Adam 1e-4, batch 40, 20,000 samples per epoch for 5-10 epochs, **252,219 parameters**, with left/centre/right camera choice (±0.2 steering), flips, translations, shadows and brightness jitter ([naokishibuya repo](https://github.com/naokishibuya/car-behavioral-cloning)). In Keras 3.15 the same stack with `Rescaling` and `Cropping2D` layers exports directly via `model.export(path, format="onnx", opset_version=17)` for `onnxruntime` and `onnxruntime-web`; the PyTorch equivalent flattens to 64 × 1 × 18 and uses `torch.onnx.export` ([Keras export](https://keras.io/api/models/model_saving_apis/export/)). At ~1 MB of weights and ~27 M multiply-adds per frame the model runs well above the 10-30 Hz a browser rover needs on the WASM provider, and a 4 GB GPU is not binding at batch 40 with 66 × 200 inputs; the bottleneck is CPU image decoding, so pre-resized caches and `DataLoader` workers matter more than VRAM. The lunar twist is to replace camera pixels with a body-aligned local heightmap patch (32² or 64² of height, slope and roughness channels plus speed and heading-error scalars) sampled from the same LOLA 5 m DEM the browser already holds, with expert labels from a scripted pure-pursuit or A* follower rather than human driving; the dataset becomes unlimited, DAgger relabelling is cheap, the Python and JavaScript physics stay identical, and sun-azimuth and shadow-fraction channels can encode the lighting penalties the JSC study measured. No source implements this exact variant, so it is an inference and should be presented as a design choice, with the camera-based PilotNet on gym-donkeycar retained as the baseline that ties to the tutorial.

On the RTX 3050 4 GB laptop the simulator landscape is narrow. NVIDIA Isaac Sim, which OmniLRS V2.5 requires, lists a **minimum of an RTX 4080 with 16 GB VRAM and 32 GB RAM**, and warns that "GPUs with less than 16GB VRAM may be insufficient" ([Isaac Sim requirements](https://docs.isaacsim.omniverse.nvidia.com/latest/installation/requirements.html); [OmniLRS](https://github.com/AntoineRichard/OmniLRS)). The NASA/JHU APL Lunar Autonomy Challenge simulator (Unreal Engine plus CARLA, IPEx digital twin, eight cameras) specifies Ubuntu 20.04/22.04 only, an RTX 3000-series with 16 GB VRAM, 32 GB RAM and Python 3.8-3.10, and is "made available to selected teams upon acceptance" at US institutions, so it is excluded by access, OS and VRAM ([LAC documentation](https://lunar-autonomy-challenge.jhuapl.edu/Challenge-Documentation/index.php); [arXiv 2603.17232](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.17232)). PUTvision's LunarSim (a name collision with this project that should be checked) is a Linux Unity binary in Docker with ROS 2 Humble and unstated hardware needs, feasible on Windows only via WSL2 GPU passthrough as an experiment ([PUTvision/LunarSim](https://github.com/PUTvision/LunarSim)). The Udacity simulator was archived read-only on 24 Feb 2022 with 2017 binaries and a Python 3.5 / TensorFlow 1.1 toolchain ([udacity/self-driving-car-sim](https://github.com/udacity/self-driving-car-sim)). The one maintained, Windows-native, Gymnasium-compatible option is gym-donkeycar ("Race Edition v25.10.06", `donkey_sim.exe`, (120, 160, 3) observations, `[steer, throttle]` actions, install from git because the PyPI package is from 2019), whose tracks are roads rather than regolith but which runs on modest GPUs and gives the camera-based BC baseline ([tawnkramer/gym-donkeycar](https://github.com/tawnkramer/gym-donkeycar); [releases](https://github.com/tawnkramer/gym-donkeycar/releases)). For lunar-specific driving the pragmatic route on this hardware is therefore the self-authored 2.5-D heightmap rover environment in Gymnasium mirrored in JavaScript, which no external simulator offers.

Two methodological commitments follow from this list. The paper must separate what the learned models produce from what the DES assumes: landing dispersion, propellant and speed/slip/energy are model outputs validated against the measured anchors above, while plume keep-out radius, stand-down duration and the ejecta-mass scaling for landers above 40 t are swept parameters whose uncertainty NASA itself acknowledges. And every number on the site and in the paper should carry the Measured / Spec / Modelled / Assumed flag used in this report, because the single most common failure in the sources reviewed was a design assumption (2 km standoff, 200 m pad, 1 m/s touchdown) being repeated until it read as a measurement.

## Conclusion

The research reframes Phase 3 from "add two tutorial models" to "close a number NASA left blank". NASA's own architecture documents supply every input band a dispersion-and-standoff-to-cost law needs and explicitly decline to quantify the output; the plume literature has replaced distance with line-of-sight blocking and admits its standoffs are placeholders; the autonomy literature measures speed under latency but never interventions per kilometre; and the learned-controller literature stops at touchdown state and tracking error. Wiring learned landers and rovers into LunarSim as stochastic generators is therefore not a feature but the mechanism that makes the law computable, and the two most likely shapes of the result are themselves findings: either delivery cost depends smoothly on dispersion through traverse distance, or, more plausibly given NASA's 50 m to 5 km relocation bands, it depends on dispersion through pad mass (quadratic in radius) and tail-risk events while the smooth part is dominated by keep-out radius and autonomy level. Either outcome is new, auditable against the documents that leave the cost blank, and robust to the PSI uncertainty if the keep-out radius is swept rather than predicted.

The implementation choices all point the same way: keep the physics identical in Python and the browser (JSON weights and a hand-written forward pass for the 5k-parameter lander, ONNX for the rover, LOLA 5 m heightmaps shared by trainer, DES and renderer), keep provenance immutable (commit, patch hash, scenario hash, named seeds, weights hash, output hash, Zenodo DOI), and keep the hosting small and fixed-cost. The three things that would most strengthen the paper before submission are reading Grogan & de Weck 2012 and the ADD Rev B landing-radius objective, obtaining Hu et al.'s slip-slope data and Lane & Metzger's ballistic tables rather than reading points off text, and measuring, not estimating, the PPO and PilotNet training times on the actual laptop so the reproducibility claims are themselves reproducible.
