5. Results

All numbers in this section come from experiment E21 (Section 4): 2,560 simulated years, 20 seeds per cell, the same seeds in every cell. Differences between cells are paired by seed and carry Student-t 95 % intervals; the coverage law and the break-evens carry bootstrap intervals over seeds (400 draws). Unless a sentence names another fleet, it refers to the reference fleet of Phase 2: six rovers, the marketplace job rule, pads 1 km from the base, the link criterion “any part of Earth's disc” and the 24 h deferral limit. The plume keep-out of v3 is off in E21, so that the link effect is seen alone.

5.1 Where Earth can be seen

Figure 1 puts the link fractions of Table 2 on the map. Four sites on the crest of the ridge (BASE, POWER, CONSTR and DEPOT) see Earth about half the time. The oxygen pilot plant (ISRU) sits in a hollow east of the base and never sees it in 2028–2031; the science site SCI_1 and the shadowed crater site PSR see it 8–12 % of the time, and only in the weeks of largest libration. A route is linked only while every 100 m sample on it sees Earth, so every route to ISRU, SCI_1 or PSR is dark or nearly so, and so is the route to pad B, which leaves the ridge.

5.2 Throughput: a reachability loss

With direct links only, the reference fleet delivers {{R1-T-DTE}} t of cargo in the year against {{R1-T-ALWAYS}} t when Earth is always in view, a paired change of {{R1-T-LOSS-PCT}} (Table 7, Figure 2). Every fleet that needs a person for some of its driving loses about the same amount: the teleoperated fleet, the Phase-2 mix, and supervised rovers under the strict policy or the look-ahead policy. A fully autonomous fleet loses nothing.

The loss is almost all one destination. The demand model makes {{R1-ISRU-BOUND-T}} of cargo a year ready for the ISRU plant, mostly regolith feedstock and plant equipment. Under direct links none of it is delivered: {{R1-LOST-ISRU-T}} is lost there, which is {{R1-LOSS-SHARE-ISRU}} of the total gap of {{R1-GAP-T}} (computed on the strict-policy fleet; the reference fleet's loss is the same within its interval, Table 7). The remaining {{R1-GAP-ELSE-T}} is cargo for linked sites that is still under way at the end of the year, because rovers wait for Earth on their way to it.

So the loss is not a shortage of rovers. One extra supervised rover adds {{R1-ROVER-GAIN-T}} a year under direct links, and a 10 m mast at the base adds {{R1-MAST-GAIN-T}}: neither interval excludes zero. The mast does link the inner routes, but the dark routes are the far ones. Any relay restores the cargo: at a coverage of 0.1 the fleet recovers {{R1-RELAY01-RESTORE-PCT}} of the lost tonnage, and the throughput knee of Stage A is at {{R1-A-KNEE-P}}, the first coverage tested.

5.3 Urgent delay: what coverage buys

Throughput saturates at the first relay level; urgent delay does not. Figure 3 shows the time within which 90 % of urgent items are delivered against relay coverage. With direct links only, the reference fleet's value rises from {{R1-P90-ALWAYS}} to {{R1-P90-DTE}}. Coverage brings it down steadily rather than at a knee, and how fast depends on how much the rovers need a person. Table 8 reads, for each fleet, the coverage at which the urgent delay falls to twice and to one and a half times its full-coverage value. Supervised rovers under the strict policy need a coverage of {{R1-P-2X-STRICT}} for the first target; under the strategic policy they need {{R1-P-2X-STRATEGIC}}. Hyperbolic and power laws fit the cells about twice as well as an exponential one (cross-validated over seeds), but no smooth form follows the slow decline between 0.2 and 1 closely enough to be inverted, so the targets in Table 8 are read from the cells.

5.4 Rovers, a mast or autonomy, priced in relay coverage

Stage B asks what else could stand in for coverage. At a relay coverage of 0.5, where Figure 3 is still falling, Table 9 reads each option's urgent delay off the strict-policy curve as an equivalent coverage (Figure 4). One extra rover shortens the delay by about a quarter and is worth {{R1-DP-ONE-LTV}} of coverage. A second rover adds nothing more, and a third gives most of the gain back, probably because operator demand grows with the fleet (the peak of linked demand rises from 1.3 to 1.6 operators). The strategic step, letting supervised rovers drive on their own between interventions, is worth more than a rover on its own, and the strategic step with one extra rover comes close to full coverage.

Priced with the capital cost of one rover in our engine (45 M$ a year, from v3's Assumed vehicle costs), 0.1 of relay coverage at this point is worth {{R1-MUSD-PER-01}} a year. The interval is wide because the rover's effect is: in {{R1-SHARE-NONPOS}} of the bootstrap draws one rover bought no delay at all. We report it as an order of magnitude for comparing a relay service with fleet purchases, not as a price.

5.5 Driving without a link: the stranding problem

The strategic policy is only as good as the worst point on its route. Under direct links it delivers {{R1-T-STRATEGIC-DTE}}, more than the strict fleet because it passes unlinked stretches, but its rovers spend {{R1-WAIT-STRATEGIC-DTE}} a year stopped at an intervention where Earth will not rise for weeks, against {{R1-WAIT-STRICT-DTE}} under the strict policy. The spread between seeds is large, because one hazard drawn on a rarely linked stretch can hold a rover for most of the year. Checking every sample of the route before committing, at the time the rover would pass it, removes the stranding ({{R1-WAIT-AVOID-DTE}}), but then the fleet refuses the same dark routes as the strict one and delivers the same tonnage. With any relay the two strategic policies are nearly equal, and both well ahead of the strict policy in urgent delay (Table 8).

The deferral limit matters just as much. When a job whose link is more than a day away is committed anyway, the rover parks with its load until the link returns, and the urgent delay of the reference fleet under direct links rises from {{R1-DEFER24-P90}} to {{R1-NODEFER-P90}} (Table 10).

5.6 Operator demand comes in bursts

When links come and go, the rovers that need an operator all need one at the next link-up. For the teleoperated fleet the peak of linked operator demand divided by its mean rises from {{R1-PTM-FULL}} with a relay always in view to {{R1-PTM-DTE}} under direct links only (Figure 5). We do not claim from E21 that the three operators on duty are overrun: the admission rule of the engine counts only demand that is linked at the moment, so a job can start while another rover is between linked stretches, and the peak exceeds the staff slightly even at full coverage. A version with a hard operator limit is in preparation; the bunching itself does not depend on that rule.

5.7 Sensitivity

Table 10 collects Stage C. Throughput hardly depends on the link criterion: requiring the centre of the disc 2° above the horizon instead of any part of it changes the delivered tonnage by at most {{R1-CRIT-RANGE}}. Urgent delay depends on it more, as expected from shorter windows. Pad standoff matters under direct links, because pads farther from the ridge see less of Earth: pads at 0.5 km deliver {{R1-PADS-05-GAIN}} more a year than pads at 1 km, and pads at 3 km {{R1-PADS-3-LOSS}} less. With a relay of coverage 0.8 the standoff has no measurable effect on tonnage. This adds a second cost to pad distance besides the travel cost measured in v3.
