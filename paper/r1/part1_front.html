What Earth's Horizon Costs a Lunar Base: Terrain-Masked Communication Windows in South-Pole Cargo Logistics

Azizjon Rahimov
Roatify Research · Preprint, October 2026 · Code, data and link windows: github.com/azizjonrahimov/Lunar-Haul · Live site: azizjonrahimov.github.io/Lunar-Haul

Abstract. At the lunar south pole Earth stays within about 7° of the horizon and is below it for roughly half of each month; terrain hides it for longer. A rover driven from Earth cannot move without a direct-to-Earth (DTE) link, and a supervised rover needs one whenever an operator has to step in. Site visibility fractions, relay coverage and single-rover planning around link gaps are published; what the gaps cost a cargo fleet is not. We computed terrain-masked Earth-link windows for 2028–2031 at our base on the Connecting Ridge, from SPICE ephemerides and LOLA terrain out to 300 km, at every site and pad and every 100 m along 182 rover routes. The ephemeris agrees with JPL Horizons to 10−6 degrees, and computed the published map's way our fractions match the 60 m LOLA Earth-visibility map to −0.004 on average over 17 points. A rover antenna at the base sees Earth 59% of the time, but 101 of the 182 routes are never in view of Earth from end to end, and the oxygen pilot plant never sees it. We added the windows to our verified discrete-event cargo simulation and ran 2,560 simulated years (experiment E21): DTE only and a relay of coverage 0.1–1.0 against six autonomy mixes; extra rovers, a 10 m base mast and supervised policies; and pad standoff, link criterion and a deferral limit. With DTE only the reference fleet delivers {{R1-T-DTE}} t a year against {{R1-T-ALWAYS}} t ({{R1-T-LOSS-PCT}}), {{R1-LOSS-SHARE-ISRU}} of the loss being cargo for the oxygen plant, and the 90th-percentile urgent delay rises from {{R1-P90-ALWAYS}} to {{R1-P90-DTE}}. A relay in view 10% of the time restores {{R1-RELAY01-RESTORE-PCT}} of the lost cargo; an extra rover adds {{R1-ROVER-GAIN-T}} a year and the mast {{R1-MAST-GAIN-T}}. In urgent delay one extra supervised rover is worth {{R1-DP-ONE-LTV}} of relay coverage, which prices 0.1 of coverage at {{R1-MUSD-PER-01}} a year. Under DTE only, operator demand in the teleoperated fleet comes in bursts: its peak is {{R1-PTM-DTE}} times its mean, against {{R1-PTM-FULL}} with full relay coverage. Code, data and the link windows are released.

1. Introduction

A base at the lunar south pole sees Earth low on the horizon. Over a month Earth wanders about ±6.9° in latitude and ±8.2° in longitude in the lunar sky [1]; De Rosa and colleagues put its centre within about ±6.5° of elevation and ±8° of azimuth as seen from polar sites [2]. At our base on the Connecting Ridge, Earth's centre moved between −7.54° and +6.23° of elevation over 2028–2031, inside a wedge of azimuth 16° wide (Modelled; Section 3.2). Its apparent disc is about 2° across. For about two weeks of every month it is below the horizon [3, 4]. Terrain makes this worse. A site below a ridge or on a slope facing away from Earth loses it earlier and regains it later, and some sites never see it at all.

This matters because of how surface rovers are expected to be driven. NASA's trials with a Lunar Terrain Vehicle measured how fast operators on Earth can drive at 0–8 s of round-trip delay, and assumed “flawless high-bandwidth comms” while doing so [5]. A teleoperated rover needs the link for every command. A supervised rover drives itself between approvals but stops at a hazard it cannot resolve, and an operator on Earth has to answer before it moves on. VIPER's planners treated the line of sight to the ground stations as a moving “radio shadow” and timed traverses so as not to be caught by it [6]. Otten, Wettergreen and Whittaker showed that requiring continuous Earth contact while driving forces a polar rover to “dwell motionless near the day–night terminator for weeks”, and proposed short, preplanned autonomous drives without communication [7]. De Rosa and colleagues concluded that windows of about 14 days “demand a relatively high level of autonomy” [2]. For Artemis III, NASA required direct communication with Earth assuming no relay is available, and screened out areas where Earth is visible less than 25% of the time (Spec) [8]; its 2026 site update prefers regions with greater Earth visibility if the mission uses a DTE architecture [9].

There are four ways to buy the link back. The first is a relay in lunar orbit. NASA's Lunar Communications Relay and Navigation Systems (LCRNS) project states its case as coverage: DTE alone gives 17–43% yearly coverage at candidate south-pole sites, DTE plus one relay 80–86%, and DTE plus two relays 100% (Modelled) [10]. The relay services contract has a maximum potential value of $4.82 billion (Spec) [11]. The second is more autonomy, which is Otten's answer [7]. The third is more rovers, which add capacity but wait for the same link. The fourth is a mast at the base that sees Earth and relays to rovers within its line of sight; NASA Glenn's surface relay concept uses a 10 m boom and expects coverage “out to 10 km, depending on local terrain” [3]. Each costs money, and each is limited by terrain in a different way.

Part of this is known. Earth-visibility fractions at named polar sites have been computed from radar and laser terrain [1, 2, 12]. Windows of about 14 days are published [2]. Relay coverage is published [10]. Traverse planning for a single rover around link gaps is published [6, 7, 13]. What we did not find, in about 35 searches and 25 full-text reads in October 2026 (Section 2.5), is a model that takes terrain-masked link windows along real routes and asks what they do to a fleet that has to move cargo: how much it delivers, how late urgent items arrive, and which of the four fixes buys the most. Nor did we find a public price for relay service per hour or per year (Section 2.2), so a break-even has to be stated as an equivalence between options rather than as a dollar verdict.

Our previous paper [14] built a discrete-event testbed of cargo movement at a south-pole base on NASA's 5 m LOLA terrain of the Connecting Ridge, with rover routes and driving tables by autonomy level, and priced landing precision, pad standoff, a plume keep-out and rover autonomy. Like the JSC trials, it assumed the link to Earth is always there. At our layout that assumption fails badly. A rover antenna 2.5 m above the base sees Earth 59% of the time, but the oxygen pilot plant, 1.2 km from the base and 139 m below it, never does, and 101 of the 182 routes between our sites and pads are never in view of Earth from end to end (Section 3.5). This paper asks what that costs and what fixes it, for this one base. We ask four questions, numbered for this paper:

  1. RQ1. How often, and in what windows, does each site, pad and route of a real terrain-masked base have a DTE link in 2028–2031, from a rover antenna and with a 10 m mast at the base, under three link criteria, and how does that compare with the published Earth-visibility map?
  2. RQ2. How much cargo throughput and urgent-delivery delay does the fleet lose to DTE gaps, for teleoperated, supervised and autonomous fleets, and how do they recover as relay coverage rises?
  3. RQ3. What are the break-evens between relay coverage, an autonomy step, extra rovers and a mast at the base, and what relay price per year do they imply?
  4. RQ4. Do link windows bunch operator demand into the hours when Earth is in view, and how sensitive are the answers to pad standoff, to the link criterion and to the rule for deferring jobs that would wait long for a link?

We answer RQ1 with data and RQ2–RQ4 with simulation. Earth's direction comes from NASA's SPICE toolkit every 10 minutes for four years. Horizon masks in Earth's direction come from the 5 m terrain tile out to 8 km and from NASA's 80 m polar terrain out to 300 km. The link windows enter the testbed of [14] as an opt-in rule: a teleoperated rover drives only while its whole route is in view, a supervised rover either does the same or waits for a link only when it needs an intervention, an autonomous rover is not affected, and a relay or mast adds link time. Experiment E21 then crosses the link regime with the autonomy mix, adds rovers, a mast and supervised policies, and varies pad standoff, link criterion and the deferral rule, over 20 seeds with common random numbers. Our contributions are:

  • Link windows for a real base layout. Run-length windows at 10-minute resolution for 2028–2031 at 7 sites, 10 pads and 2,404 route cells along 182 legs, under three criteria, with and without a base mast, computed from SPICE and LOLA terrain to 300 km and checked against JPL Horizons and the published LOLA Earth-visibility map. The file is 383 KB with its provenance. We found no published dataset that gives windows along routes.
  • An opt-in Earth-link rule in a verified logistics engine. Rules for teleoperation, three supervised policies and autonomy; a relay; a mast; operator demand booked only while linked; and a deferral rule for jobs that would wait long for a link. With the rule off, or with the link always up, the engine's output is byte-identical to the previous version's.
  • The cost of link gaps for a cargo fleet, and the break-evens. Throughput and urgent delay by autonomy mix and relay coverage, a fitted delay–coverage law with bootstrap intervals, the relay coverage that one extra rover, one autonomy step or a mast is worth, the relay price per year that this implies, the peaks of linked operator demand, and their sensitivity to pad standoff, link criterion and deferral.

We do not claim a radio link budget. Our criteria are geometric (any part of Earth's disc above the terrain horizon, or its centre 0.5° or 2° above it); we model no diffraction, no Fresnel-zone loss near grazing, and no scheduling of ground stations. The relay is a parametric on–off process with a stated coverage and mean gap, taken to see every route point (Assumed); we did not propagate the LCRNS orbits or mask them by terrain. Crewed periods, when astronauts at the base could drive rovers locally, are not modelled. The engine's lunar night is the synodic model of [14] with a dark fraction of 0.15, not the real Sun at each site, so E21 does not test how Earth windows and sunlit periods overlap. Supervised intervention rates are the Assumed hazard model of [14]; no measured interventions-per-kilometre figure exists for any lunar rover. The relay price we report is the price at which a relay equals extra rovers in our model, not a market price. One site, one terrain tile and one base layout are simulated. Every physical number carries a flag at first appearance, as in [14]: Measured (flight, laboratory or survey data), Spec (a published design value or requirement), Modelled (produced by a model, including ours) or Assumed (our choice, swept where it matters). Link fractions and windows are Modelled: we computed them from measured terrain and a fitted ephemeris.
