6. Discussion

In this layout, one site that never sees Earth accounts for {{R1-LOSS-SHARE-NEVER}} of the tonnage lost under DTE, and that tonnage is the feedstock haul of our demand model. The oxygen plant was placed in [14] for its regolith and its distance from the pads, and nothing in that placement looked at Earth. A site that never sees Earth can be served only by autonomous rovers, by rovers that drive without a link, or through a relay. The result that does not depend on the feedstock assumption is the cargo from landers: under DTE a fleet that needs the link loses {{R1-LANDED-LOSS-PCT-PLAIN}} of it with pads at 1 km and all of it with pads at 3 km, while pads at 0.5 km keep {{R1-C-LANDED-05}} a year moving. Earth visibility along routes, not only at sites, therefore belongs in base layout next to slope, illumination and plume distance. Farther pads cost travel time [14] and, under DTE, link time as well.

For this lightly used fleet, and with a relay assumed to see every route point, a relay in view {{R1-P-LOW-PCT}} of the time restores {{R1-RESTORE-LOW-STRICT}} of the lost tonnage; urgent delivery needs far more coverage. Tonnage turns on whether a route can be driven at all, urgent delay on how often. Under the strict policy the fleet needs coverage {{R1-P-2X-STRICT}} to come within twice its full-coverage delay. NASA's LCRNS figures of 80–86 % a year for DTE plus one relay [10] are combined coverage. At a site that DTE links about half the time they correspond to a relay alone at p ≈ 0.6–0.7 (Sections 2.2 and 3.7), where the strict fleet's urgent delay is {{R1-LCRNS-RATIO}} times its full-coverage value: at or just inside our 2× target, not on the flat part of the curve. Two qualifications apply. The oxygen plant sits in a hollow 139 m below the base, and a relay masked by terrain there would restore less; we modelled no relay masks. And with the real Sun the urgent delay at coverage 0.5 is several times longer than with the fixed night share (Section 5.8), so the coverage targets of Table 7 are optimistic.

Under DTE only, letting supervised rovers drive between interventions recovers nearly all the tonnage and most urgent deliveries if a helper rover comes to any rover held longer than a day; with some relay coverage it shortens the delay of the items delivered, but not significantly the censored delay. This supports Otten and colleagues' case for short preplanned drives without contact [7] where Earth returns, and qualifies it where it does not. On the {{R1-F-NEVER-LEGS}} main-network legs that hold a never-linked point, the value of the rule depends on the help a stopped rover gets. Help within a week is not significantly different from the model's free second chance: the strategic fleet then delivers most urgent items, and most of the tonnage in {{R1-STRAT-GOOD-YEARS}} start years. Help after a month keeps the urgent gain but not the tonnage. With no help the fleet delivers {{R1-F-STRAND-T}} a year, less than a fleet that drives only when linked. Stages G and G2 leave out the helper's own time; Stage H includes it. The fourth start year shows a second risk of the rule. A rover that waits wherever an intervention stops it can be held for more than a year by a single route point, and in 2028 one point on the haul from the oxygen plant, dark until {{R1-SY-FIRST-LINK}}, captured nearly the whole fleet (Section 5.4). Such years recur about every six years: without help the fleet collapses in {{R1-J-DARK-N}} of the {{R1-J-N-YEARS}} start years from 2032 to 2046. Help for any rover held longer than a week removes that risk as well: the fleet then delivers within 5 % of its always-linked tonnage in every start year from 2028 to 2031, but needs help {{R1-G2-RESCUES-168}} times a run. One real helper does the same if it is called after a day, though not after a week, and from 2032 to 2046 it stays within 5 % in every start year, where the ideal help after a week does so in {{R1-J-G2-OK}} of {{R1-J-N-YEARS}}. This fits the relay result: a relay at coverage {{R1-P-LOW}} ends most waits within hours, and it too restores the tonnage. The policy that looks ahead along its route avoids the long waits but, under DTE, also avoids the routes it was meant to open. The two policies looked like the ends of a range, with a middle rule that avoids only points whose next window is far away. Stage F tested that rule: no bound kept the oxygen plant's haul and avoided the 2028 collapse, and keeping the haul exposed a dispatch effect, deferred items filling the auction pool. The design question is therefore not how long a rover may wait, but how a held rover is helped. Parking points in line of sight exist near every held point, and one helper called after a day keeps the fleet within 5 % of its always-linked tonnage, far more than one extra hauler buys. No helper on the ground escapes Earth's monthly cycle, though: {{R1-PK-UNCOV}} of calls wait more than a week for Earth at every nearby point, so a helper mostly waits for Earth in a better place, and only a relay above the terrain could end those waits. For a fleet busy about {{R1-UTIL-ALWAYS}} of the year, one extra rover did not change urgent delay significantly at coverage 0.2, 0.5 or 0.8, and the gain that two rovers show at 0.5 is gone with three. One rover's equivalent coverage ({{R1-DP-ONE-LTV}}) and the price it implies ({{R1-MUSD-PER-01}} per 0.1 of coverage a year, at coverage 0.5) are weakly determined, and the difference between the autonomy step and a rover ({{R1-DP-DIFF}}) is not significant. So “autonomy before rovers” holds here only in the sense that extra rovers bought nothing measurable. The price compares a relay with one base's fleet, whereas a relay serves every user in its footprint, and it leaves out the operators and energy of the rover.

With teleoperated rovers and a soft operator pool, link windows push the hourly peak of linked operator demand above the operators on duty in most seeds; a hard limit keeps it at the staff level at a small cost. Under DTE the teleoperated fleet's peak is {{R1-OP-PEAK-TELEOP-DTE}} operators against three on duty ({{R1-OP-SEEDS-TELEOP-DTE}} of 20 seeds), and {{R1-OP-PEAK-TELEOP-MAX}} at relay coverage {{R1-OP-PEAK-TELEOP-MAXP}}. With a hard limit the same fleet waits at most {{R1-A2-WAIT-MAX}} vehicle-hours a year for an operator, with no measurable loss of tonnage or urgent completion. The reference mix, with two teleoperated vehicles, stays within its three operators in every seed. Staffing for a teleoperated fleet should follow the peaks in the link windows, not the yearly mean; with this demand, a hard limit on operators costs little.

7. Limitations

  • Geometry, not radio. A link is up when Earth's disc, or its centre with a margin, is above the terrain horizon. There is no link budget, no loss when terrain enters the Fresnel zone near grazing, and no ground-station scheduling. We expect a real link to fall between the any-part and the centre criteria, because a ground station can sit anywhere on Earth's disc and 0.5° above the horizon clears the first Fresnel zone beyond about 0.5 km (Section 3.4); multipath off terrain at 0–6° elevation could make it worse than either. Stage C shows that throughput does not depend on the criterion and that the censored urgent delay is not shorter under the stricter ones. LCRNS reports 17–43 % DTE coverage at candidate sites [10], less than our base sees (Table 2), so its definition probably includes more than geometry; we did not compute a link budget for a 2.5 m rover antenna and an 18 m ground station.
  • The relay is parametric (Assumed): an on–off process of the stated coverage, independent of DTE, that sees every route point. Stage E shows that its gap pattern changes the delay curve at low coverage, and that a 30 h periodic relay at coverage 0.1 costs the teleoperated fleet tonnage. Real relays may be masked by terrain at low elevation, for instance at the oxygen plant in its hollow; we ran no relay masks and no reduced availability there.
  • Night. Stages A–C and E keep the fixed night share of [14], which starts at hour 0 of every run whatever the start year. Stage D shows that the real Sun lengthens urgent delay several times over at relay coverage 0.5 while leaving throughput unchanged. We did not repeat the coverage targets or the break-evens with the real Sun.
  • Operators. The main stages use the soft operator pool, whose admission check sees only linked demand (Table 4). Stage A2 bounds its effect for the teleoperated and reference mixes; the all-supervised fleets peak well below their staff.
  • Censoring. The engine books a delivery and its delay at pickup. We report the completion share and a censored 90th percentile beside every delay; cell means of the censored measure are dominated by a few seeds, so we compare medians and geometric-mean ratios.
  • Calendar and statistics. Each cell has 20 seeds over four start years, so every interval includes the calendar variation of 2028–2031 and rests on four calendar draws; the year-blocked intervals rest on four values. These four years are not typical for the strategic fleet: without help it collapses in one of them, but in {{R1-J-DARK-N}} of the {{R1-J-N-YEARS}} start years from 2032 to 2046 (Stage J). Of the {{R1-N-EFFECTS}} paired effects, six comparisons are primary, and no interval is adjusted for multiplicity. The coverage targets carry {{R1-LAW-BOOT}}-draw bootstrap intervals.
  • Routes and interventions. The slope-only route stands for every lighting case, and pad-to-pad legs are inferred from their end points. The link windows use one of several equally short routes per leg: the route search breaks ties between equal-cost paths one way for the link windows and another for the driving tables, and the two differ by more than 20 m on {{R1-TIES-LEGS}} legs (up to {{R1-TIES-MAX-M}} apart). On {{R1-TIES-SHARE-GT5}} legs this moves the share of hours in which the whole route sees Earth by more than 5 percentage points, at most by {{R1-TIES-SHARE-MAX}}, so a single leg's window is partly an arbitrary choice among equal routes. Strategic interventions are placed uniformly along a route, at rates from the Assumed hazard model of [14]; halving and doubling the rate (Stage E) moves the strategic results but not the conclusions. If hazards cluster in the hollows and slopes that are dark, the strategic policy would do worse than shown.
  • The strategic rule's two features. The strategic rule re-draws, at no cost, an intervention that would fall on a never-linked sample, but it waits out any finite wait, however long. The first flatters the rule and the second can hold a rover for more than a year. Stage F brackets the first between a free re-draw and a rover held until the year ends, Stage G puts a fixed delay on help between these ends, and Stage G2 extends that help to every long wait (Section 5.4). Stage H adds the helper's travel, its own link and a session, but its driving needs no link, operators foresee every wait, line of sight rests on the 5 m terrain (the best point near the 2028 dark sample clears it by {{R1-PK-188-CLEAR}}), and calls at points outside the searched set get no helper ({{R1-H-UNSERVED}} a run).
  • The auction pool. Each dispatch call auctions only the {{R1-F-POOL-CAP}} most urgent, then oldest, waiting items, a limit of the simulation that keeps each call fast under heavy backlogs, and items that no free rover may take keep their places. Under the bounded rule that leaves never-linked samples to the re-draw, deferred items filled the pool in 2028 (Section 5.4); we did not test a pool that skips them. The strict and look-ahead fleets do not filter such items before the limit either, but in a daily check of one seed per start year their pool was never full and blocked items held {{R1-F-POOL-STRICT}} of its places, so the limit did not bind for them.
  • Choices made during the study. The 24 h deferral limit was set from development runs; Stage C shows that 12 and 72 h and no limit are indistinguishable under DTE in completion and censored delay, and that a long limit costs a little completion at coverage 0.8. Stage B's coverage of 0.5 was fixed after Stage A; Stage B repeats the strict and strategic policies with and without one extra rover at 0.2 and 0.8.
  • The link file's end. A wait that runs past a sample's last link-up in the 2028–2031 windows counts as never linked, so near the end of the 2031 runs, and for a few rarely linked samples much earlier, the strategic rule re-draws where it would otherwise wait. Rerunning the six most-quoted cells with windows that continue to 2050 leaves every 2028–2030 run unchanged and changes {{R1-EDGE-MOVED}} of the {{R1-EDGE-OF}} values the paper quoted at the time in their printed digits, none in sign or in whether an interval excludes zero. The largest moves are the strategic fleet's 2031 tonnage ({{R1-EDGE-STRAT-2031}}; over all start years {{R1-EDGE-STRAT-T}}) and its re-draws per run ({{R1-EDGE-REDRAWS}}). We report the 2028–2031 windows as run.
  • The never-linked filter. When every vehicle needs a link, items whose delivery leg is never linked leave the candidate list (Table 4). The throughput results depend on this engine choice; without it, dispatch would keep assigning jobs that cannot start.
  • One site, one fleet, one demand. All results are for the Connecting Ridge layout of [14], the Phase 2 demand of [33], in which bulk regolith is about 500 of the roughly 530 t made ready a year, and one six-vehicle fleet busy about {{R1-UTIL-ALWAYS}} of the year. We tested no busy fleet, no other antenna height than 2.5 m, and the plume keep-out in two cells only. The rover's cost is the capital cost Modelled in [33], without operators or energy. Crewed periods, when astronauts could drive locally, are not modelled.

8. Conclusions

We computed when Earth can be seen along every route of a south-pole base on real terrain and put those windows into a cargo simulation checked against analytic and earlier results. For this layout, the Phase 2 demand and a lightly used six-vehicle fleet, three findings hold. First, with DTE only a fleet whose rovers need the link to drive delivers {{R1-T-LOSS-PCT-PLAIN}} less cargo a year. Most of that is the regolith we assume is hauled to an oxygen plant that never sees Earth, but cargo from landers also falls by {{R1-LANDED-LOSS-PCT-PLAIN}}, and half or fewer of the urgent items arrive within the year. Second, a relay assumed to see every route point restores the tonnage from the lowest coverage tested, while urgent delay keeps falling up to full coverage; extra rovers and a 10 m mast restore none of the tonnage and little of the urgent completion. Third, in exploratory stages, letting supervised rovers drive between interventions helps under DTE as far as a held rover gets help. If one helper rover comes to any rover held longer than a day and parks in its line of sight on ground that sees Earth more often, the fleet delivers within 5 % of its always-linked tonnage in every start year; the helper spends most of its time waiting for Earth, because no nearby ground escapes Earth's monthly cycle. If help comes only where Earth is never in view, one route point that loses Earth for {{R1-J-SPELL-LEN}} about every six years collapses the fleet in 2028 and in {{R1-J-DARK-N}} of the {{R1-J-N-YEARS}} start years from 2032 to 2046; without help the fleet delivers {{R1-F-STRAND-T}} a year. With a relay it shortens delivered delays, and whether it is worth more than an extra rover is not established. Three points follow for planning, each under these conditions: check Earth visibility along routes, not only at sites, when placing work sites and pads, and plan a helper rover and its parking points for any rover held out of Earth's sight; plan relay service against urgent delay rather than throughput, and with the real Sun, which lengthens urgent delay several times over at half coverage; and staff teleoperation for the peaks in the link windows.
