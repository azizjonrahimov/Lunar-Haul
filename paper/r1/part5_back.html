6. Discussion

Where a site is matters as much as how it is served. The largest single number in this paper, the loss of {{R1-LOSS-SHARE-ISRU}} of the gap at one plant, is a siting result. The oxygen plant was placed in v3 [14] for its regolith and its distance from the pads, and nothing in that placement looked at Earth. A site that never sees Earth can only be served by autonomous rovers or through a relay, whatever the fleet. Earth visibility along the routes, not only at the sites, belongs in base layout next to slope, illumination and plume distance. The same applies to the pads: farther pads cost travel time (v3) and, under direct links, link time as well.

A little relay coverage goes a long way for throughput, and a long way is needed for urgent delay. A relay in view a tenth of the time returns the lost cargo, because the lost cargo was a question of whether a route can be driven at all, not of how often. Urgent delay is a question of how often, and there the coverage curve is long: strict supervision needs about {{R1-P-2X-STRICT}} coverage to come within twice the full-coverage delay. NASA's LCRNS reference numbers (one relay 80–86 % a year [10], Section 2) fall on the flat part of our curve, so a single relay would remove most of the effect for this layout.

Autonomy and relays are substitutes, and rovers are not. Letting supervised rovers drive between interventions moved the coverage needed for the same delay from about {{R1-P-2X-STRICT}} to {{R1-P-2X-STRATEGIC}}, and at half coverage the strategic step was worth more relay coverage than an extra rover. This agrees with Otten and colleagues' argument for short preplanned drives without contact [7], and puts a number on it for a fleet. It also shows the condition: the policy must look ahead along its route, or a single hazard on a rarely linked stretch strands a rover for weeks.

Operations planning has to expect bursts. Link windows make operator demand arrive together. Staffing from yearly averages would underestimate the peak, and with teleoperation the bunching is strongest. How much delay the bursts cause with a hard operator limit is the next question.

7. Limitations

  • Geometry, not radio. A link is up when Earth's disc (or its centre with a margin) is above the terrain horizon. There is no link budget, no Fresnel-zone loss near grazing, and no ground-station scheduling. Stage C shows the throughput result does not depend on the criterion; urgent delay does, and a real link budget would sit between our criteria.
  • The relay is parametric (Assumed): an alternating up/gap process of the stated coverage with exponential gaps of mean 7.7 h, taken to see every route point. Real relay passes are correlated in time and may be masked by terrain at low elevation, which would lengthen gaps at the dark sites.
  • Night is not the real Sun. E21 keeps v3's fixed night share. Earth's elevation cycle (27.2 days) and the lunar day (29.5 days) beat with a period of about 347 days, so the overlap of Sun and Earth drifts through the year; testing that needs illumination per site, which is in preparation.
  • Operators. The admission rule counts only linked demand (Section 5.6). The bursts are real in the model; whether they overrun a fixed staff is not settled here.
  • Routes. The slope-only route stands for every lighting case, and pad-to-pad legs are inferred from their end points. Strategic interventions are placed uniformly along a route; intervention rates come from v3's Assumed hazard model.
  • Choices made during the study. The 24 h deferral limit was set from development runs, and Stage B's coverage of 0.5 was fixed after Stage A showed the throughput knee at 0.1. Both are reported with their alternatives (Table 10).
  • One site, one fleet, one phase. All results are for the Connecting Ridge layout, the Phase-2 demand and a six-rover fleet, with v3's Assumed vehicle costs, 20 seeds per cell and the plume keep-out off. Crewed periods, when astronauts could drive locally, are not modelled.

8. Conclusions

We computed when Earth can be seen along every route of a south-pole base on real terrain and put those windows into a verified cargo simulation. With direct links only, a fleet that needs operators on Earth delivers about {{R1-T-LOSS-PCT-PLAIN}} less cargo a year, almost all of it because one work site never sees Earth; more rovers or a mast at the base do not change that, and any relay or full autonomy does. Relay coverage then buys urgent delivery time along a long curve, and an autonomy step that lets supervised rovers drive between interventions is worth more of that curve than an extra rover, provided the rovers look ahead along their routes. For base planners the practical points are three: check Earth visibility along routes when siting work sites and pads; plan relay service against urgent delay, not throughput; and buy autonomy before rovers when links are scarce.
