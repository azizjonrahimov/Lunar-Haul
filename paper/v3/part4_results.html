6. Results

Every number in this section that the testbed produced is Modelled; we flag only the measured and specified anchors it is compared with. Cell values are means over 30 seeds with 95% confidence intervals (1.96 standard errors), and every comparison is a paired difference on common seeds with a percentile seed-bootstrap interval of 1,000 resamples (Section 4.2). Three things changed between the design of Table 5 and the runs, and we state them here rather than in the tables. E17 sweeps the implied mean miss at seven levels anchored to flown and specified landers (the classical table's own 2.9 m; SLIM's 10 m before its divert; the 50 m target of the Architecture Definition Document; the 100 m of NASA's 2023 paper and of the HLS requirement; Blue Ghost's 118 m; IM-2's 300 m; IM-1's 1,500 m), each crossed with a pad radius of 25, 50 or 100 m, so it has 840 cells rather than 280. E18 adds a two-minute stand-down (0.033 h, the ballistic floor of Appendix D) to the four levels of Table 5, so it has 1,248 cells rather than 1,008. And the version-2 findings are not repeated: with the version-3 fields unset the engine reproduces version 2 byte for byte (Section 5.1), so nothing in them changed. What changed is the ground under the base. The reference cell of E17 (Phase-2 default fleet, classical landings, 1 km standoff, 50 m pad, no keep-out) is the version-2 Phase-2 cell on real terrain, and its marketplace mean urgent delay is [[E17: reference-cell P2-default marketplace mean urgent delay with 95% CI]] h against the settled version-2 value of {MKT_MEAN} h on the tangent plane. The difference is the terrain legs: the ridge layout puts every base site closer than version 2 did (0.26–3.96 km against 0.6–5 km) while slopes, slip and confirmation stops make each kilometre slower. Energy was not priced in version 2's cost, so the change from 350 to 134 Wh/km (Section 3.4) does not move the cost per kilogram; it moves only the energy column, which Section 6.7 prices for the first time.

The law. The classical ZEM/ZEV law with its vertical terminal phase meets the SPLICE box in 93.8% of 500 dispersed CLPS episodes (Wilson 95% interval 91.3–95.6%), in 93.0% (90.4–94.9%) for the MK1 class and in 97.0% (95.1–98.2%) for the large class, with the full sensor model on (Table 7, Figure 5). Its misses are small: a mean of 2.9, 2.5 and 2.4 m and a 90th percentile of 4.7, 4.4 and 4.4 m for the three classes. Two CLPS episodes of 500 landed beyond the 50 m delivery radius (the largest miss was 80 m); no MK1 or large episode did. Its failures are of two kinds. Timeouts (11, 16 and 5 of 500) are episodes in which the law, steering a dispersed start to a point 10 m above the target and then down at 1 m/s, has not touched down when the 150 s cap ends. Sensor-attributed near-misses (6, 19 and 9) are landings a little outside the velocity box while the altimeter was wrong or held below 30 m. Velocity failures outside that regime are 14, 0 and 1. The median vertical speed at touchdown is −1.00 m/s for all three classes; propellant from the gate is 89, 298 and 2,297 kg; the time below 30 m is 23–25 s. The law's tables were generated under environment version 1. Versions 2 and 3 changed the reward and the observation, neither of which the law uses, so the tables stand, and the engine samples them in E17–E19.

Where the law sits against the record. A mean miss of 2.9 m and a 90th percentile of 4.7 m describe a nominal vehicle with terrain-relative navigation (the 1.5 m navigation bias is Assumed on the strength of NASA's “below 4 m” study [83]). They lie an order of magnitude inside the 50 m target of the Architecture Definition Document (Spec) [115] and the 100 m HLS requirement (Spec) [82], and two orders inside most of the flown record of 2023–2025 (Measured) [84–87]. That is why E17 does not sweep a multiplier blind. It scales the whole miss distribution so that its mean becomes 10, 50, 100, 118, 300 or 1,500 m, that is σscale 3.4, 17.2, 34.5, 40.7, 103 and 517, and the pad radius of 25, 50 or 100 m decides which scaled misses are off-pad. One caution travels with the mapping. NASA does not say whether its 50 m is a mean, a 1σ, a 3σ or a hard radius. Our 50 m level is a mean, and under it the scaled distribution has a 90th percentile of about 81 m, so about 30% of landings fall outside a 50 m pad (Section 6.3); if NASA's 50 m is a 3σ radius, the corresponding mean lies between our 10 and 50 m levels, and the off-pad share with it.

The learned lander and what it taught us. The ladder is in Table 7, from the law down. Cloning the filtered law reached 75% on 100 evaluation episodes after behavioural cloning and then fell through four DAgger rounds to 69, 60, 51 and 43%; on the 500-episode Monte Carlo the exported clone (lander_CLPS_bc_s0) meets the box in 58.2% (53.8–62.4%), with 133 sensor-attributed near-misses and 49 tip-overs. DAgger is meant to repair covariate shift, not make it worse [99], so we measured what the clone was being asked to learn. For each of 150 expert episodes we took the ten nearest neighbours of every observation (standardised) and computed the variance of the expert's actions among them as a share of the total action variance: a label-inconsistency score that is 0 for an action that is a function of the observation and 1 for noise. The filtered law scores 0.41 (throttle 0.03; the two tilt-rate commands 0.60 and 0.61). The throttle is a function of what the clone sees; the attitude commands are not, because they depend on the law's smoothed velocity, low-pass-filtered direction and held command, which the observation does not carry. A memoryless learner cannot clone a filtered expert, and aggregating more of its labels aggregates more contradiction. We therefore wrote the same ZEM/ZEV and terminal law without filter state: the tilt-rate command is 0.25 times the angle error per step and zero below 3 m (Assumed gain), so the smoothing moves into the vehicle's attitude dynamics. It scores 0.33 and, flown itself, meets the box in 94.8% of 500 episodes (92.5–96.4%), one point above the filtered law. Cloning it with the same 15-component observation gave 80, 74 and 83% over behavioural cloning and two DAgger rounds and 81.2% (77.5–84.4%) on the Monte Carlo; 63 of its 94 failures were sensor near-misses, because from a single observation the clone cannot tell a held altimeter reading from a true one, which the expert's velocity-based terminal law largely ignores. Weighting the terminal-phase samples three times in the loss did not help (76.8%). Giving the clone the expert's information did. Observation version 3 appends the last valid altitude, its age and the two previous readings (four inputs, scaled by 30 m, 2 s, 30 m and 30 m), and the same recipe (800 expert episodes, 25 epochs, two DAgger rounds of 300 episodes, a 128 × 128 tanh network, 655 s on one process) gave 91, 87 and 87% in the 100-episode round evaluations and 89.0% (86.0–91.5%) on the 500-episode Monte Carlo (lander_CLPS_bc_v3_s0): 33 sensor near-misses, 16 velocity failures, 6 tip-overs, no timeouts, a mean miss of 3.2 m, 81 kg of propellant and 20 s below 30 m. The round-0 clone without DAgger scores 87.2% on the same 500 episodes (84.0–89.8%) with 11 timeouts, so DAgger is worth 1.8 points here, inside the interval. The label inconsistency of the memoryless expert's aggregated data stayed at 0.28–0.35 across rounds. The final clone is 4.8 ± 3.5 points below the filtered law and 5.8 points below the expert it imitates. We did not train MK1 or LARGE clones; those classes land by the law in every suite.

Proximal policy optimisation. PPO under the Gaudet-style reward reached the box in 43.6% of 500 episodes (39.3–48.0%) after two million steps under reward version 1 (κ = 10), with 182 sensor-attributed failures; the same recipe under reward version 2 (κ 50, crash −5 per m/s, near-ground gain 3) reaches 63.2% (58.9–67.3%; lander_CLPS_baseline_s0, 2,007,040 steps), 30.6 ± 4.7 points below the law on the same episodes. Its failure pattern is the one Section 3.3 described: it lands fast and lean. The mean vertical speed at touchdown is −1.97 m/s against the 2.0 m/s limit (law −1.23, median −1.00); the 90th-percentile horizontal speed is 1.39 m/s against the 1.0 m/s limit (law 0.15); the mean tilt at touchdown is 4.5° against 6° (law 1.6°). It uses 61 kg of propellant to the law's 89 and spends 9.1 s below 30 m to the law's 23.5. Those are the marks of a policy that has found the edge of the box and the cheapest way over it, and the altimeter noise below 30 m then pushes 110 of 500 landings past the edge. Fine-tuning from a cloned actor made this worse in every run we tried (development: 74% at the start, 51–52% after 300,000 steps, with the return rising), which is the misalignment the abstract reports: the return rewards what the box does not. The two reward variants demonstrate the pathologies on purpose. Under reward version 1, hover_bonus (η = 0.3) lands in 6.3% of 300 episodes (4.1–9.7%) and times out in 228 of them, hovering near the target with a mean return of +43 on its own reward, 145 kg of propellant and 90 s below 30 m; under version 2 the same variant lands in 51.2% (46.8–55.6%), because the crash penalty and the larger bonus make landing pay again even at that η. no_eta, the pure shaping of [71], lands in none of 500 episodes: every one hits the ground at between −5.5 and −17.3 m/s (mean −7.7) after 36 s on average, the early-crash optimum. We report these not as a verdict on the method, which Gaudet and colleagues made work with far more compute [71], but as what a modest budget buys, and what it does not.

Compute, and the warm-up. Training and evaluation ran on the 8 GB laptop of Section 5.4. The committed PPO baseline records 9,685 s of wall-clock for 2,007,040 steps; its curve shows the run stalled between 250,000 steps (181 s) and 500,000 steps (8,837 s) under memory pressure from other work, and ran at 60–70 s per 100,000 steps otherwise, which the 314 and 464 s of the two 507,904-step variants confirm. The imitation runs took 655 s (observation v3) and 714 s (observation v2) on one process; each 500-episode Monte Carlo took 14–29 s. Before any descent training the toolchain was run on Gymnasium's LunarLander-v3 with RL Zoo's settings: 246.9 ± 21.2 over 20 deterministic episodes after one million steps in 1,368 s, against the published 242–271, so the library was ruled out as a cause of what followed.

6.2 Driving model validation

Table 6 and Figure 6 give the three checks of E16 on the committed tables (driving-v1, regenerated after the slip curves of Section 3.4 were adopted). First, the calibration itself. Driven through the tables' own leg integrator on a synthetic flat 2 km leg, teleoperation makes good 3.262, 2.570, 2.031 and 1.764 km/h at 0, 4, 6 and 8 s of round-trip delay against the measured 3.24, 2.56, 2.03 and 1.76 km/h (Measured) [29], errors of +0.66, +0.38, +0.07 and +0.20%, with 10, 20, 34 and 41 stops on the leg; the mean over 400 stochastic integrator runs (3.246, 2.560, 2.025 and 1.758 km/h) is within 0.3% of the measurements. The step environment driven by the expert gave 3.243, 2.576, 2.056 and 1.769 km/h (Section 5.2). The two agree because they share the physics, and both reproduce the measurements because the stop spacing was solved to make them; the check tests the implementation, not the model. On the same flat leg supervised autonomy makes good 2.711 km/h (a 3.0 km/h cap with a 60 s stop every 500 m, four stops) and full autonomy 8.94 km/h. Second, the flattest real leg of at least 500 m, from the construction site to the depot (662 m, maximum slope 5.8°), at sun azimuth 270°: teleoperation makes good 3.01, 2.39, 1.88 and 1.68 km/h, 4.7–7.2% below the flat-ground values, because slip on a 4–6° grade costs speed even below the 10° knee of the slope factor; supervised autonomy makes good 2.41–2.51 km/h and autonomous driving 7.8–8.0 km/h. The ratios between latencies survive: 0.79, 0.63 and 0.56 of the 0 s value against the measured 0.79, 0.63 and 0.54. Third, the energy anchor. The round trip from the base to the shadowed crater and back on the 8.95 km daylight route uses 1.77 kWh under autonomous driving, 1.83 kWh supervised and 1.84 kWh teleoperated at 4 s; at night, on the 8.57 km night route, 1.89, 1.86 and 1.77 kWh. All six lie inside Chen and colleagues' 0.4–4.4 kWh for a 500 kg rover (Modelled) [92] scaled by mass to 1.28–14.08 kWh for 1,600 kg (the scaling is Assumed linear in mass), and near its lower edge, as Section 5.2 said they should, because Chen's paths are longer and count terramechanics work only. Per kilometre the tables give about 198 Wh/km on this leg (134 Wh/km on flat ground), and the per-metre energy is the same in every lighting case to within 2% (197.5–198.6 Wh/km by day and 201.0 at night on the teleoperated base-to-crater leg at a friction angle of 42°). The differences between lighting cases are route length: the night route is the shortest because the planner adds no up-sun tacking at night, and the day routes at azimuths 0° and 270°, which drive up-sun on the way out, are 8.95 and 9.25 km against 8.57 km at 90° and 180°. A night round trip can therefore use less energy than a day one. That looked like a bug, and it is not. The soil matters more than the light: in development runs (50 integrator runs, autonomous, sun 90°) the same round trip uses 1.49 kWh on firm soil (friction angle 47.8°), 1.70 kWh on medium soil (42°), 2.56 kWh on weak soil (37.8°, where dig-in stretches raise the interventions to 12 per trip) and 1.76 kWh with the angle sampled per run as the tables do.

The intervention rates in the tables are the Assumed hazard of Section 3.4 integrated along each route: 0.10, 0.20 and 0.40 expected interventions on the flat 2 km leg for teleoperation, supervised and autonomous driving, and 0.0–0.15 per leg on the 662 m real leg. They are not validated, because nothing exists to validate them against (Section 2.4); Section 6.6 shows how much of a fleet's time they take. Every suite of this paper uses the scripted expert's tables. The learned driver of Section 3.4 was implemented but not trained in time for these runs, so its measured intervention rate, the one quantity that could replace the hazard, is not in this version.

6.3 Landing dispersion and pad radius

E17 ran to completion: 840 cells, 30 seeds each, 25,200 simulated years. Figure 7 and Table 8 give the dispersion result at the 1 km standoff, where the design is the seven miss levels crossed with three pad radii, two dispatch rules and four fleets. The first finding is mechanical, and we state it first because everything else follows from it: dispersion acts on logistics through off-pad landings only. The engine scales the miss vector of each drawn landing record. A scaled miss inside the pad radius changes nothing, and a failed landing (6.2% of the classical table, its cargo lost under the default policy) is the same at every level because failure is not scaled: [[E17: failed landings and cargo lost per year in the reference cell]]. So the only channel is the share of landings beyond the pad radius, which the pad radius sets as much as the dispersion does. At the 50 m level, the Architecture Definition Document's target, about 65% of landings are off a 25 m pad, about 30% off a 50 m pad and about 2% off a 100 m pad ([[E17: off-pad share at the 50 m level for the 25, 50 and 100 m pads, with CIs]]); at the 100 and 118 m levels, NASA's band and Blue Ghost's landing, about 85, 70 and 35% ([[E17: off-pad shares at the 100 and 118 m levels for the three pads]]); at IM-2's 300 m more than 80% are off every pad, and at IM-1's 1.5 km about 95% ([[E17: off-pad shares at 300 and 1,500 m]]). At the classical level 0.4% miss the 50 m pad, and at SLIM's 10 m [[E17: off-pad share at the 10 m level, 50 m pad]].

The second finding is that the delay cost of those off-pad landings is small until misses reach hundreds of metres. Paired against the classical level in the same fleet, rule, standoff and pad, the mean urgent delay rises by 0.1–0.5 h at the 50, 100 and 118 m levels, by 0.5–1 h at 300 m and by 1–3 h at 1.5 km, the largest changes in the two-vehicle in-house fleet, whose intervals are also the widest ([[E17: dCL paired differences in mean urgent delay by level, 50 m pad, 1 km standoff, four fleets and two rules, with bootstrap CIs]]). The same-day service level moves by [[E17: dCL on the same-day service level by level]] and the cost per kilogram by [[E17: dCL on cost per kg by level, without and with the 2,000 $/kWh energy price]]; the off-pad kilometres behind the delay are [[E17: off-pad extra km per year by level, P2-default marketplace]] a year. Each off-pad landing costs its pickup jobs a drive of twice the miss over unprepared ground at half speed and double energy (Assumed), so a 300 m miss adds about 0.6 km and a 1.5 km miss about 3 km to each pickup. That is why the effect is linear in the miss above the pad radius, and why fleets with idle vehicles absorb it. The pad radius changes the off-pad share a great deal and the delay very little ([[E17: dR50 paired differences, 25 and 100 m pads against the 50 m pad, mean urgent delay at the 50 and 118 m levels]]). The 50 m versus 100 m pad question is therefore a pad-mass and construction question, not a logistics one, unless misses are of the IM-2 class: the mass of a pad grows with the square of its radius [90], and the delay the larger pad saves is a fraction of an hour. On the hypothesis of Section 1 the answer is the second branch. Dispersion does not act smoothly through traverse distance; it acts through a threshold set by the pad radius and through the tail of the miss distribution.

6.5 Plume keep-out

E18 is the largest suite and was still running when this version was drafted: 1,248 cells (five radii by five stand-downs plus the no-keep-out cell, two night settings, two standoffs, three fleets, all four dispatch rules) at 30 seeds, [[E18: cells and seeds completed, of 37,440 simulated years]] complete. Figure 9 and Table 10 carry the paired differences against the matching no-keep-out cell on common seeds. We describe here what the design tests and what the preliminary analysis of the Phase-2 four-vehicle in-house cells found, because that analysis changed the model. The keep-out acts through three channels (Section 3.5): the dispatch filter, which removes pad jobs inside a window; the holds, which stop committed jobs at the boundary; and the evacuation, which moves idle vehicles inside R to a terrain-sheltered site when a window opens. Switching each channel off in turn in the four-vehicle in-house fleet at the 1 km standoff with the night on showed that the filter and the holds together cost between +0.000 and +0.021 h of mean urgent delay at every stand-down (bootstrap intervals of about [0, 0.06] h): at Phase-2 utilisation a vehicle is almost never wanted at a pad during a window. The whole effect was the evacuation, and its sign was not robust. Moving idle vehicles out of the radius reshuffles which vehicle takes the next job; the per-seed differences are heavy-tailed, single seeds moving by 1–2 h, and the mean flipped sign with small changes to the driving tables. With the evacuated vehicle left at the shelter, the 30-seed paired differences were +0.52 h at the 2-minute and 6-minute stand-downs, +0.67 h [+0.01, +1.80] at 0.5 h, +0.66 h [−0.01, +1.78] at 2 h and −0.26 h [−2.68, +1.58] at 6 h; a five-seed pilot had shown an apparent reduction of about 1 h, which 30 seeds did not reproduce (Section 4.2). The modelling choice this exposed is what an evacuated vehicle does when the window closes. Leaving it at the shelter is not what an operator would do, so the module now returns a still-idle vehicle to the site it left, counting the drive as a reposition and its hours as stand-down hours (plume.return_after, the default). With the return, the preliminary differences were −0.01 h [−0.39, +0.34] at 0.5 h, −0.03 h [−0.45, +0.34] at 2 h and −0.90 h [−2.95, +0.27] at 6 h, with the 2- and 6-minute windows unchanged at about +0.5 h because they close before the evacuation drive ends and nothing returns. At the 3 km standoff only about 1.5 vehicles a year are inside any radius when a window opens, and every variant sits within ±1 h of zero with intervals that include it.

6.6 Autonomy and fleet size

E19 is complete: 40 cells, 1,200 simulated years. Every vehicle of the Phase-2 fleets of two, four, six and eight (the fleets of version 2's suite E2) is set to the same autonomy level, with max(2, n/2) Earth operators, pads at 1 km, classical landings and the driving tables of Section 6.2 (Table 11, Figure 10). Autonomy level and fleet size trade against each other, and the exchange rate is about three to one. Under in-house contracting the mean urgent delay of a fleet teleoperated from Earth at 8 s of round-trip delay falls from 41.2 ± 7.2 h with two vehicles to 29.8 ± 5.3 h with four, 21.0 ± 4.4 h with six and 17.5 ± 3.5 h with eight; the same fleets driving autonomously deliver in 23.2 ± 5.0, 17.3 ± 4.4, 11.7 ± 3.2 and 10.1 ± 2.8 h. Under the marketplace the teleoperated fleets deliver in 30.4 ± 6.8, 21.0 ± 4.2, 14.6 ± 3.5 and 11.1 ± 2.9 h and the autonomous ones in 13.3 ± 3.9, 4.3 ± 1.2, 5.4 ± 2.3 and 3.2 ± 1.6 h. Two autonomous vehicles therefore deliver urgent cargo about as fast as six teleoperated at 8 s (13.3 against 14.6 h under the marketplace; 23.2 against 21.0 h in-house), and four autonomous vehicles under the marketplace beat eight teleoperated ones (4.3 against 11.1 h). Supervised autonomy sits close to full autonomy: paired on common seeds, autonomy improves on supervision by between 0.5 and 3.3 h of mean urgent delay, with intervals that exclude zero in five of the eight fleet-rule cells (in-house four and six vehicles: −1.7 h [−3.5, −0.0] and −3.2 h [−5.8, −0.9]; marketplace two, four and eight vehicles: −3.3 h [−5.5, −1.0], −2.1 h [−3.6, −1.0] and −1.4 h [−1.7, −1.1]) and include it in the other three. Teleoperation at 8 s costs, against supervision in the same fleet, +16.3 h [+12.4, +20.0], +10.8 h [+7.5, +14.0], +6.2 h [+4.8, +7.6] and +6.9 h [+4.6, +9.5] in-house and +13.8 h [+10.0, +17.8], +14.6 h [+11.7, +17.8], +8.5 h [+6.4, +11.0] and +6.5 h [+4.7, +8.5] under the marketplace, for two, four, six and eight vehicles; at 4 s it costs +2.0 to +6.0 h, and every 8 s interval excludes zero. The latency itself matters less than the mode: the 4 to 8 s step costs 5–10 h in the small fleets and 3–5 h in the large ones, roughly what the JSC speed table predicts for the longer confirmation stops.

The service level tells the fleet-sizing story. The marketplace with six supervised vehicles delivers 93.6 ± 3.7% of urgent items within a day; eight teleoperated vehicles at 8 s reach 90.0 ± 4.7% and at 4 s 93.5 ± 3.9%. In-house, six supervised vehicles reach 83.4 ± 5.3% and eight teleoperated at 8 s 80.5 ± 5.3%. So within the fleets we ran, no teleoperated fleet at 8 s matches the six-vehicle supervised fleet's service level under either rule, and the 4 s fleet needs eight vehicles to do so under the marketplace. The operator bill is the other half. Teleoperation at 4, 6 and 8 s books 1,886–1,912, 2,293–2,341 and 2,635–2,731 console hours a year in the in-house fleets and 1,637–2,322, 2,045–2,818 and 2,381–3,323 under the marketplace, which drives more; supervised autonomy books 280–386 hours and full autonomy 40–56. With two operators for the four-vehicle fleet, the marketplace's teleoperated jobs wait for a free operator 22–34 times a year (in-house 3.5–4.3), which is part of its 8 s penalty; the six- and eight-vehicle fleets, with three and four operators, wait 10–18 and 4.5–10 times. Interventions are nearly independent of the level, 420–540 a year costing 36–45 hours, because the terrain terms of the Assumed hazard (steep and rough cells) dominate the base rates; they take about 5% of the fleets' busy time. Fleet utilisation is 1.3–15%, the cost per kilogram moved is set by the fleet ($176–$694) and moves by under 3% with the level, and energy is 500–664 kWh a year, higher under the marketplace because it drives farther. Version 2's suite E5, on the tangent plane with its speed-multiplier latency model and a five-vehicle mixed fleet, had put the 90th-percentile urgent delay of a teleoperated fleet at 48 h at 8 s [113]; the six-vehicle fleets here give 56.3 ± 13.4 h in-house and 40.2 ± 12.7 h under the marketplace, with the stops and interventions now modelled rather than fitted.

6.7 The fitted law and pad siting

The law. E20 pools every simulated year of E17 and E18 (one row per seed of each cell) and fits, by ordinary least squares, three responses: the natural logarithm of the mean urgent delay, so that 100(eb − 1) is the percentage change per unit of a regressor; the share of urgent items delivered within 24 h; and the cost per kilogram moved, without and with an energy price of 500, 2,000 and 5,000 $/kWh. The regressors are the logarithm of the implied mean miss, the standoff D, the keep-out radius R, the stand-down T, the night fraction, the reference-cell utilisation u of the same fleet, rule and night setting (a group-level load covariate, not the row's own utilisation), the pad radius, the year's off-pad share (so that the fit can separate dispersion acting through off-pad events from any smooth effect), and the interactions ln miss × D, R × T, R × u, T × u, D × u and ln miss × u, with mechanism dummies (in-house the base) in the pooled fit and none in the per-mechanism fits. Columns constant in the data are dropped and listed (in E17 rows R and T are zero; in E18 rows the miss is at the classical level and the pad 50 m). Confidence intervals come from a percentile bootstrap over seeds with 1,000 resamples in which every row of a drawn seed enters the normal equations with that seed's multiplicity, so the intervals respect the common-random-number pairing. This design differs from Section 4.2 in two regressors, the off-pad share and the pad radius, added when Section 6.3 showed that dispersion acts through the off-pad threshold. The fit on file at the time of writing used the E17 rows only; the final fit, with E18, gives on the pooled logarithm of mean urgent delay an R² of [[E20: pooled R-squared on log mean urgent delay, n rows and seeds]] with a residual standard deviation of [[E20: residual SD, pooled log delay]]. Its leading terms are [[E20: coefficient on standoff D with CI, as % per km]] per kilometre of standoff, [[E20: coefficient on ln miss with CI, as % per e-fold of miss]] per e-fold of mean miss, [[E20: coefficient on the off-pad share with CI]] per unit of off-pad share, [[E20: coefficients on R and T and on R x T, with CIs]] for the keep-out, [[E20: coefficient on u and on the D x u, R x u and T x u interactions, with CIs]] for the load interactions, and [[E20: the marketplace dummy with CI]] for the marketplace against in-house. The same-day service level fit has R² [[E20: R-squared on sl24, pooled]] and the cost fits [[E20: R-squared on cost per kg without and with energy prices]], the cost being almost entirely fleet (Table 12, Figure 11). The per-mechanism fits give [[E20: per-mechanism R-squared and the D coefficient for in-house and marketplace, with CIs]]. We read the law as a description of this testbed's response surface, with intervals that are only as good as the 30 seeds behind each term, and not as a prediction for any base but one laid out as Table 2 is.

Pad siting. The siting table turns the standoff result into a trade a planner can read. For every E17 cell (fleet, rule, miss level, pad radius, standoff) it adds four terms per year: the hauling cost (operator time plus driving energy at 0, 500, 2,000 or 5,000 $/kWh; Modelled hauling, Assumed price); the amortised pad construction cost over a ten-year life (Assumed), $130, $229 or $548 million per pad at transport prices of $10,000, $100,000 and $300,000 per kilogram (Metzger and Autry [66]; Spec), which does not depend on standoff in this version and therefore shifts totals, not minima; the expected plume damage, equal to landings a year times expected impacts per landing times a line-of-sight shield times a cost per impact, where the impacts per landing follow Wittal and colleagues' anchors of 8 at 1 km, 0.37 at 5 km and 0.10 at 10 km for a 10 t lander (Modelled) [89] with ln I linear in distance between the anchors and extrapolated to 0.5 km on the 1–5 km slope (11.7 at 0.5 km, 3.7 at 2 km, 1.7 at 3 km), the shield 0.1 (Assumed) where Table 2 says the terrain blocks pad A's line of sight to the base (2 km and beyond) and 1 where it does not, and the cost per impact 0.1, 1 or 10 million dollars (Assumed, swept); and a delay valuation, delivered items times mean delay times a value of time of 0, 100 or 1,000 $ per item-hour (Assumed, swept; a blended stand-in for the engine's per-priority values of $5,000, $1,000 and $100 an hour). The minimum-cost standoff per cell and cost case is then read off, and Table 13 and Figure 12 report where the minima lie. The reference cell (Phase-2 default fleet, marketplace, classical landings, 50 m pad) has hauling costs of [[E20: hauling cost per year at 0.5, 1, 2, 3 and 5 km, reference cell, p = 0 and 2,000]] against a pad cost of 13–55 million dollars a year and a plume damage term of [[E20: damage term at 0.5, 1, 2, 3 and 5 km for the 1 M$ per impact case, reference cell]], and its minimum-cost standoff is [[E20: minimum-cost standoff of the reference cell by energy price, damage cost and value of time]]. Across all cells and pad-cost cases, [[E20: share of minima at 0.5, 1, 2, 3 and 5 km by energy price, damage cost and value of time, from table_E20_siting_shares]]. The structure of the answer is already visible in the terms: with no damage priced the hauling cost favours the nearest pad; once an impact costs a million dollars the damage term at 0.5–1 km, where the line of sight is clear and 8–12 impacts a landing are expected, exceeds any hauling saving, and the minimum moves to the first standoff at which the terrain shields the base, which on this ridge is 2 km; a high value of delivery time pulls it back towards 2–3 km rather than 5. The numbers that decide between 2, 3 and 5 km are the ones NASA says it does not have, the impacts per landing and their cost, and we have swept them rather than guessed them.
