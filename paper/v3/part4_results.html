Every number in this section that the testbed produced is Modelled; we flag only the measured and specified anchors it is compared with. One version-2 number is corrected here. Version 2 reported the cost per kilogram of landed cargo as about $5,100 for the Phase-2 four-vehicle marketplace cell and about $5,000 for the Phase-3 default fleet. That figure divided the fleet and operator cost by delivered mass minus bulk released, which keeps return items in the denominator and subtracts lots that were released but not delivered. The exact metric, the same cost over the delivered mass of items that came down on a lander (bulk surface flows and return items excluded), computed as the ratio of means over 30 years with a seed-bootstrap 95% interval because single years with one landing make per-year ratios unstable, gives $5,525 [4,460, 7,281] per landed kilogram for the Phase-2 four-vehicle marketplace cell and $5,630 [4,883, 6,655] for the Phase-3 default marketplace cell, against $10,019 [8,067, 12,756] and $7,199 [6,182, 8,612] in-house. The approximation under-read by about 10%: “about $5,000” becomes “about $5,500”. The cost per kilogram moved is unchanged ($306–314 at Phase 2, $264–267 at Phase 3), and so is every conclusion that rested on it. The engine now logs both quantities.

Where the law sits against the record. A mean miss of 2.9 m and a 90th percentile of 4.7 m describe a nominal vehicle with terrain-relative navigation (the 1.5 m navigation bias is Assumed on the strength of NASA's “below 4 m” study [83]). They lie an order of magnitude inside the 50 m target of the Architecture Definition Document (Spec) [115] and the 100 m HLS requirement (Spec) [82], and two orders inside most of the flown record of 2023–2025 (Measured) [84–87]. That is why E17 does not sweep a multiplier blind. It scales the whole miss distribution so that its mean becomes 10, 50, 100, 118, 300 or 1,500 m, that is σscale 3.4, 17.2, 34.5, 40.7, 103 and 517, and the pad radius of 25, 50 or 100 m decides which scaled misses are off-pad. One caution travels with the mapping. NASA does not say whether its 50 m is a mean, a 1σ, a 3σ or a hard radius. Our 50 m level is a mean, and under it the scaled distribution has a 90th percentile of about 81 m, so about 30% of landings fall outside a 50 m pad (Section 6.3); if NASA's 50 m is a 3σ radius, the corresponding mean lies between our 10 and 50 m levels, and the off-pad share with it.

The learned lander and what it taught us. The ladder is in Table 7, from the law down. Cloning the filtered law reached 75% on 100 evaluation episodes after behavioural cloning and then fell through four DAgger rounds to 69, 60, 51 and 43%; on the 500-episode Monte Carlo the exported clone (lander_CLPS_bc_s0) meets the box in 58.2% (53.8–62.4%), with 133 sensor-attributed near-misses and 49 tip-overs. DAgger is meant to repair covariate shift, not make it worse [99], so we measured what the clone was being asked to learn. For each of 150 expert episodes we took the ten nearest neighbours of every observation (standardised) and computed the variance of the expert's actions among them as a share of the total action variance: a label-inconsistency score that is 0 for an action that is a function of the observation and 1 for noise. The filtered law scores 0.41 (throttle 0.03; the two tilt-rate commands 0.60 and 0.61). The throttle is a function of what the clone sees; the attitude commands are not, because they depend on the law's smoothed velocity, low-pass-filtered direction and held command, which the observation does not carry. A memoryless learner cannot clone a filtered expert, and aggregating more of its labels aggregates more contradiction. We therefore wrote the same ZEM/ZEV and terminal law without filter state: the tilt-rate command is 0.25 times the angle error per step and zero below 3 m (Assumed gain), so the smoothing moves into the vehicle's attitude dynamics. It scores 0.33 and, flown itself, meets the box in 94.8% of 500 episodes (92.5–96.4%), one point above the filtered law. Cloning it with the same 15-component observation gave 80, 74 and 83% over behavioural cloning and two DAgger rounds and 81.2% (77.5–84.4%) on the Monte Carlo; 63 of its 94 failures were sensor near-misses, because from a single observation the clone cannot tell a held altimeter reading from a true one, which the expert's velocity-based terminal law largely ignores. Weighting the terminal-phase samples three times in the loss did not help (76.8%). Giving the clone the expert's information did. Observation version 3 appends the last valid altitude, its age and the two previous readings (four inputs, scaled by 30 m, 2 s, 30 m and 30 m), and the same recipe (800 expert episodes, 25 epochs, two DAgger rounds of 300 episodes, a 128 × 128 tanh network, 655 s on one process) gave 91, 87 and 87% in the 100-episode round evaluations and 89.0% (86.0–91.5%) on the 500-episode Monte Carlo (lander_CLPS_bc_v3_s0): 33 sensor near-misses, 16 velocity failures, 6 tip-overs, no timeouts, a mean miss of 3.2 m, 81 kg of propellant and 20 s below 30 m. The round-0 clone without DAgger scores 87.2% on the same 500 episodes (84.0–89.8%) with 11 timeouts, so DAgger is worth 1.8 points here, inside the interval. The label inconsistency of the memoryless expert's aggregated data stayed at 0.28–0.35 across rounds. The final clone is 4.8 ± 3.5 points below the filtered law and 5.8 points below the expert it imitates. We did not train MK1 or LARGE clones; those classes land by the law in every suite.

Proximal policy optimisation. PPO under the Gaudet-style reward reached the box in 43.6% of 500 episodes (39.3–48.0%) after two million steps under reward version 1 (κ = 10), with 182 sensor-attributed failures; the same recipe under reward version 2 (κ 50, crash −5 per m/s, near-ground gain 3) reaches 63.2% (58.9–67.3%; lander_CLPS_baseline_s0, 2,007,040 steps), 30.6 ± 4.7 points below the law on the same episodes. Its failure pattern is the one Section 3.3 described: it lands fast and lean. The mean vertical speed at touchdown is −1.97 m/s against the 2.0 m/s limit (law −1.23, median −1.00); the 90th-percentile horizontal speed is 1.39 m/s against the 1.0 m/s limit (law 0.15); the mean tilt at touchdown is 4.5° against 6° (law 1.6°). It uses 61 kg of propellant to the law's 89 and spends 9.1 s below 30 m to the law's 23.5. Those are the marks of a policy that has found the edge of the box and the cheapest way over it, and the altimeter noise below 30 m then pushes 110 of 500 landings past the edge. Fine-tuning from a cloned actor made this worse in every run we tried (development: 74% at the start, 51–52% after 300,000 steps, with the return rising), which is the misalignment the abstract reports: the return rewards what the box does not. The two reward variants demonstrate the pathologies on purpose. Under reward version 1, hover_bonus (η = 0.3) lands in 6.3% of 300 episodes (4.1–9.7%) and times out in 228 of them, hovering near the target with a mean return of +43 on its own reward, 145 kg of propellant and 90 s below 30 m; under version 2 the same variant lands in 51.2% (46.8–55.6%), because the crash penalty and the larger bonus make landing pay again even at that η. no_eta, the pure shaping of [71], lands in none of 500 episodes: every one hits the ground at between −5.5 and −17.3 m/s (mean −7.7) after 36 s on average, the early-crash optimum. We report these not as a verdict on the method, which Gaudet and colleagues made work with far more compute [71], but as what a modest budget buys, and what it does not.

Compute, and the warm-up. Training and evaluation ran on the 8 GB laptop of Section 5.4. The committed PPO baseline records 9,685 s of wall-clock for 2,007,040 steps; its curve shows the run stalled between 250,000 steps (181 s) and 500,000 steps (8,837 s) under memory pressure from other work, and ran at 60–70 s per 100,000 steps otherwise, which the 314 and 464 s of the two 507,904-step variants confirm. The imitation runs took 655 s (observation v3) and 714 s (observation v2) on one process; each 500-episode Monte Carlo took 14–29 s. Before any descent training the toolchain was run on Gymnasium's LunarLander-v3 with RL Zoo's settings: 246.9 ± 21.2 over 20 deterministic episodes after one million steps in 1,368 s, against the published 242–271, so the library was ruled out as a cause of what followed.

6.2 Driving model validation

Table 6 and Figure 6 give the three checks of E16 on the committed tables (driving-v1, regenerated after the slip curves of Section 3.4 were adopted). First, the calibration itself. Driven through the tables' own leg integrator on a synthetic flat 2 km leg, teleoperation makes good 3.262, 2.570, 2.031 and 1.764 km/h at 0, 4, 6 and 8 s of round-trip delay against the measured 3.24, 2.56, 2.03 and 1.76 km/h (Measured) [29], errors of +0.66, +0.38, +0.07 and +0.20%, with 10, 20, 34 and 41 stops on the leg; the mean over 400 stochastic integrator runs (3.246, 2.560, 2.025 and 1.758 km/h) is within 0.3% of the measurements. The step environment driven by the expert gave 3.243, 2.576, 2.056 and 1.769 km/h (Section 5.2). The two agree because they share the physics, and both reproduce the measurements because the stop spacing was solved to make them; the check tests the implementation, not the model. On the same flat leg supervised autonomy makes good 2.711 km/h (a 3.0 km/h cap with a 60 s stop every 500 m, four stops) and full autonomy 8.94 km/h. Second, the flattest real leg of at least 500 m, from the construction site to the depot (662 m, maximum slope 5.8°), at sun azimuth 270°: teleoperation makes good 3.01, 2.39, 1.88 and 1.68 km/h, 4.7–7.2% below the flat-ground values, because slip on a 4–6° grade costs speed even below the 10° knee of the slope factor; supervised autonomy makes good 2.41–2.51 km/h and autonomous driving 7.8–8.0 km/h. The ratios between latencies survive: 0.79, 0.63 and 0.56 of the 0 s value against the measured 0.79, 0.63 and 0.54. Third, the energy anchor. The round trip from the base to the shadowed crater and back on the 8.95 km daylight route uses 1.77 kWh under autonomous driving, 1.83 kWh supervised and 1.84 kWh teleoperated at 4 s; at night, on the 8.57 km night route, 1.89, 1.86 and 1.77 kWh. All six lie inside Chen and colleagues' 0.4–4.4 kWh for a 500 kg rover (Modelled) [92] scaled by mass to 1.28–14.08 kWh for 1,600 kg (the scaling is Assumed linear in mass), and near its lower edge, as Section 5.2 said they should, because Chen's paths are longer and count terramechanics work only. Per kilometre the tables give about 198 Wh/km on this leg (134 Wh/km on flat ground), and the per-metre energy is the same in every lighting case to within 2% (197.5–198.6 Wh/km by day and 201.0 at night on the teleoperated base-to-crater leg at a friction angle of 42°). The differences between lighting cases are route length: the night route is the shortest because the planner adds no up-sun tacking at night, and the day routes at azimuths 0° and 270°, which drive up-sun on the way out, are 8.95 and 9.25 km against 8.57 km at 90° and 180°. A night round trip can therefore use less energy than a day one. That looked like a bug, and it is not. The soil matters more than the light: in development runs (50 integrator runs, autonomous, sun 90°) the same round trip uses 1.49 kWh on firm soil (friction angle 47.8°), 1.70 kWh on medium soil (42°), 2.56 kWh on weak soil (37.8°, where dig-in stretches raise the interventions to 12 per trip) and 1.76 kWh with the angle sampled per run as the tables do.

The intervention rates in the tables are the Assumed hazard of Section 3.4 integrated along each route: 0.10, 0.20 and 0.40 expected interventions on the flat 2 km leg for teleoperation, supervised and autonomous driving, and 0.0–0.15 per leg on the 662 m real leg. They are not validated, because nothing exists to validate them against (Section 2.4); Section 6.6 shows how much of a fleet's time they take. Every suite of this paper uses the scripted expert's tables. The learned driver of Section 3.4 was implemented but not trained in time for these runs, so its measured intervention rate, the one quantity that could replace the hazard, is not in this version.

6.3 Landing dispersion and pad radius

Figure 7 and Table 8 give the dispersion result at the 1 km standoff, where the design is the seven miss levels crossed with three pad radii, two dispatch rules and four fleets. The first finding is mechanical, and we state it first because everything else follows from it: dispersion acts on logistics through off-pad landings only. The engine scales the miss vector of each drawn landing record. A scaled miss inside the pad radius changes nothing, and a failed landing is the same at every level because failure is not scaled: 0.37–0.77 landings a year fail in the reference cells (4.5–7.0% of the 8.1 Phase-2 and 11.0 Phase-3 landings a year), losing 0.6–4.3 t of cargo with intervals as wide as the means (Phase-2 default fleet, marketplace: 0.37 ± 0.20 failed landings and 624 ± 443 kg lost a year). So the only channel is the share of landings beyond the pad radius, which the pad radius sets as much as the dispersion does. At the 50 m level, the Architecture Definition Document's target, 67.7 ± 6.5% of landings are off a 25 m pad, 31.7 ± 5.2% off a 50 m pad and 3.3 ± 2.1% off a 100 m pad (Phase-2 default fleet, marketplace; the means over the eight fleet-and-rule cells are 66.2, 30.3 and 2.6%); at the 100 and 118 m levels, NASA's band and Blue Ghost's landing, 85.6 ± 5.6, 68.8 ± 6.3 and 34.8 ± 5.4% and then 89.0 ± 4.9, 76.4 ± 6.2 and 40.6 ± 6.4%; at IM-2's 300 m, 94.7 ± 3.1, 89.8 ± 5.0 and 81.7 ± 5.6%; at IM-1's 1.5 km, 95.5 ± 2.8, 95.3 ± 2.8 and 94.6 ± 2.8%, which is the ceiling, since the landings that fail are not counted as off-pad. At the classical level no landing in these runs missed any pad, and at SLIM's 10 m 0.8 ± 1.1% missed the 25 m pad and none the larger two.

The second finding is that the delay cost of those off-pad landings is small until misses reach hundreds of metres. Paired against the classical level in the same fleet, rule, standoff and pad (50 m), the mean urgent delay changes by between −0.05 h [−0.18, +0.03] (Phase-3 fleet, marketplace) and +0.49 h [−0.10, +1.56] (Phase-2 four-vehicle fleet, in-house) at the 50 m level, by −0.05 h [−0.36, +0.16] to +0.87 h [+0.05, +2.18] at 100 m, by −0.03 h [−0.32, +0.20] to +0.59 h [−0.48, +1.90] at 118 m, by −0.16 h [−0.69, +0.26] to +1.05 h [−0.17, +2.96] at 300 m, and by +0.62 h [−0.90, +1.77] to +2.85 h [+1.93, +3.88] at 1,500 m. Up to 118 m the intervals exclude zero only in the in-house fleets, and there the differences are 0.05–0.9 h (Phase-2 default fleet: +0.05 h [+0.03, +0.09] at 50 m, +0.24 h [+0.10, +0.53] at 100 m, +0.27 h [+0.12, +0.54] at 118 m); at 300 m four of the eight cells exclude zero (Phase-2 default fleet +0.54 h [+0.36, +0.83] in-house and +0.48 h [+0.20, +0.79] under the marketplace; two-vehicle in-house fleet +0.64 h [+0.16, +1.25]; Phase-3 in-house +0.52 h [+0.05, +1.07]); at 1,500 m six of eight do (Phase-2 default fleet +2.18 h [+1.52, +2.91] in-house and +1.45 h [+0.24, +2.65] marketplace; four-vehicle marketplace +1.84 h [+1.31, +2.39]; Phase-3 fleet +2.13 h [+1.45, +2.85] in-house and +1.02 h [+0.27, +1.68] marketplace). The same-day service level moves by at most one point up to 300 m (−0.009 to +0.010) and by −0.032 [−0.053, −0.014] at worst at 1,500 m; the cost per kilogram moved by at most $0.7 (−0.61 [−1.60, −0.01] to +0.71 [−0.02, +2.17]), and with energy priced at 2,000 $/kWh by up to +0.90 $ [+0.33, +1.59] at 1,500 m. The off-pad kilometres behind the delay are 2.2, 6.7, 8.2, 21.1 and 104.9 a year at the 50, 100, 118, 300 and 1,500 m levels in the Phase-2 default marketplace cell (2.8–158.2 in the Phase-3 fleet, which lands more often). Each off-pad landing costs its pickup jobs a drive of twice the miss over unprepared ground at half speed and double energy (Assumed), so a 300 m miss adds about 0.6 km and a 1.5 km miss about 3 km to each pickup. That is why the effect is linear in the miss above the pad radius, and why fleets with idle vehicles absorb it. The pad radius changes the off-pad share a great deal and the delay very little: at the 50 m level a 25 m pad instead of 50 m changes the mean urgent delay by −0.05 h [−0.18, +0.02] to +0.09 h [−0.04, +0.27] and a 100 m pad by −0.54 h [−1.56, −0.02] to +0.05 h [−0.03, +0.18]; at 118 m the corresponding ranges are −0.00 to +0.06 h and −0.27 h [−0.73, −0.04] to +0.12 h [−0.05, +0.44]. The 50 m versus 100 m pad question is therefore a pad-mass and construction question, not a logistics one, unless misses are of the IM-2 class: the mass of a pad grows with the square of its radius [90], and the delay the larger pad saves is a fraction of an hour. On the hypothesis of Section 1 the answer is the second branch. Dispersion does not act smoothly through traverse distance; it acts through a threshold set by the pad radius and through the tail of the miss distribution, and the fitted law of Section 6.7 confirms it: the coefficient on the logarithm of the mean miss is indistinguishable from zero.

6.4 Pad standoff

Figure 8 and Table 9 hold the landing at the classical level and the pad at 50 m and move the pads from 0.5 to 5 km. Standoff is the variable NASA's documents describe and do not price, and here it has a price in every fleet. From 0.5 to 5 km the mean urgent delay rises by 4.7 h in the best case, the Phase-3 nine-vehicle fleet under the marketplace (3.15 ± 0.60 to 7.85 ± 1.19 h), and by 18.5 h in the worst, the two-vehicle Phase-2 fleet under in-house contracting (24.81 ± 5.40 to 43.28 ± 6.99 h). 6.5 Plume keep-out

E18 is the largest suite: 1,248 cells at 30 seeds, 37,440 simulated years. Figure 9 and Table 10 carry the paired differences in mean urgent delay against the matching no-keep-out cell on common seeds. The design was run twice in part, because the first analysis changed the model, and we report what it found before the final numbers. The keep-out acts through three channels (Section 3.5): the dispatch filter, which removes pad jobs inside a window; the holds, which stop committed jobs at the boundary; and the evacuation, which moves idle vehicles inside R to a terrain-sheltered site when a window opens. Switching each channel off in turn in the four-vehicle in-house fleet at the 1 km standoff with the night on showed that the filter and the holds together cost between +0.000 and +0.021 h of mean urgent delay at every stand-down (bootstrap intervals of about [0, 0.06] h): at Phase-2 utilisation a vehicle is almost never wanted at a pad during a window. The whole effect was the evacuation, and its sign was not robust at 30 seeds, because moving idle vehicles out of the radius reshuffles which vehicle takes the next job and the per-seed differences are heavy-tailed, single seeds moving by 1–2 h. Two modelling questions came out of that. First, what an evacuated vehicle does when the window closes: leaving it at the shelter is not what an operator would do, so the module now returns a still-idle vehicle to the site it left, counting the drive as a reposition and its hours as stand-down hours (plume.return_after, the default). Second, where the marketplace stages a vehicle ahead of a landing whose pad will have a window: staging at the line-of-sight shelter produced a +0.92 h [+0.16, +2.16] penalty for a 0.2 km radius with a 6 h stand-down in the four-vehicle fleet, a radius that encloses no site and evacuates nothing, because the shelter is farther from the pad than the vehicle's usual staging spot and the first pickup started from there after the window; the staging rule now sends the vehicle to the nearest site outside R from the pad, the keep-out boundary, and the penalty went to +0.39 h [−0.14, +0.94]. The stand-down hours were also split into holds (jobs held at the boundary, times vehicles) and drives (evacuations and returns); in every marketplace cell the holds are under one vehicle-hour a year (at most 0.95) and the drives are the rest, so the keep-out's cost in vehicle time is driving, not waiting. The marketplace cells were re-run under the new staging rule; the other three rules do not stage and were unaffected.

The final tables say four things. The exclusion itself is nearly free; the radius decides. Of the 1,200 keep-out cells, 941 have intervals that include zero, 240 are positive with intervals that exclude it (41 of them under 0.1 h, 132 between 0.1 and 0.5 h, 49 between 0.5 and 1 h and 18 above 1 h) and 19 are negative (all between −0.07 and −0.48 h, in the pooled rules with the night off). Averaged over fleets, rules and stand-downs at the 1 km standoff with the night on, the paired difference is +0.08 h at radii of 0.2 and 0.5 km, +0.01 h at 1 km, +0.44 h at 2 km and +0.67 h at 3 km; with the night off +0.01, +0.01, −0.01, +0.22 and +0.37 h. The 1 km radius, the one that matches NASA's “more than 1,000 m” band, costs nothing we can measure: with a 2 h stand-down the paired differences are −0.10 h [−0.41, +0.20] in the Phase-3 fleet under the marketplace and −0.03 h [−0.36, +0.29] in-house, +0.22 h [−0.03, +0.60] and +0.02 h [−0.28, +0.28] in the Phase-2 default fleet, and −0.36 h [−1.86, +0.55] and −0.03 h [−0.44, +0.35] in the four-vehicle fleet, with the night on; with the night off they are +0.03 h [−0.16, +0.24], −0.07 h [−0.27, +0.12], −0.01 h [−0.21, +0.16], +0.16 h [−0.02, +0.41], −0.13 h [−0.42, +0.11] and −0.21 h [−0.77, +0.18]. The 2 and 3 km radii, which enclose most of the base, do cost: the largest effects in the design are +1.86 h [+0.64, +3.22] (four-vehicle in-house fleet, 2 km, two-minute and six-minute stand-downs, night on), +1.73 h [+0.90, +2.80] (Phase-2 default in-house, 3 km, same stand-downs) and +1.56 h [+0.05, +3.13], and in the Phase-3 fleet +0.82 h [+0.18, +1.63] (marketplace, 2 km, 2 h) and +0.78 h [+0.29, +1.30] (in-house, 3 km, 0.5 h). The stand-down time hardly matters, and when it does the short windows cost more. Averaged the same way, the two-minute, six-minute, 0.5 h, 2 h and 6 h stand-downs cost +0.33, +0.33, +0.30, +0.20 and +0.12 h with the night on. The evacuation happens whatever T is; after a two-minute window the evacuated vehicle is still driving to the shelter when the window closes, so it stays displaced, while after a longer window it is either re-dispatched from the shelter or driven back. In the Phase-3 fleet under the marketplace a 1 km keep-out costs −0.01 h [−0.34, +0.40] at two minutes and −0.14 h [−0.40, +0.08] at six hours (in-house −0.04 h [−0.38, +0.30] and −0.21 h [−0.54, +0.11]). The night roughly doubles the cost of the large radii (+0.44 and +0.67 h at 2 and 3 km against +0.22 and +0.37 h without it), because a window and the dark can coincide, and the only cells above +1 h are night cells of the Phase-2 fleets. Fleet load does not change the sign. The Phase-3 fleet, the only loaded one (utilisation 0.042–0.076 against 0.021–0.056 for the Phase-2 fleets), shows the same pattern at smaller amplitude; the fitted law of Section 6.7 gives the keep-out radius a coefficient that shrinks with utilisation. At the 3 km standoff, where the radii enclose fewer sites, every cell is within ±1.5 h and only two Phase-3 marketplace cells exclude zero (1 km, 2 h: +0.26 h [+0.02, +0.61]). The same-day service level moves by at most two points anywhere (−0.023 to +0.018).

What the keep-out does cost is driving. In the Phase-3 fleet under the marketplace at the 1 km standoff with the night on, stand-down vehicle-hours a year are 18.4 ± 2.6 (1 km radius, two-minute window), 31.0 ± 3.8 (0.5 h), 37.6 ± 5.5 (2 h) and 32.5 ± 4.5 (6 h), for 25–27 stand-down events and 35–60 repositions a year; at 3 km they are 72.7 ± 8.5, 85.5, 160.2 ± 19.2 and 148.3 ± 18.4, and at 0.2 km 0.9–2.2. The 2 h window costs more vehicle-hours than the 6 h window at the large radii in every fleet and rule (the Phase-2 four-vehicle fleet under pooled greedy dispatch: 72.9 against 67.3 at 3 km), which looked like an error and is the return drive: a vehicle is driven back only if it is still idle at the shelter when the window closes, and over a six-hour window more evacuated vehicles are dispatched from the shelter first. Without the return the two windows cost the same (32.6 against 33.7 vehicle-hours). We keep the return, because it is time the keep-out costs, and report the split. Against the fleets' 8,760 vehicle-hours a vehicle a year, the worst case, 160 vehicle-hours for nine vehicles, is 0.2% of fleet time; against their 5–7% utilisation it is 3%.

6.6 Autonomy and fleet size

E19 is complete: 40 cells, 1,200 simulated years. Every vehicle of the Phase-2 fleets of two, four, six and eight (the fleets of version 2's suite E2) is set to the same autonomy level, with max(2, n/2) Earth operators, pads at 1 km, classical landings and the driving tables of Section 6.2 (Table 11, Figure 10). Autonomy level and fleet size trade against each other, and the exchange rate is about three to one. Under in-house contracting the mean urgent delay of a fleet teleoperated from Earth at 8 s of round-trip delay falls from 41.2 ± 7.2 h with two vehicles to 29.8 ± 5.3 h with four, 21.0 ± 4.4 h with six and 17.5 ± 3.5 h with eight; the same fleets driving autonomously deliver in 23.2 ± 5.0, 17.3 ± 4.4, 11.7 ± 3.2 and 10.1 ± 2.8 h. Under the marketplace the teleoperated fleets deliver in 30.4 ± 6.8, 21.0 ± 4.2, 14.6 ± 3.5 and 11.1 ± 2.9 h and the autonomous ones in 13.3 ± 3.9, 4.3 ± 1.2, 5.4 ± 2.3 and 3.2 ± 1.6 h. Two autonomous vehicles therefore deliver urgent cargo about as fast as six teleoperated at 8 s (13.3 against 14.6 h under the marketplace; 23.2 against 21.0 h in-house), and four autonomous vehicles under the marketplace beat eight teleoperated ones (4.3 against 11.1 h). Supervised autonomy sits close to full autonomy: paired on common seeds, autonomy improves on supervision by between 0.5 and 3.3 h of mean urgent delay, with intervals that exclude zero in five of the eight fleet-rule cells (in-house four and six vehicles: −1.7 h [−3.5, −0.0] and −3.2 h [−5.8, −0.9]; marketplace two, four and eight vehicles: −3.3 h [−5.5, −1.0], −2.1 h [−3.6, −1.0] and −1.4 h [−1.7, −1.1]) and include it in the other three. Teleoperation at 8 s costs, against supervision in the same fleet, +16.3 h [+12.4, +20.0], +10.8 h [+7.5, +14.0], +6.2 h [+4.8, +7.6] and +6.9 h [+4.6, +9.5] in-house and +13.8 h [+10.0, +17.8], +14.6 h [+11.7, +17.8], +8.5 h [+6.4, +11.0] and +6.5 h [+4.7, +8.5] under the marketplace, for two, four, six and eight vehicles; at 4 s it costs +2.0 to +6.0 h, and every 8 s interval excludes zero. The latency itself matters less than the mode: the 4 to 8 s step costs 5–10 h in the small fleets and 3–5 h in the large ones, roughly what the JSC speed table predicts for the longer confirmation stops.

The service level tells the fleet-sizing story. The marketplace with six supervised vehicles delivers 93.6 ± 3.7% of urgent items within a day; eight teleoperated vehicles at 8 s reach 90.0 ± 4.7% and at 4 s 93.5 ± 3.9%. In-house, six supervised vehicles reach 83.4 ± 5.3% and eight teleoperated at 8 s 80.5 ± 5.3%. So within the fleets we ran, no teleoperated fleet at 8 s matches the six-vehicle supervised fleet's service level under either rule, and the 4 s fleet needs eight vehicles to do so under the marketplace. The operator bill is the other half. Teleoperation at 4, 6 and 8 s books 1,886–1,912, 2,293–2,341 and 2,635–2,731 console hours a year in the in-house fleets and 1,637–2,322, 2,045–2,818 and 2,381–3,323 under the marketplace, which drives more; supervised autonomy books 280–386 hours and full autonomy 40–56. With two operators for the four-vehicle fleet, the marketplace's teleoperated jobs wait for a free operator 22–34 times a year (in-house 3.5–4.3), which is part of its 8 s penalty; the six- and eight-vehicle fleets, with three and four operators, wait 10–18 and 4.5–10 times. Interventions are nearly independent of the level, 420–540 a year costing 36–45 hours, because the terrain terms of the Assumed hazard (steep and rough cells) dominate the base rates; they take about 5% of the fleets' busy time. Fleet utilisation is 1.3–15%, the cost per kilogram moved is set by the fleet ($176–$694) and moves by under 3% with the level, and energy is 500–664 kWh a year, higher under the marketplace because it drives farther. Version 2's suite E5, on the tangent plane with its speed-multiplier latency model and a five-vehicle mixed fleet, had put the 90th-percentile urgent delay of a teleoperated fleet at 48 h at 8 s [113]; the six-vehicle fleets here give 56.3 ± 13.4 h in-house and 40.2 ± 12.7 h under the marketplace, with the stops and interventions now modelled rather than fitted.

6.7 The fitted law and pad siting

The law. E20 pools every simulated year of E17 and E18 (one row per seed of each cell, 62,640 rows from 30 seeds) and fits, by ordinary least squares, three responses: the natural logarithm of the mean urgent delay, so that 100(eb − 1) is the percentage change per unit of a regressor; the share of urgent items delivered within 24 h; and the cost per kilogram moved, without and with an energy price of 500, 2,000 and 5,000 $/kWh. The regressors are the logarithm of the implied mean miss, the standoff D, the keep-out radius R, the stand-down T, the night fraction, the reference-cell utilisation u of the same fleet, rule and night setting (a group-level load covariate, not the row's own utilisation), the pad radius, the year's off-pad share (so that the fit can separate dispersion acting through off-pad events from any smooth effect), and the interactions ln miss × D, R × T, R × u, T × u, D × u and ln miss × u, with mechanism dummies (in-house the base) in the pooled fit and none in the per-mechanism fits. No column was constant in the pooled data. Confidence intervals come from a percentile bootstrap over seeds with 1,000 resamples in which every row of a drawn seed enters the normal equations with that seed's multiplicity, so the intervals respect the common-random-number pairing. This design differs from Section 4.2 in two regressors, the off-pad share and the pad radius, added when Section 6.3 showed that dispersion acts through the off-pad threshold.

The pooled fit on the logarithm of mean urgent delay has R² = 0.48 and a residual standard deviation of 0.59 (Table 12, Figure 11). Distance is what costs. Each kilometre of standoff adds 0.334 [0.312, 0.360] to the logarithm, that is +39.7% [+36.6%, +43.4%] per kilometre at the fit's origin; each kilometre of keep-out radius adds 0.114 [0.099, 0.129], +12.1% [+10.4%, +13.8%]; each hour of stand-down adds 0.023 [0.018, 0.028], +2.3% [+1.8%, +2.8%]. Dispersion does not: the coefficient on the logarithm of the mean miss is +0.015 [−0.004, +0.038] per e-fold (+1.6% [−0.4%, +3.9%]), the coefficient on the off-pad share +0.025 [−0.097, +0.139] per unit share and the coefficient on the pad radius +0.00016 [−0.00027, +0.00057] per metre, all three indistinguishable from zero; what Section 6.3 saw at 1.5 km appears here as the interaction of the miss with utilisation (+1.37 [+1.14, +1.58] per e-fold per unit utilisation) rather than as a main effect. The load interactions all shrink the distance effects: D × u is −0.74 [−1.18, −0.33], R × u −2.58 [−3.05, −2.04] and T × u −0.66 [−0.79, −0.52], so that at the Phase-3 marketplace's utilisation of 0.057 the keep-out radius costs 0.114 − 0.147 = −0.033 per kilometre, in agreement with the near-zero cells of Table 10, and the standoff 0.292 per kilometre; R × T is +0.0002 [−0.0019, +0.0019]. Pad siting. The siting table turns the standoff result into a trade a planner can read. For every E17 cell (fleet, rule, miss level, pad radius, standoff) it adds four terms per year: the hauling cost (operator time plus driving energy at 0, 500, 2,000 or 5,000 $/kWh; Modelled hauling, Assumed price); the amortised pad construction cost over a ten-year life (Assumed), $130, $229 or $548 million per pad at transport prices of $10,000, $100,000 and $300,000 per kilogram (Metzger and Autry [66]; Spec), that is 13.0, 22.9 or 54.8 million dollars a year, which does not depend on standoff in this version and therefore shifts totals, not minima; the expected plume damage, equal to landings a year times expected impacts per landing times a line-of-sight shield times a cost per impact, where the impacts per landing follow Wittal and colleagues' anchors of 8 at 1 km, 0.37 at 5 km and 0.10 at 10 km for a 10 t lander (Modelled) [89] with ln I linear in distance between the anchors and extrapolated to 0.5 km on the 1–5 km slope (11.7 at 0.5 km, 3.7 at 2 km, 1.7 at 3 km), the shield 0.1 (Assumed) where Table 2 says the terrain blocks pad A's line of sight to the base (2 km and beyond) and 1 where it does not, and the cost per impact 0.1, 1 or 10 million dollars (Assumed, swept); and a delay valuation, delivered items times mean delay times a value of time of 0, 100 or 1,000 $ per item-hour (Assumed, swept; a blended stand-in for the engine's per-priority values of $5,000, $1,000 and $100 an hour). The minimum-cost standoff is then read off for each of the 504 cell-and-pad-case groups under each of the 36 assumption sets, 18,144 minima in all (Table 13, Figure 12).

For the reference cell (Phase-2 default fleet, marketplace, classical landings, 50 m pad, eight landings a year) the hauling cost is 0.47, 0.48, 0.53, 0.56 and 0.63 million dollars a year at 0.5, 1, 2, 3 and 5 km with free energy and 1.61, 1.65, 1.92, 2.10 and 2.33 million at 2,000 $/kWh; the delay valuation at 100 $ per item-hour is 1.22–1.26 million (12.1–12.6 million at 1,000 $); and the expected damage at one million dollars an impact is 94.0, 64.0, 2.97, 1.38 and 0.30 million dollars a year (9.4–0.03 million at a tenth of that cost, 940–3.0 million at ten times it). The shield at 2 km does the work: the step from 1 to 2 km cuts the expected damage by a factor of 22 and the hauling cost rises by 0.05–0.27 million. The total at 2,000 $/kWh, one million dollars an impact and 100 $ per item-hour is 119.7, 89.8, 29.0, 27.6 and 26.8 million dollars a year with the $229 million pad (109.8–16.9 and 151.6–58.7 million with the cheaper and dearer pads), and the minimum lies at 5 km. Across the 36 assumption sets the reference cell's minimum is at 5 km in 27, at 3 km in three (free or 500 $/kWh energy with damage at 0.1 million an impact and delivery time valued at 100–1,000 $) and at 2 km in six (2,000 or 5,000 $/kWh with damage at 0.1 million, at any value of time); the pad case never moves it. Across all 18,144 minima, none lies at 0.5 or 1 km; 75.1% lie at 5 km, 19.0% at 2 km and 5.9% at 3 km. The pattern is simple to state. Once an impact costs a million dollars or more, the residual damage at 2 km (3.0 million a year in the reference cell) still exceeds the hauling and delay cost of going on to 5 km, so the farthest pad wins in 82.1–100% of groups whatever the energy price and value of time. At a tenth of that cost the damage left at 2 km (0.3 million) is of the order of the hauling increment, and the other two terms decide: with free energy and no value on delivery time 100% of groups still choose 5 km, with 500 $/kWh 88.7%; valuing delivery time at 100 $ per item-hour moves 59.5% of groups to 3 km at 500 $/kWh; at 1,000 $ per item-hour the pads come back to 2 km (48.2% of groups) and 3 km (51.8%) even with free energy and to 2 km in 75% at 500 $/kWh; and at 2,000 or 5,000 $/kWh the energy term alone puts 75–100% of groups at 2 km. By value of time alone, 2 km holds 14.6, 15.6 and 26.9% of minima at 0, 100 and 1,000 $ per item-hour. Dispersion and pad radius barely move the minima. The numbers that decide between 2, 3 and 5 km are therefore the ones NASA says it does not have, the impacts per landing and their cost, and we have swept them rather than guessed them; the one terrain fact that enters is that this ridge shields the base from a pad at 2 km, which is why 2 km, and not 1 km, is the near alternative.
