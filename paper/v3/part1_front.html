Where the Lander Lands: Landing Precision, Pad Standoff, Plume Keep-Out and Rover Autonomy Priced in Lunar Surface Logistics

Azizjon Rahimov
Roatify Research · Preprint, version 3, October 2026 · Code, data, terrain, policies, seeds and figure scripts: github.com/azizjonrahimov/lunar-logistics-research · Live site (3D world, lander lab, rover lab, simulator): azizjonrahimov.github.io/lunar-logistics-research

Abstract. Version 2 of this paper introduced LunarHaul†, a discrete-event testbed of cargo movement at a nine-site lunar south-pole base, verified against thirteen analytic and published references and cross-checked against an independent browser implementation, and used it to compare four dispatch rules for rover fleets owned by several companies. It found that pooling vehicles across owners matters only when the market is fragmented (with six owners sharing six vehicles the in-house 90th-percentile delay of urgent cargo is {E3_GAIN} h longer than the marketplace's; with one owner the two rules are indistinguishable); that any pooled rule meets a 90% same-day service level with four vehicles at Phase-2 demand where in-house fleets do not with eight, and that because the fleet is 99% of the cost a surface move costs about $300 per kilogram moved; that 1.6-tonne rovers can move 40% of Phase-2 landed mass, a 10-tonne hauler 88% and only a 15-tonne capability all of it; and that Earth teleoperation works at lunar latency and collapses at Mars latency. This version answers a question those results could not reach and NASA's documents leave blank. NASA writes that for the distance between pads and the base “a reduction in distance means a reduction in transfer time and risk” and gives planning bands rather than numbers (more than 1,000 m for blast ejecta, up to 5,000 m to aggregate elements); its December 2025 architecture document sets a “50m landing accuracy” target where the previous revision said “TBD”; NASA Kennedy writes that “no safe scientific or operational standoff distances have been defined for various landers”. No document says what landing precision, pad standoff, a plume keep-out rule or rover autonomy cost in delivery delay, service level or dollars. We put the base on NASA's Lunar Orbiter Laser Altimeter terrain of the Connecting Ridge at 5 m per post (Measured), place pads 0.5–5 km from the base on ground that meets the pad-slope rule, and add three models to the engine. A terminal-descent environment from NASA's SPLICE gate to the SPLICE touchdown box, with an altimeter that degrades below 30 m, is flown by a classical ZEM/ZEV law, by a policy cloned from that law and corrected with DAgger, and by proximal policy optimisation; thousands of dispersed landings per controller give the miss, touchdown-state and propellant distributions that the engine samples at every landing. A driving environment on the same terrain, with slip from lunar-gravity slope–slip curves by soil friction angle, energy calibrated to the Apollo Lunar Roving Vehicle, and a teleoperation model that reproduces NASA JSC's measured speeds of 3.24, 2.56, 2.03 and 1.76 km/h at 0, 4, 6 and 8 s of round-trip delay within 1.3%, gives per-leg time, energy and intervention distributions by autonomy level and lighting. Because NASA calls plume predictions “largely qualitative and uncertain”, we predict no ejecta: a keep-out radius (0.2–3 km) and a stand-down time (0.1–6 h) are swept, and vehicles inside the radius shelter where terrain blocks the line of sight to the pad. Across [[E17-E19: total simulated years]] simulated years with 30 common-random-number seeds per cell, we find that moving the pads from 1 km to 5 km changes the mean urgent delay by [[E17: paired difference in mean urgent delay, 5 km against 1 km, Phase-2 default fleet, marketplace]] h and the cost per kilogram moved by [[E17: paired difference in cost per kg moved, 5 km against 1 km, with and without an energy price]]; that landing dispersion [[E17: whether delay depends smoothly on sigma or through off-pad and failed landings, and the sigma at which the effect appears]]; that a 1 km keep-out with a 2 h stand-down costs [[E18: paired difference in mean urgent delay, R 1 km and T 2 h against no keep-out, Phase-3 default fleet]] h of urgent delay and [[E18: stand-down vehicle-hours per year, same cell]] vehicle-hours a year, [[E18: interaction with night and with fleet size, one clause]]; that teleoperated fleets at 8 s need [[E19: vehicles needed to match the supervised fleet's service level]]; and that a fitted law explains [[E20: R-squared of the pooled fit on log mean urgent delay]] of the variance in urgent delay with [[E20: the sign and size of the leading terms, one clause]]. The learned models also taught us something we did not expect: PPO under a Gaudet-style reward reached the touchdown box in [[E15: PPO baseline box rate]] of dispersed landings and traded box rate for return when fine-tuned, while cloning the classical law reached [[E15: BC box rate before DAgger]] before DAgger and [[E15: box rate of the DAgger policy]] after, against [[E15: classical box rate]] for the law itself. We release the terrain pack, both environments, the policies, the outcome and driving tables, the scenarios, seeds and figure scripts.

1. Introduction

Cargo lands at a pad and is needed somewhere else. NASA's Moon Base plan lands about 4 tonnes of cargo at the lunar south pole before 2029, about 60 tonnes in 2029–2032 and about 150 tonnes after that [1], and expects 2,000–10,000 kg a year of recurring logistics during the foundational phase (Spec) [3, 61]. Landing pads will sit away from the habitation zone because of plume ejecta, so every kilogram that lands must be moved, some of it as far as 5 km over slopes of up to 20° [2]. How far away the pads sit is therefore a logistics variable as much as a safety one. NASA says so: “a reduction in distance means a reduction in transfer time and risk” [60, 61], and its 2023 landing paper adds that precision landing “reduces post-landing travel distances between surface assets” [60]. The agency gives planning bands rather than numbers: more than 50 m to escape the lander's shadow, more than 1,000 m for blast ejecta, up to 5,000 m to aggregate elements in habitation zones (Spec) [2, 61]; its Architecture Definition Document says only that habitation points may be “separated from landing sites” [114]. No NASA document, and no paper we could find from 2000 to 2026, says what a kilometre of standoff costs in delivery delay or in dollars.

Landing precision has a requirement, a target and a record, and they span two orders of magnitude. The Human Landing System requirement HLS-R-0322 is 100 m (3σ) (Spec) [82]. NASA's SPLICE ground rules for precision landing ask for delivery within a 50 m radius with touchdown at under 2.0 m/s vertical, 1 m/s horizontal, 6° attitude and 2°/s rates, all 3σ and “derived from past human lunar missions” (Spec) [81]. The 2024 revision of the Architecture Definition Document left the accuracy of its precision-landing gap “TBD” [114]; the December 2025 revision fills it in: “Enable crew and cargo to be landed at lunar South Pole region exploration sites in various illumination conditions with 50m landing accuracy for FE segment with <10 minute initialization time to confirm position within 25m” (Spec) [115]. NASA does not say whether 50 m is a 1σ, 3σ or hard radius; its 2023 paper describes precision landing as touching down “within 50 to 100 meters of a given target” [60], and Blue Moon MK1 and Griffin advertise 100 m (Spec) [119, 121]. The flown record since 2023 is wider than any of these: SLIM about 10 m before its divert and 55–60 m after losing a nozzle (Measured) [85]; Blue Ghost 58 m from its targeted ellipse and about 118 m from the target centre (Measured) [86]; IM-2 between 250 and more than 400 m, on its side (Measured) [87]; IM-1 about 1.5 km, with a broken leg (Measured) [84]. Every tip-over or crash in that record traces to the altitude-sensing chain, not to guidance or propulsion [84, 87, 109]. Precision also buys pad area: the Artemis pad requirement was 200 m in diameter with an effort to reach 100 m, because 200 m “is not feasible with a single Starship payload” (Spec) [90], and the mass of a pad grows with the square of its radius.

The plume side has no number at all. NASA's 2011 guidance for heritage sites set a 2.0 km descent and landing exclusion radius (Spec) [64]; one of its authors has since said, “It's just a number we made up because we couldn't do any better at the time” [63]. NASA's 2023 landing paper states that the agency “currently lacks direct in-situ measurements of PSI phenomena, leaving predictions largely qualitative and uncertain” [60]; its 2023 plume poster says Apollo experience “cannot be extrapolated” to Human Landing Systems [97]; and the December 2025 Architecture Definition Document lists in-situ measurement of plume particle velocity and of landing-site alteration as open data gaps traced to the precision-landing objective [115]. NASA Kennedy's 2026 Lunar Innovation Park study writes that “no safe scientific or operational standoff distances have been defined for various landers” and then plans small landers about 20 m apart behind 2.5 m regolith berms (Spec) [62]; the Constellation outpost design had put the landing zone 2.2 km from and 290 m below the habitation zone (Spec) [96]. The first in-situ plume instrument, SCALPSS on Blue Ghost, has so far produced a terrain map flat to ±20 mm with one 60–80 mm depression of unattributed origin, and no erosion volume (Measured) [111]. The lunar surface community expects the decision on landing near or far from habitats by about 2027 [104]. Nobody has priced the alternatives.

The reason is structural. Four research traditions hold the pieces and never meet (Section 2). Campaign-level space logistics models treat the lunar surface as one node. Plume and pad engineering stops at a static standoff geometry or a pad construction cost. Rover autonomy and teleoperation studies stop at a single vehicle's speed. The young literature of agent-based and discrete-event base models has rovers but no landing dispersion and no plume. Learned controllers for landing and driving, which could supply the missing distributions, report touchdown states and tracking errors and never a delivered kilogram. The one quantity that would connect them, delivery delay or cost as a function of landing dispersion, pad standoff, keep-out rule and rover autonomy, has not been computed.

Version 2 of this testbed [113] could not compute it either, because it did not contain the variables. Its base sat on a tangent plane with pads at 1.7 and 2.1 km; every landing arrived exactly on its pad; there was no plume and no keep-out; slopes were three classes that multiplied travel time; and control latency entered only as a speed multiplier fitted to NASA's teleoperation table. Those were the right simplifications for a paper about dispatch rules. They are the wrong ones for a paper about where the lander lands.

We therefore extended the testbed in three places and asked four further questions, continuing the numbering of version 2.

  1. RQ6. How do landing dispersion σ and pad standoff D change urgent-cargo delay, the same-day service level and the cost per kilogram, and does dispersion act smoothly through traverse distance or through thresholds (off-pad landings, failed landings, pad mass)?
  2. RQ7. What does a plume keep-out rule cost, as a function of its radius R and stand-down time T, under each dispatch rule, with and without the lunar night, and at what fleet load does it begin to bind?
  3. RQ8. How does rover autonomy level, with interventions charged at the round-trip delay rather than at a co-located operator's six seconds, change the effective capacity of a fleet, and how many teleoperated vehicles equal one supervised one?
  4. RQ9. Can learned landing and driving controllers serve as stochastic generators inside a logistics simulation, and what do they produce relative to the classical laws they were trained against?

We answer them with simulation on real terrain. The base is placed on NASA's LOLA 5 m digital elevation model of the Connecting Ridge between Shackleton and de Gerlache craters (Measured) [95], with the nine sites and five sets of landing pads chosen by a stated rule from slope, illumination and line of sight. A terminal-descent environment in Gymnasium, parameterised from flown and specified landers and starting at NASA's SPLICE gate, is flown by a classical zero-effort-miss/zero-effort-velocity law, by a policy that imitates that law and is corrected with DAgger, and by proximal policy optimisation under a reward of the kind used in the published Mars landing work; each controller is run through thousands of dispersed landings, and the distributions of miss, touchdown state, propellant and time near the ground are what the engine samples at every landing. A driving environment on the same terrain, with a kinematic bicycle, lunar-gravity slip curves by soil friction angle, Apollo-calibrated energy, the lighting penalties NASA measured at the pole, and a teleoperation model that reproduces NASA JSC's speed-versus-latency measurements within 1.3%, is driven by a scripted expert over every leg between sites; its per-leg time, energy and intervention distributions are what the engine samples for every trip. A plume module turns each landing into a keep-out window of radius R and duration T, holds jobs at the boundary and sends vehicles inside the radius to a terrain-sheltered site. Both R and T are swept, not predicted. Our contributions are:

  • We give the first quantitative relation between landing dispersion, pad standoff, plume keep-out and rover autonomy and the cost of surface logistics: delay, same-day service level and cost per kilogram as a function of (σ, D, R, T, autonomy level, fleet, demand phase), fitted with bootstrap confidence intervals over common-random-number seeds, and tested against the hypothesis that dispersion acts through pad mass and tail events rather than smoothly through traverse distance. NASA's documents state the relation qualitatively and leave the number blank; we found no paper that fills it.
  • We introduce plume-event-aware dispatch: the keep-out as a time-varying constraint that interacts with anticipatory staging, the lunar night and multi-owner fleets, with terrain-chosen shelter sites, and we report its cost under each of the four dispatch rules.
  • We show that learned lander and rover controllers can serve as stochastic generators inside a discrete-event logistics simulation and report logistics-level effects of their output distributions, which the space literature has not done. We also report what training them taught us: under a Gaudet-style reward, proximal policy optimisation traded touchdown-box rate for return, and imitation of the classical law with DAgger did better, the opposite of the direction the imitation-learning literature predicted for this problem.

We are explicit about what we do not claim. We do not predict plume ejecta, a keep-out radius or a safe standoff; NASA calls such predictions “largely qualitative”, and we treat R and T as parameters and report the cost of each choice. We do not claim measured intervention rates: no interventions-per-kilometre figure exists for any lunar or Mars rover, and our rates come from a stated hazard model and from the learned driver's behaviour in simulation. Our slip–slope relation is a physics simulation validated against laboratory single-wheel tests on a 440 kg rover; Earth tests are known to be optimistic in lunar gravity, and no vehicle heavier than Yutu-2 has measured slip on the Moon. The lander is a 3-DoF point mass with a rate-limited thrust axis, not a 6-DoF vehicle. One site, one terrain tile and one base layout are simulated. Every physical number in this paper carries a flag the first time it appears: Measured (flight or laboratory data), Spec (a published design value or requirement), Modelled (produced by a model, including ours) or Assumed (our choice, swept where it matters), because the most common failure in the sources we read was a design assumption (a 2 km standoff, a 200 m pad, a 1 m/s touchdown) repeated until it read as a measurement.
