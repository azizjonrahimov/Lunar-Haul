2. Related work

2.1 Sharing tasks among robots

Gerkey and Matarić classified multi-robot task allocation by whether a robot does one task or several at a time, whether a task needs one robot or several, and whether tasks are assigned once or over time [1]. Our problem has single-task robots, tasks that need one robot or two, and assignment over time as crates land. For the instantaneous single-robot case the Hungarian method gives the optimum in polynomial time, and greedy assignment is within a factor of two of it. The time-extended version is strongly NP-hard. Gerkey and Matarić also judged that for fewer than about 200 robots a centralised assignment over a broadcast network is “likely the better choice” [1], which is the case for one base with a dozen robots, provided a central computer can hear them all.

Auctions are the standard way to share tasks without one. In a sequential single-item auction every robot bids its cost of adding each open task to its plan, the lowest bid wins one task, and the round repeats. Its total cost is at most twice the optimum when travel costs obey the triangle inequality, and in experiments it comes close to combinatorial auctions [2, 3]. The consensus-based bundle algorithm (CBBA) lets each robot build a bundle of several tasks greedily and then settle conflicts with its neighbours only; it is conflict-free and tolerates inconsistent information [4]. Later papers from its authors' group report that it reaches the sequential greedy solution, and that this solution guarantees half the optimal score when each added task is worth no more than the one before [5, 74]; we could not read the original proof [4] and quote the bound as those two papers state it. Our own derivation (Proposition 1, Appendix B) gives one half when each robot takes one task or the limit on bundle length does not bind, and one third when it binds. These are bounds on a score, which for our cost auction means value below a ceiling bid; they are not bounds on cost or on waiting, where a greedy round can be arbitrarily worse than the best assignment. An asynchronous version drops the global rounds [6]. Market-based architectures such as TraderBots let robots trade tasks to raise their own profit, with local leaders planning centrally when that pays [7]. Our on-Moon auction is a sequential single-item auction whose winning bids are rebroadcast, with CBBA's rule for settling conflicts and an optional bundle depth (Section 3.7).

2.2 Allocation when messages are lost

Otte, Kuhlman and Sofge compared six auctions under random and bursty message loss, in simulation and on quadrotors [8]. The ranking of the auctions changed with the loss rate, single-round auctions did worst because a robot that misses the only round sits idle, and rebroadcasting earlier winners in later rounds helped every multi-round auction. Nayak and colleagues compared five decentralised allocators under three loss models and found that all degrade, and that the best algorithm at high communication is not the best at none [9]. Lott and Honary measured the bundle depth of CBBA-type allocators under 25 % loss: the asynchronous CBBA's penalty in total travel grew from 19.7 % at depth 1 to 64.7 % at depth 12, and the best depth moved from 12 with perfect links to 2 under loss [10]. Their second benchmark of six allocators over 25 communication conditions found the best methods losing about 11–13 % of makespan between perfect delivery and 40 % loss [11]. Algorithms for very low communication exist [12].

These studies share a design. Robots move on a grid or an open plane, links fail at random or in bursts, every robot belongs to one team, and each algorithm is compared with itself under perfect links, so the cost of decentralisation relative to a central dispatcher is not measured. Lott and Honary also showed that settings chosen under one communication condition can be wrong under another, and that tuning and evaluation need separate scenarios [10]; we follow that rule (Section 4.3).

2.3 Tasks that need two robots

Assigning tasks that need several robots at once is a set-partitioning problem and strongly NP-hard [1]; by the abstracts of two later analyses, which is all we read of them, it is in general hard to approximate [14, 15]. A later taxonomy, also read in abstract, adds the dependencies between schedules: when two robots must meet, the task's value depends on both robots' plans [13]. Market methods handle complex tasks by letting robots bid on decompositions [16], and a coupled-constraint extension of CBBA handles tasks whose value depends on when or whether another task is done [5]. Two-rover cargo carrying has been demonstrated on lunar-analogue terrain: two 800 kg rovers carried a 475 kg, 5.5 m load with a mean separation error of 9.2 cm, over 13.8 KB/s of traffic between them, in the unit that paper gives [17]. Those rovers belonged to one team and coordinated at the level of control. With a dozen robots and lifts by pairs there are few pairs to enumerate. The hard part is the meeting: both robots must arrive, and under message loss one can wait for a partner that never comes. We found no study of lifts by robots of different owners, or of what a meeting protocol costs under message loss at the level of a logistics operation.

2.4 Learned allocation

Learned allocators either output assignments directly, such as graph-attention policies trained by reinforcement learning or imitation ([21]; [22], read in abstract), or learn scores that a classical solver then assigns. In the second group, a learned incentive feeds the Hungarian method [20], a graph network approximates CBBA's score [19], and a 2026 study replaced CBBA's hand-built bid with a policy trained by proximal policy optimisation while keeping the auction and consensus phases [18]. That study is the closest prior work to our learned bids. We read only its abstract, which describes cooperative robots and does not mention message loss; we could not check its communication model. Ride-hailing dispatch at scale adds a learned value of a driver's position to a combinatorial matching, and its deployed gains are in the low single digits of percent: by its abstract, over 1.3 % more driver income in A/B tests and up to 5.3 % in major metrics after deployment [23]. Neural solvers for routing problems generalise poorly beyond the sizes they were trained on [25], and operations researchers have asked for evaluations against strong classical heuristics on the same instances and hardware [26]. Imitation of an expert by dataset aggregation (DAgger) avoids the compounding errors of plain cloning [24]. We ran only the first iteration of that procedure, found that it left nothing to imitate, and did not run the aggregation loop; the learned bids come from a search on the simulated objective alone (Section 3.10). We keep the auction unchanged and test a network against a linear model with the same inputs.

2.5 Fleets of several owners

Robotics allocation usually assumes cooperative robots of one owner. Transport research has measured what fragmentation among owners costs. Splitting the demand for mobility on demand among platforms that do not coordinate raises the cost of rebalancing, and unequal market shares make it grow faster [27]. With data from five cities, Kondor and colleagues found that each extra operator raises the fleet needed by a factor that ranges from 2.5 % of demand in Manhattan to 67 % in Curitiba, and that in Singapore with shares of 50, 35 and 15 % the smallest operator's fleet grows by 48 % against 8.5 % for the largest [28]. In freight, joint route planning among carriers saves about 20–30 % [51], and carriers rank a trusted coordinator and a fair split of the benefits as the main obstacles [29]. Open-RMF lets robots of unrelated vendors share a traffic schedule and bid for tasks across fleets without changing their own fleet managers [30]; it is the closest working system to what we simulate, and it assumes a building network that is always up.

2.6 Lunar surface autonomy and networks

CADRE, NASA's three-rover technology demonstration, has not flown: its lander, Intuitive Machines' third mission, was planned for the first quarter of 2027 according to a report of 15 September 2026 [73]. Its design runs a central planner on a leader elected among the rovers and the lander's base station, with execution on every rover [31, 71]. The planner schedules tasks greedily in order of priority. There is no auction, a choice its authors make to keep messages few on lossy surface links [71]. The rovers are awake for 25 minutes in every 30, and while they are awake the loss of the leader costs at most 10 s before planning resumes on a new one [71]. CADRE has one owner and three identical rovers of under 10 kg that share at least 1 Mbit/s of mesh radio, and its team reports packet loss from motor interference while the rovers drive [72]; they explore, and there is no cargo. By its abstract, a 2024 lunar variant of the consensus-based auction models payload, priority, lunar night and cooperative tasks for one owner's robots [33], and an auction hierarchy has been simulated for regolith mining [34]. In the DARPA Subterranean Challenge, robots relayed data for one another through mesh radios, dropped relays and carried data out of range [35]; the winning team deployed single-robot autonomy only, citing the field testing that multi-robot coordination would need [36]. Lunar surface traffic has been raised as a regulatory question without algorithms [50].

NASA's LunaNet specification standardises interoperability among lunar users at the network layer, with Internet protocols and the Bundle Protocol version 7 for delay-tolerant networking; the radio link between two surface vehicles is left to bilateral agreement [39]. Delay-tolerant networking became an operational NASA service in 2026 [49]. The relay programme states its case as coverage: 17–43 % a year at candidate south-pole sites from direct-to-Earth links alone, 80–86 % with one relay [38]. None of these documents says how vehicles of different owners should share work, or what happens to a shared operation while the base cannot see Earth.

What two vehicles of different owners say to each other is the layer these documents leave open. The space-link standards fix what lies below it: packets [55], frames for proximity and long-haul links [58, 59], link security [56], delay-tolerant bundles [60] and, since 2024, Wi-Fi 6 and LTE as surface radios [57]. LunaNet's own messages go from a service provider to a user (almanac, alerts, search and rescue), and its security document was still a placeholder in version 5 [39]. The closest precedent for mixed makers is the factory floor. VDA 5050, at version 3.0.0 since March 2026, defines order, state, connection and factsheet topics between a fleet controller and mobile robots of any vendor, over a broker that is always reachable [64]; the MassRobotics standard reduces the agreed minimum to an identity report and a status report [65]. MAVLink makes the same split for drones, a broadcast stream plus command-and-acknowledge exchanges, with an optional 13 B signature [66]. CADRE avoids the question by having one owner: its rovers synchronise state to the leader and do not negotiate [71]. The protocol of Section 3.12 takes its message set from these precedents and its capability description from VDA 5050's factsheet and from the tool descriptions of the Model Context Protocol [67]. It differs in three conditions: the parties are peers with different owners, the link can be gone for hours, and every message is authenticated on a channel of a few kilobits a second.

2.7 Our earlier work and the gap

Our first paper built a discrete-event simulation of cargo movement at a south-pole base and showed that one marketplace offering every job to every vehicle keeps cargo waiting far less than owners who use only their own vehicles [40]. The second added landing precision, pad standoff, a plume keep-out and rover autonomy on NASA's 5 m terrain of the Connecting Ridge [41]. The third computed the base's Earth-link windows from the ephemerides of SPICE, NASA's planetary geometry toolkit, and laser-altimeter terrain and priced their gaps for a fleet driven or supervised from Earth [42]. All three assumed one dispatcher that sees everything.

Table 1 sets the closest works against the features our question needs. We did not find a study that compares allocation by robots on the Moon with a shared dispatcher on Earth limited to real terrain-masked Earth-link windows, for vehicles of several owners, over a radio mesh computed from real terrain, with lifts by pairs of robots from different owners, measured in cargo delay. Each feature on its own is published; the comparison is what this paper adds.
