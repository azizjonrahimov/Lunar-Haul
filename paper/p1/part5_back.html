6. Discussion

6.1 What the results say about the question

We asked whether the gain from sharing vehicles among owners survives when the decisions move from Earth onto the robots. It does, and the measurements split the gain into three parts that behave differently. The first is capacity. Most of the gap between owners working alone and a pooled fleet, {{P1-H1-CAPACITY-SHARE}} of it at the middle load, is cargo that owners alone cannot move at all: owner C's small vehicles cannot lift its crates, and no single owner can pair its robots for most team items (Section 5.2). This part needs no dispatcher anywhere. It needs an agreement that lets one owner's rover carry another owner's cargo and lets two owners' rovers lift together. The second part is where the pooled decision is made. Keeping it on Earth cost {{P1-H2-STRICT-MINUS-O}} of mean urgent wait per year when the dispatcher stopped at every blackout, {{P1-H2-STRICT-OVER-O-RATIO}} times the wait under uninterrupted dispatch, and the cost appeared in every start year in which the base saw Earth less than 60 % of the time (Section 5.3). Uploading plans before each blackout left {{P1-PLAN-MINUS-O}} of it and an 80 % relay left {{P1-RELAY08-MINUS-O}}, less, but more than our equivalence margin allows. Moving the decision onto the robots removed it: the auction among the robots, which hear one another only over the terrain and lose one message in ten, differed from the full-information marketplace by {{P1-H3A-DIFF}}, a difference not distinguishable from zero and a small fraction of what the Earth-bound dispatcher lost (Section 5.4). The third part is the quality of the bids. A learned linear correction with {{P1-LIN-N-WEIGHTS}} weights cut the auction's urgent wait by {{P1-H4-LIN-PCT-30-ABS}} at the middle load, and a network trained the same way added nothing (Section 5.6). {{P1-H6-ABSTRACT-CLAUSE}} (Section 5.7).

Not every registered expectation held, and the failures say something. The equivalence test of the auction against the marketplace is reported as it came out in Section 5.4: the point difference is {{P1-H3A-DIFF}}, the 90 % interval {{P1-H3A-CI90}}, the margin ±{{P1-H2-MARGIN}}. Ten percent of a mean of {{P1-O-URGENT-30}} is a narrow margin in hours, which is why an interval that includes zero can still fail to show equivalence, and why the practical reading is the one in the previous paragraph rather than a verdict on the hypothesis. The 80 % relay missed its margin for the same reason: its loss is small next to the strict dispatcher's and still larger than a tenth of the marketplace's mean. The network's failure to beat the hand-built bids says more about how we trained it than about networks (Section 7). We also expected learning to help nothing at light load, {{P1-H4-LIGHT-CLAUSE}}.

6.2 Comparison with earlier studies

Studies of allocation under message loss compare an algorithm with itself under perfect links. The best decentralised allocators in Lott and Honary's benchmark lose 11–13 % of makespan between perfect delivery and 40 % loss [10, 11]. Our comparison is different in kind: the auction at 10 % loss is set against a dispatcher with perfect information, and the quantity is cargo delay, not makespan. On that harder comparison it differed from the reference by {{P1-H3A-PCT}}. The slope against loss, which is the quantity those benchmarks report, is measured in Section 5.5. NASA's CADRE rovers coordinate through an elected leader [31, 32]. Our leader regime, which runs the marketplace on the base computer over the same lossy radio, came within {{P1-LEADER-MINUS-O}} of the marketplace, so on this terrain, with a mast that most robots can see, the two on-Moon designs are interchangeable at this loss.

Transport research measures what fragmentation among operators costs when every vehicle can serve every customer: the cost is slack, and the smallest operator pays most of it [27, 28]. On the Moon the fleets are heterogeneous, and fragmentation removes capability, not only slack: {{P1-H1-CAPACITY-SHARE}} of the gap we measured is cargo that the owner's own fleet cannot lift. The direction for the smallest owner is the same as Kondor and colleagues report, and the per-owner numbers are in Section 5.2. Open-RMF lets robots of unrelated vendors share tasks and a traffic schedule, over a building network that is always up [30]; our regimes with an uninterrupted link to Earth are the lunar version of that assumption, and the strict regime is what happens when it fails.

Published learned allocators gain a few percent over their baselines: 1.3–5.3 % in deployed ride-hailing dispatch [23], and the learned bid inside CBBA of [18] is evaluated against CBBA's own bid under perfect links. Our {{P1-H4-LIN-PCT-30-ABS}} is larger, and the reason is the baseline, not the learner. The linear correction beat not only the hand-built bids but the full-information marketplace that uses the same bid rule, so it measures the weakness of that rule under this demand; the hand-built rule was written, not tuned by search on the objective, while the linear model was. A stronger hand-built rule would shrink the gain, which is the comparison that Accorsi and colleagues ask for [26], and which we only partly made by testing the linear model against the network. What we add to that literature is the negative result for the network against a linear model with the same inputs, and the finding that imitation of the reference dispatcher teaches a bidder nothing when the reference uses the same rule.

6.3 Implications

For whoever runs a south-pole base, the results order the options. A relay to Earth buys less than moving the dispatch decision onto the robots: the relay at 80 % left {{P1-RELAY08-MINUS-O}} of extra urgent wait, the robots' auction a difference not distinguishable from zero, and the robots' auction keeps working at the oxygen plant, which no relay reaches. A dispatcher on Earth with plans uploaded before each blackout is an acceptable interim design at the lightest load, where every pooled regime except the strict one sits within {{P1-POOLED-SPREAD-10}} of the others, and today's planned traffic sits at that load. The value of on-Moon dispatch grows with traffic.

For the owners, the agreement to share matters more than the software that shares. {{P1-H1-CAPACITY-SHARE}} of the gain is capacity that only pooling unlocks, and it is available with any dispatcher; the rest depends on where and how well the dispatcher decides. Who pays whom for a lift, and what keeps an owner from misreporting its costs, are contract and market questions, taken up in the companion paper on the vehicles, the network and the market [44]. For the designers of LunaNet [39], the traffic that matters here is robot-to-robot: bids, awards, meeting commitments and relays of all three, which no current specification standardises; the message volumes and the computer each robot needs are measured in [44]. For the word “neural”, the gain from learning came from {{P1-LIN-N-WEIGHTS}} weights that an operator can read, and the safety check on each vehicle decides whether a move is allowed whatever the bids say [45]; a lunar fleet does not need an opaque planner to get the gain we measured.

6.4 Limitations

Section 7 sets out the limitations in full. The main ones are that the radio model is geometric, that one site, one terrain tile and one demand model are simulated, that the robots' lost-link rule is our assumption, that the full-information marketplace is a reference and not an optimal dispatcher, and that the network was trained with less freedom than the linear model. The comparisons between regimes that never wait for Earth rest on 30 paired seeds, which is enough to show the differences we found and not enough to show equivalence within 10 % of a small mean.

6.5 Future work

Three extensions follow directly. The first is to train the network end to end on the simulated objective and from the linear policy's starting point, so that the question of whether nonlinearity buys anything is answered rather than left open. The second is a second site and a second terrain tile, with a link budget in the radio model, to learn whether the auction's result depends on the Connecting Ridge's mast coverage. The third is a hardware test: the bidding, the meeting protocol and the lost-link rule on rovers of the CADRE class over a real radio on an analogue site, which would measure what our simulation cannot, the message losses and timings of real links. Beyond these, the companion papers take the roads, the separation rules and the safety check [45], and the payments, the audits and the hardware sizing [44].

7. Limitations

The radio model is geometric. A link exists when two antennas see each other within a set range, with no link budget, no fading and no interference, and the range and clearance are provisional values that Section 5.5 varies. Real links on the ridge will fail in ways this model does not produce, and the message-loss rates we impose stand in for all of them.

One base, one terrain tile, one demand model. The base layout, the terrain, the cargo mix and the arrival process are those of our earlier papers [40–42]; three levels of demand are simulated, of which the lightest is the closest to today's plans. Nothing here says how the results change at another site, and the mast's coverage of the ridge is part of why the on-Moon regimes work.

The lost-link rule is ours. Robots that nobody hears drive to a rally point that the base's mast can see (Section 3.9). Without that rule the robots' network falls apart on this terrain, and the rule's cost is part of every on-Moon result. A base would need some such rule, and it need not be ours.

The marketplace is a reference, not a bound. It is the auction's own bid rule given perfect information and no dependence on Earth; it is not an optimal dispatcher, and the learned linear bids beat it. “Within 10 % of the marketplace” therefore means within 10 % of a good hand-built dispatcher, not of the best possible one.

Owners are honest and roads are free. Owners report their costs truthfully and accept the assignments they win, and vehicles do not interfere with one another on the road. Both assumptions are removed in the companion papers [44, 45], and the delay that rules of the road add is measured there.

The network was given a narrow test. Imitation left the network's two hidden layers at their random initial values, and the search on the objective adjusted only its {{P1-CEM-MLP-N-SEARCHED}} output weights, while the linear model's search covered all {{P1-LIN-N-WEIGHTS}} of its weights (Section 3.10). The network was therefore a linear model on fixed random features of the inputs, searched with fewer candidates per weight than the linear model. Its failure to beat the hand-built bids is no evidence that a network helps as trained here; it is no evidence that a network cannot help. Two tests would settle it: training all {{P1-MLP-N-PARAMS}} weights end to end on the simulated objective, with a gradient-free method such as an evolution strategy or with a policy-gradient method, and starting the network from the learned linear policy, so that it begins at the linear model's result and can only add what nonlinearity buys. Neither was run, and the pre-registration froze both models before the main runs (Section 4.6).

The simulation is checked, not validated against a lunar fleet. The engine's checks (Section 3.11) and those of our earlier papers compare it with closed-form cases, degenerate settings and its own earlier versions. No multi-owner fleet operates on the Moon, so no measured delay exists to validate against, and the absolute waits in Table 5 should be read as model outputs under stated assumptions; the paired differences between regimes are the result.

Censored waits. Cargo not delivered by the end of a simulated year carries the wait it had accumulated by then, so the primary outcome is a censored mean that understates the true wait of what owners alone leave behind, and the delivered-only means in Table 5 condition on delivery and so compare different sets of items. We report both and test neither as a substitute for the other; the treatment is the analogue, for a simulation, of reporting how missing observations were handled.

Two defects were found in the runs and corrected before the analysis. Regimes that never wait for Earth give the same run for a seed whatever the start year, so their second block repeated seeds; comparisons between such regimes use the first block only (n = 30), and an interim reading that counted the repeats is withdrawn (Section 4.6). The auction's ordering of robots depended on a per-process hash, which made runs under message loss valid draws but not bit-reproducible; the order was fixed and the affected runs repeated, and the repeated runs changed no result. Both are recorded with dates in the pre-registration file [43].

8. Conclusion

This paper asked whether the gain from pooling the vehicles of several owners at a lunar south-pole base survives when dispatch moves from Earth onto the robots. On a simulated base with real vehicle classes, Earth-link windows and terrain, it does. A pooled dispatcher that stops when Earth sets costs {{P1-H2-STRICT-MINUS-O}} of mean urgent wait per year at the middle load, in every start year with poor Earth visibility; planning ahead and a relay recover most of that; an auction among the robots, at 10 % message loss, recovers all of it. Most of the value of pooling, {{P1-H1-CAPACITY-SHARE}} of the gap, is cargo that no single owner can move, and lifts by two owners' rovers unlock it. A learned correction to the bids with {{P1-LIN-N-WEIGHTS}} weights cut the wait by a further {{P1-H4-LIN-PCT-30-ABS}}; a small neural network trained the same way did not.

For practice, the order of value is an agreement to share first, dispatch by the robots on the Moon second, a relay to Earth third and a learned bid last. For research, the negative results, a network no better than a linear model and imitation of a same-rule reference that teaches nothing, count as much as the positive ones, and the pre-registered plan shows which is which. The results hold for one base, one terrain, a geometric radio and honest owners. A fleet of several owners can work together without Earth. What it needs most is permission to.
