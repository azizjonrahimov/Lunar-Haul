6. Discussion

6.1 What the results say about the question

We asked whether the gain from sharing vehicles among owners survives when the decisions move from Earth onto the robots. In this model it does, and the measurements separate three things that behave differently. The first is capability, and it is set by the scenario. In this fleet the gain from pooling is cargo that owners alone had not delivered by the end of the year: owner C's small vehicles cannot lift its crates, and no single owner can pair its robots for most team items (Section 5.2). On cargo that both deliver, owners alone with Earth always in view waited {{P1-H1-DELIV-DIFF}} longer. Lifts by pairs of different owners' rovers account for {{P1-H1-TEAM-SHARE}} of the censored difference, and single-robot jobs taken by another owner's rover, together with the pooled dispatcher, for the rest. The design cannot separate the dispatcher's share of that rest, but the pooled regimes that differ only in their dispatcher sit within a few hours of one another (Section 5.1), so it can be little. This part of the gain needs no dispatcher in any particular place. It needs an agreement that lets one owner's rover carry another owner's cargo and lets two owners' rovers lift together. The second part is where the pooled decision is made. Keeping it on Earth cost {{P1-H2-STRICT-MINUS-O}} of mean urgent wait per year when the dispatcher stopped at every blackout, {{P1-H2-STRICT-OVER-O-RATIO}} times the wait under uninterrupted dispatch, and the cost appeared in every start year in which the base saw Earth less than 60 % of the time (Section 5.3). The Earth-bound dispatcher's loss comes from the blackouts. While Earth is down, nothing is assigned; vehicles finish their current job and wait, and cargo that lands in the meantime queues at the pads (Section 5.3). Uploading plans before each blackout left {{P1-PLAN-MINUS-O}} of it and an 80 % relay left {{P1-RELAY08-MINUS-O}}, less, but more than our equivalence margin allows. Moving the decision onto the robots left none that the data could detect, though not none that they could exclude: the auction among the robots, which hear one another only over the terrain and lose one message in ten, kept urgent cargo waiting {{P1-H3A-DIFF}} than the full-information marketplace, a difference not distinguishable from zero and a small fraction of what the Earth-bound dispatcher lost (Section 5.4). Two things qualify that reading: the auction's robots are sent back towards the mast when unheard and the marketplace's are not, and in the model the best bid of a round always wins whatever is lost (Sections 5.4 and 3.7). The third part is the quality of the bids. A learned linear correction with {{P1-LIN-N-WEIGHTS}} weights, registered as the secondary model, cut the auction's urgent wait by {{P1-H4-LIN-PCT-30-ABS}} at the middle load, and the registered network added nothing (Section 5.6). Beyond these, {{P1-H6-ABSTRACT-CLAUSE}} (Section 5.7).

Not every registered expectation held, and the failures say something. The equivalence test of the auction against the marketplace is reported as it came out in Section 5.4: the point estimate is {{P1-H3A-DIFF}} than the marketplace, the 90 % interval {{P1-H3A-CI90}}, the margin ±{{P1-H2-MARGIN}}. Ten percent of a mean of {{P1-O-URGENT-30}} is a narrow margin in hours, which is why an interval that includes zero can still fail to show equivalence, and why the practical reading is the one in the previous paragraph rather than a verdict on the hypothesis. The 80 % relay missed its margin for the same reason: its loss is small next to the strict dispatcher's and still larger than a tenth of the marketplace's mean. The network's failure to beat the hand-built bids says more about how we trained it than about networks (Section 7). Its training gave it less chance than the linear model: imitation left its hidden layers at their initial values and the search adjusted only its output layer (Section 3.10), so this is no evidence that a network helps as trained here, and no evidence that a network cannot help.

The protocol work puts a size on what moving the decisions costs in messages. By calculation, the radio budget of {{P1-E53-NB-KBPS}} that the companion paper assumed [44] holds for 20 rovers at one authenticated position report a second on a scheduled channel, counting packets before link framing and coding; it fails at ten reports a second, on random access, and when reports are relayed (Section 5.8). Away from the base the limit is reach before it is rate: spread over the routes, fewer than two rovers in three had a path to the base at a fleet of 20. In the replay of a month's messages, either transport rarely lost a job, and the difference between them is mostly the baseline's cap of five tries; the return to the last link point mattered for what the base knows: {{P1-E54-AOI-CLAUSE}} (Section 5.9). The propositions of Appendix B say how far the rest can be argued without an experiment, and Table 12 says which is which.

6.2 Comparison with earlier studies

Studies of allocation under message loss compare an algorithm with itself under perfect links. The best decentralised allocators in Lott and Honary's benchmark lose 11–13 % of makespan between perfect delivery and 40 % loss [10, 11]. Our comparison is different in kind: the auction at 10 % loss is set against a dispatcher with perfect information, and the quantity is cargo delay, not makespan. On that harder comparison it differed from the reference by {{P1-H3A-PCT}}. The slope against loss, which is the quantity those benchmarks report, is measured in Section 5.5. NASA's CADRE rovers are designed to coordinate through an elected leader with a greedy scheduler and no auction [31, 71]. Our leader regime is not CADRE's planner: it runs our marketplace on the base computer over the same lossy radio, with a 0.5 h election. It came within {{P1-LEADER-MINUS-O}} of the marketplace, so on this terrain, with a mast that most robots can see, our two on-Moon regimes did not differ measurably at this loss, though equivalence could not be shown (Section 5.5). The marketplace is the auction's own bid rule given perfect information, which is why we call it a reference and not a bound (Section 5.4).

Transport research measures what fragmentation among operators costs when every vehicle can serve every customer: the cost is slack, and the smallest operator pays most of it [27, 28]. On the Moon the fleets are heterogeneous, and fragmentation removes capability, not only slack: what separates owners alone from the pooled fleet here is cargo that owners alone had not delivered by the end of the year, a result of giving one owner no cargo rover. Kondor and colleagues found that the smallest operator pays most for fragmentation [28]; here the owner that pays most is the one without a cargo rover. Owner C's urgent cargo waited {{P1-H1-OWNER-C-DIFF}} longer per year alone than pooled, against {{P1-H1-OWNER-A-DIFF}} and {{P1-H1-OWNER-B-DIFF}} for owners A and B (Section 5.2). What an owner lacks on the Moon is a vehicle class, not fleet size. Open-RMF lets robots of unrelated vendors share tasks and a traffic schedule, over a building network that is always up [30]; our regimes with an uninterrupted link to Earth are the lunar version of that assumption, and the strict regime is what happens when it fails. Most of what separates the pooled regimes is when cargo moves, while what separates them from owners alone is whether it moves at all (Section 5.1). Refuelling the hoppers changed nothing because a hopper carries 10 kg and the cargo that waits is heavy (Section 5.10).

Published learned allocators gain a few percent over their baselines: 1.3–5.3 % in deployed ride-hailing dispatch [23], and the learned bid inside CBBA of [18] is evaluated against CBBA's own bid under perfect links. Our {{P1-H4-LIN-PCT-30-ABS}} is larger, and the reason is the baseline, not the learner. The linear correction beat not only the hand-built bids but the full-information marketplace that uses the same bid rule, so it measures the weakness of that rule under this demand; the hand-built rule was written, not tuned by search on the objective, while the linear model was. A stronger hand-built rule would shrink the gain, which is the comparison that Accorsi and colleagues ask for [26], and which we only partly made by testing the linear model against the network. What we add to that literature is the negative result for the network against a linear model with the same inputs, and the finding that imitation of the reference dispatcher teaches a bidder nothing when the reference uses the same rule. The linear model has 13 weights, which we have not interpreted (Section 5.6). We could have seen that imitation would teach nothing from the definitions, before collecting {{P1-IMIT-N-ROWS}} robot-item rows, and report it so others do not repeat it (Section 5.11).

6.3 Implications

For whoever runs a south-pole base, the results order the options. A relay that is always up equals the full-information marketplace run for run, and the robots' auction was not distinguishable from either. Only a relay that is up 80 % of the time did worse than the auction: it left {{P1-RELAY08-MINUS-O}} of extra urgent wait against the marketplace, and the auction's wait was {{P1-AUCTION-MINUS-RELAY08}} shorter than its own ({{P1-AUCTION-MINUS-RELAY08-CI}}; an unregistered comparison, Section 5.4). A dispatcher on Earth with plans uploaded before each blackout is an acceptable interim design at the lightest load, where every pooled regime except the strict one sits within {{P1-POOLED-SPREAD-10}} of the others, and today's planned traffic sits at that load. The value of on-Moon dispatch grows with traffic.

For the owners, the agreement to share matters more than the software that shares. In this fleet the gain is cargo that owners alone had not delivered by the end of the year, and it is available with any dispatcher that pools the fleet; what the dispatcher's place and quality add is the smaller sum measured in Sections 5.3 to 5.6. Who pays whom for a lift, and what keeps an owner from misreporting its costs, are contract and market questions, taken up in the companion paper on the vehicles, the network and the market [44]. For the designers of LunaNet [39], the traffic that matters here is robot-to-robot: bids, awards, meeting commitments and relays of all three, which no current specification standardises. Section 3.12 is one way to write that layer down, small enough to count: {{P1-E53-PER-ROVER-1HZ}} per rover at one position report a second. The computer each robot needs is measured in [44]. The gain from learning needed no neural network: it came from a linear model of {{P1-LIN-N-WEIGHTS}} weights, and the safety check on each vehicle decides whether a move is allowed whatever the bids say [45]; a lunar fleet does not need an opaque planner to get the gain we measured.

6.5 Future work

Two results are not in this paper. The six cells outside the training range and the guard runs of Table 6 have not been rerun on the corrected engine. Version 0.2 of the protocol, which adds a frame, a time-scale identifier and a covariance to the position report, has no sizes yet. Four control runs would settle points left open here: drop and retry without a cap on its tries, the marketplace with the same return-to-coverage move as the auction, bundle depths 2 and 5 without message loss, and an ownership split in which every owner can lift its own cargo. Three extensions follow directly. The first is to train the network end to end on the simulated objective and from the linear policy's starting point, so that the question of whether nonlinearity buys anything is answered rather than left open. The second is a second site and a second terrain tile, with a link budget in the radio model, to learn whether the auction's result depends on the Connecting Ridge's mast coverage. The third is a hardware test: the bidding, the meeting protocol and the lost-link rule on rovers of the CADRE class over a real radio on an analogue site, which would measure what our simulation cannot, the message losses and timings of real links. For the protocol the next steps are a second implementation written from the schema alone and run against the conformance vectors, the messages on real radios through a channel emulator, and the lost-link return driven inside the engine. CADRE offers a check that needs nobody's permission: set our leader regime to one owner, three identical rovers, 25-minute wake cycles and a 10 s election, and see whether it reproduces the published behaviours, at most 10 s without a planner after the leader is lost and two-rover operation when one rover is excluded [71]; then run our auction on that case and count its messages against the leader's, which CADRE's authors expect to be fewer; then compare both with flight data once the mission has flown. Beyond these, the companion papers take the roads, the separation rules and the safety check [45], and the payments, the audits and the hardware sizing [44].

7. Limitations

The radio model is geometric. A link exists when two antennas see each other within a set range, with no link budget, no fading and no interference, and the range and clearance are provisional values that Section 5.5 varies. Real links on the ridge will fail in ways this model does not produce, and the message-loss rates we impose stand in for all of them.

One base, one terrain tile, one demand model. The base layout, the terrain, the cargo mix and the arrival process are those of our earlier papers [40–42]; three levels of demand are simulated, of which the lightest is the closest to today's plans. Nothing here says how the results change at another site, and the mast's coverage of the ridge is part of why the on-Moon regimes work.

The lost-link rule is ours. Robots that nobody hears drive to a rally point that the base's mast can see (Section 3.9). Without that rule the robots' network falls apart on this terrain. The rule is part of every on-Moon result and not of the reference: the marketplace has no such move, and where the radio reaches every robot the auction's small advantage over it disappears (Section 5.4), so the rule looks more like a help to the on-Moon regimes than a cost. The marketplace was not run with the same move. A base would need some such rule, and it need not be ours.

The marketplace is a reference, not a bound. It is the auction's own bid rule given perfect information and no dependence on Earth; it is not an optimal dispatcher, and the learned linear bids beat it. “Within 10 % of the marketplace” therefore means within 10 % of a good hand-built dispatcher, not of the best possible one.

Owners are honest and roads are free. Owners report their costs truthfully and accept the assignments they win, and vehicles do not interfere with one another on the road. Both assumptions are removed in the companion papers [44, 45], and the delay that rules of the road add is measured there.

The network was given a narrow test. Imitation left the network's two hidden layers at their random initial values, and the search on the objective adjusted only its {{P1-CEM-MLP-N-SEARCHED}} output weights, while the linear model's search covered all {{P1-LIN-N-WEIGHTS}} of its weights (Section 3.10). The network was therefore a linear model on fixed random features of the inputs, searched with fewer candidates per weight than the linear model. Its failure to beat the hand-built bids is no evidence that a network helps as trained here; it is no evidence that a network cannot help. Two tests would settle it: training all {{P1-MLP-N-PARAMS}} weights end to end on the simulated objective, with a gradient-free method such as an evolution strategy or with a policy-gradient method, and starting the network from the learned linear policy, so that it begins at the linear model's result and can only add what nonlinearity buys. Neither was run, and the pre-registration froze both models before the main runs (Section 4.6).

The simulation is checked, not validated against a lunar fleet. The engine's checks (Section 3.11) and those of our earlier papers compare it with closed-form cases, degenerate settings and its own earlier versions. No multi-owner fleet operates on the Moon, so no measured delay exists to validate against, and the absolute waits in Table 5 should be read as model outputs under stated assumptions; the paired differences between regimes are the result.

Censored waits. Cargo not delivered by the end of a simulated year carries the wait it had accumulated by then, so the primary outcome is a censored mean that understates the true wait of what owners alone leave behind, and the delivered-only mean reported in Section 5.2 conditions on delivery and so compares different sets of items. We report both and test neither as a substitute for the other; the treatment is the analogue, for a simulation, of reporting how missing observations were handled.

Three defects were found in the runs and corrected before the final analysis. Regimes that never wait for Earth give the same run for a seed whatever the start year, so their second block repeated seeds; comparisons between such regimes use the first block only (n = 30), and an interim reading that counted the repeats is withdrawn (Section 4.6). The auction's ordering of robots depended on a per-process hash, which made runs under message loss valid draws but not bit-reproducible; the order was fixed and the affected runs repeated, and the repeated runs changed no result. The third, in the engine's event handling, is described with its before-and-after comparison in Section 4.6. All three are recorded with dates in the pre-registration file [43].

The registered seed rule was not applied. No seeds were added in any cell, although the half-width exceeded 10 % of the mean in {{P1-SEEDS-N-OVER}} of the {{P1-SEEDS-N-CELLS}} E40 cells (Section 4.5). Every interval is from the fixed design, and we did not extend any sample after seeing a result.

The ownership split and the horizon set the size of the sharing result. Three owners have a third of the cargo each, and one has no vehicle that can lift most of its own; team items are drawn at masses that only one same-owner pair can reach. That split was not varied in E40 to E42. Each run is one year from an empty base, so every regime is in its start-up year, and urgent cargo not delivered by day 365 counts with its wait up to then: the hours of H1 scale with the horizon.

The auction is modelled at the level of rounds. The best bid of each connected part of the network always wins, and a lost bid costs a parallel winner or a wasted trip. A message-level implementation, in which the best bid can go unheard, could do worse. The base computer holds every manifest and is the default leader, so the on-Moon regimes are less distributed than “among the robots” suggests. The engine has no channel capacity: messages are counted and never queue or collide, so the radio limit of Section 5.8 does not feed back into the main results. No vehicle fails in any run.

Table 6 and the guard runs predate the engine fixes. They come from the first evaluation of the learned bids, made on the engine before changes 4 and 5, and have not been rerun (Section 5.6). The frozen policies were also run three times on the evaluation seeds, not once; no training or selection used those seeds.

The protocol is counted and replayed, not transmitted. E53 counts bytes on a geometric mesh, with no radio hardware, no link budget, no interference and no channel-access protocol; the efficiency of a scheduled channel, {{P1-E53-ETA-SCHED}}, is assumed and was not swept. The sizes are packets before link framing and channel coding, the traffic model holds {{P1-E53-N-TYPES}} of the {{P1-LRIP-N-MSG}} message types, and its fleet geometry samples where eight vehicles spend their time, which is near the base. The position report of v0.1 carries no frame, time-scale identifier or covariance; version 0.2 adds them and its sizes are not in this paper. E54 replays one recorded month of one fleet at the level of messages. Its baseline, drop and retry capped at five tries in twelve minutes, is assumed and was not swept, and most of the difference between the transports follows from that cap. Its lost-link return is applied to the contact plan while the recorded jobs stay, so a vehicle is at its last link point for the radio and on its job for the logistics: the return's gain in contact is counted and its cost in interrupted jobs is not. A returning vehicle is given a direct link to the base, which is optimistic. The Earth blackout bears only on the ledger. No reservation is released for a silent holder; the replay counts the segments that would stay unconfirmed, and the delay of crossing them at caution speed is not priced.

The protocol has one implementation. The schema, the codec and the conformance vectors were written together. Until a second implementation, written from the schema alone, decodes the vectors, interoperability is a design aim. The authentication code stops forged and replayed messages from outside the site; a vehicle that holds the site key can still lie about itself, and the handling of keys is designed and not tested.

The propositions are about models. Proposition 1 covers one allocation round with comparable bids that reach every bidder, and bounds a score below a ceiling; it gives no bound on cost or waiting. It says nothing about the schedule over a year, about lost messages (measured in Section 5.5) or about owners who shade their bids [44]. For the published 50 % bound of the consensus-based bundle algorithm we read two later papers of its authors' group [5, 74] and not the original proof [4]. Proposition 3(a) assumes independent loss; bursts make ages worse. Propositions 4 and 5 are formulas with assumed inputs: no rover failure is simulated and no operator was observed, and the inputs of Proposition 5 were chosen to match published ratios. Proposition 6 counts the bits of a layout whose ranges we set by its bit widths.

CADRE has not flown, and we have not reproduced it. The comparison in Section 3.13 rests on its team's papers [71, 72]. We did not run its design in our engine, and our leader regime takes 0.5 h to elect where CADRE's design takes at most 10 s.

8. Conclusion

The gain from pooling several owners' vehicles at a lunar south-pole base survives, in this simulation, when dispatch moves from Earth onto the robots. A pooled dispatcher that stops when Earth sets costs {{P1-H2-STRICT-MINUS-O}} of urgent wait to delivery; planning ahead and a relay recover most of that; the robots' auction {{P1-H3A-ABSTRACT-CLAUSE}}. With one owner lacking a cargo rover, the gain from pooling is cargo that owners alone had not delivered by year-end; on cargo both delivered, they waited {{P1-H1-DELIV-DIFF}} longer. The registered learned bid, a network, did not beat hand-built bids; a linear correction, registered as secondary, cut the wait by {{P1-H4-LIN-PCT-30-ABS}}.

By calculation, {{P1-LRIP-CONCLUSION-SENTENCE}}. In a replay of one month, {{P1-E54-SHORT-CLAUSE}}.

An always-up relay equals the marketplace run for run, the auction was not distinguishable from it, and only an 80 % relay was worse than the auction, by {{P1-AUCTION-MINUS-RELAY08}} ({{P1-AUCTION-MINUS-RELAY08-CI}}). The results hold for one base, one ownership split, a geometric radio and honest owners.

Appendix B. Guarantees

Each proposition is stated with its assumption and proved in a few lines. Propositions 5 and 6 are counts on inputs we chose and not theorems about the Moon; they keep the name for the numbering. The numbers that follow each proof are computed by the build script from the files named in Data and code availability, and the script checks them against the codec, the result files or a seeded simulation where the text says so. Table 12 says which of these are also tested.

Proposition 1 (one auction round). Robots i bid for jobs j. An assignment A is a set of pairs (i, j) in which each job appears at most once and each robot at most L times. Robot i scores the set Ai of its jobs by Si(Ai), with Si of the empty set zero, and the team score is F(A) = Σi Si(Ai). Assume that adding a job never lowers a robot's score and that a job is never worth more to a robot that already holds more (diminishing marginal gain): for B contained in B′, Si(B + j) − Si(B) ≥ Si(B′ + j) − Si(B′) ≥ 0. The greedy rule fixes, among the pairs that keep the assignment allowed, the one that raises F most, and repeats until no allowed pair raises it. Let G be its result and O any allowed assignment. Then (a) if L = 1, F(G) ≥ F(O)/2, and non-negative pair values are all that is needed; (b) if no robot holds L jobs in G, F(G) ≥ F(O)/2; (c) in every case F(G) ≥ F(O)/3.

Proof. Write g1, …, gm for the pairs in the order greedy fixed them, Gk for the first k of them and δk = F(Gk) − F(Gk−1) > 0, so that the δk sum to F(G). For a pair o write Δ(o | A) = F(A + o) − F(A); F is defined for any set of pairs, each robot scoring the jobs paired with it. Scores never fall, so F(O) ≤ F(O ∪ G). Adding the pairs of O that are not in G to G one at a time, each gains at most what it would gain on G alone, because gains diminish. Hence

F(O) ≤ F(G) + Σ Δ(o | G), the sum over the pairs o of O not in G. (1)

Take such a pair o = (i, j). If G + o is still allowed, greedy stopped with o available, so Δ(o | G) ≤ 0. Otherwise let k be the first step after which o is no longer allowed. Before any step up to k, o was allowed, so greedy's choice at that step gained at least Δ(o | G), again because gains diminish. Step k blocked o in one of two ways. Either gk took job j: charge o to gk; O holds job j once, so a greedy pair receives at most one charge of this kind. Or gk was the L-th job of robot i: then o was allowed at each of the L steps that gave robot i a job; O gives robot i at most L pairs, so those blocked in this way can be charged one each to robot i's L greedy pairs. Both cannot happen at one step, since a pair of robot i that takes job j is o itself. Every greedy pair is thus charged at most twice, once through its job and once through its robot, and each charge is no more than its δ. With (1), F(O) ≤ F(G) + 2F(G), which is (c). If no robot is full in G, only charges through jobs occur and F(O) ≤ 2F(G), which is (b). For (a), with one job per robot F is the plain sum of pair values w(i, j) ≥ 0 and (1) is not needed. Each pair of O is in G, or has a value that is not positive, or was blocked by a greedy pair worth at least as much. A greedy pair shares its robot with at most one pair of O and its job with at most one, and a greedy pair that is itself in O shares neither with another pair of O. Hence F(O) ≤ 2F(G). ∎

One half cannot be raised in (a): with two robots, two jobs and values w(1, a) = 1 + ε, w(1, b) = w(2, a) = 1 and w(2, b) = 0, greedy scores 1 + ε and the best assignment 2. For (c) we know no instance below one half and no proof above one third. That the robots' exchange of bids reaches the greedy result at all is the convergence that the algorithm's authors state: under diminishing gain the largest bid in the network cannot be beaten later, so every robot agrees on it after as many message rounds as the network's diameter, and the argument repeats for the next pair [74]. We take that from them.

What the proposition covers in this paper is narrow. The auction of Section 3.7 at bundle depth 1 fixes the lowest bid first and gives each free robot at most one job in a round. With a ceiling C at or above every bid and the value C minus the bid, lowest bid first is largest value first, so (a) applies: the round's total value is at least half the best possible. The guarantee is in value below the ceiling, depends on the free constant C, and gives no bound in cost: with costs c(1, a) = 0, c(1, b) = c(2, a) = 1 and c(2, b) = M, lowest bid first pays M where the best assignment pays 2, for any M. Diminishing gain was not verified for the engine's bid, and the small instances use a toy score, not that bid. Also, the factor of two in cost published for sequential auctions [2] is for robots that chain several tasks on one path and does not cover one job per robot. It treats each load as one job, and a load of several items can overlap other robots' loads in more than one item, which the proof does not cover. It holds for one round in which every bid reaches every bidder; the schedule over a year and the effect of lost messages are measured in Sections 5.4 and 5.5 and not proved. For bundle depths above 1, which Section 5.5 found worse under loss, (b) and (c) are what we can prove. The 50 % usually quoted for the consensus-based bundle algorithm is as reported in [5, 74]; we could not read the original proof [4]. On the {{P1-BOUNDS-N}} small instances of Section 3.11 the lowest share of the optimum was {{P1-BOUNDS-CBBA-MIN-D1}} at depth 1 and {{P1-BOUNDS-CBBA-MIN-DEEP}} at depths {{P1-BOUNDS-CBBA-DEPTHS}}.

Proposition 2 (the bit rate a fleet needs). Let each of N rovers send message type m at rate fm as a packet of sm bytes (to which link framing and coding add). The load offered at the sources is L = 8N Σm fmsm bit/s. If every message is transmitted h times on average (its hops) on one shared channel, and the channel carries successful transmissions for a share η of the time, then the channel's bit rate R must satisfy R ≥ Lh/η.

Proof. Over a long time τ the sources generate Lτ bits. Each is transmitted h times on average, so Lhτ bits must be transmitted successfully. The channel carries at most ηRτ. ∎

On a scheduled channel with a share g of the time left as guard intervals, η = 1 − g; E53 uses g = 0.2 (Assumed). On slotted random access in which each of N stations transmits in a slot independently with probability a, a slot carries exactly one transmission with probability Na(1 − a)N−1, largest at a = 1/N, where it equals (1 − 1/N)N−1: {{P1-PROP2-ALOHA-N20}} for N = 20, falling towards 1/e = {{P1-PROP2-ALOHA-LIMIT}} as N grows. E53 uses 1/e. {{P1-PROP2-EXAMPLE-SENTENCE}} The condition is necessary and not sufficient: it ignores queues, capture and stations that cannot hear each other.

Proposition 3 (the age of a relayed report). A source sends a report every T seconds. The age at a receiver at time t is t minus the sending time of the newest report it has received [80]. (a) Relay without retry. Each report crosses k hops, each taking D seconds and losing the report independently with probability p, with no retransmission (the engine's loss model of Section 3.5, without its retry). Let q = (1 − p)k. Then the time-average age is kD + T(2 − q)/(2q); the mean age just before a report arrives is kD + T/q; and at almost every time, for every whole number m ≥ 0, the age exceeds kD + mT with probability (1 − q)m. (b) Store and forward. If no report is lost or expires, and a report waits Wj for a contact and takes Dj on hop j, then at all times the age is at most T plus the largest total Σj (Wj + Dj) over the reports.

Proof. (a) A report arrives kD after it was sent, with probability q, independently of the others. Between two arrivals the age rises in a straight line from kD to kD + X, where X = NT and N, the number of reports from one arrival to the next, takes the value n with probability q(1 − q)n−1, with mean 1/q and second moment (2 − q)/q2. The time average of such a sawtooth is kD + E[X2]/(2E[X]) = kD + T(2 − q)/(2q), and its mean peak is kD + E[X] = kD + T/q. The age at t exceeds kD + mT exactly when none of the reports sent in the interval from t − kD − mT (excluded) to t − kD (included) arrived. Unless t − kD is a multiple of T, that interval holds m sending times, and the probability is (1 − q)m. (b) Let Δ be the largest total. A report was sent in the interval of length T that ends at t − Δ, and it has arrived by t, so the newest report received is no older than T + Δ. ∎

So with probability 1 − ε the age in (a) is at most kD + mT for the smallest whole m ≥ 1 with (1 − q)m ≤ ε (Table B1). {{P1-PROP3-EXAMPLE-SENTENCE}} A relay that forwards the newest report first keeps the age lowest in a multi-hop network [81]. {{P1-PROP3-E54-SENTENCE}} No experiment of this paper relays a report over more than one hop, so Table B1 is an illustration of the formula.

Proposition 4 (hand-over after a failure). A vehicle sends a heartbeat every Thb, and a neighbour declares it silent when k heartbeats in a row have not arrived, that is, a time kThb after the last one it received. (a) A vehicle that fails at a moment uniformly placed between two heartbeats, and whose last heartbeat was received, is declared silent after a delay between (k − 1)Thb and kThb, with mean (k − ½)Thb; if its last heartbeats were lost the delay is shorter. (b) If each heartbeat of a live vehicle is lost independently with probability p, a false declaration begins at a given heartbeat with probability (1 − p)pk. (c) The expected extra wait of the failed vehicle's job is E[ΔT] = E[detection] + RTround + E[d]/v + cTx − Tsaved, for R auction rounds of length Tround, a new winner at distance d driving at speed v, cargo aboard the failed vehicle with probability c and a transfer time Tx, and work Tsaved that the new winner need not repeat. (d) If instead the decision waits until Earth is in view, and Earth is hidden in gaps of length G separated by windows of length W, a failure at a uniformly random time waits G2/(2(G + W)) on average for the next window.

Proof. (a) Let the failure fall a time U after the last heartbeat sent, U uniform between 0 and Thb. The declaration comes kThb after that heartbeat, which is kThb − U after the failure. If the last n heartbeats were lost, the count began nThb earlier. (b) A false declaration begins at a heartbeat when it and the k − 1 before it are lost and the one before those was received. (c) The delays add, and expectation is linear. (d) The failure falls in a gap with probability G/(G + W) and is then uniformly placed in it, with mean remaining time G/2. ∎

Proposition 5 (operators against fleet size and autonomy; a model). Let each of N vehicles ask for help λ times an hour, each request taking h hours of one operator's attention, and let operators be busy for at most a share ρ of their time. Then the number of operators M satisfies M ≥ Nλh/ρ: one operator serves at most ρ/(λh) vehicles. With a round trip of Trt to Earth and n exchanges per request, h = h0 + nTrt.

Proof. The requests need Nλh operator-hours an hour, and M operators supply at most ρM. ∎

This is a statement about average load; it does not say how long a request waits. In the terms of Olsen and Wood [77], with interaction time IT = h and activity time AT = 1/λ − h, the ratio is ρ(AT + IT)/IT, which is ρ(AT/IT + 1). No request rate has been measured for a lunar rover. Table B2 uses rates and handling times that we assumed, chosen so that the result sits near published ratios: it is a consistency check of the formula against those ratios and not a prediction, and it is not evidence for any of them. VIPER plans about four people on console for one rover [76]. A simulator study of people monitoring self-driving vehicles found performance best at about five vehicles, steady to seven and clearly worse at nine, as reported by a trade body [78]. A robotaxi operator was reported in early 2026 to have about 70 remote assistants on duty for about 3,000 vehicles, about 43 vehicles each [79]. The last two are secondary reports. During a blackout no operator on Earth can serve at all, and a request waits as in Proposition 4(d).
