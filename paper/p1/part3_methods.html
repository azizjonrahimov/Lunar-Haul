3. Model

The model extends the discrete-event simulation of [40, 41, 42]: time jumps from one event, such as a landing, the end of a drive or a lost message being sent again, to the next. Everything that was in those papers and is not described here is unchanged, and with the new features switched off the engine reproduces their results exactly (Section 3.11). Every input carries a flag at first use, as in [41, 42]: Measured (flight, laboratory or survey data), Spec (a published design value or company claim), Modelled (produced by a model, including ours) or Assumed (our choice, varied where it matters). Results produced by this study are never flagged; they come from the result files listed under Availability.

3.1 Base, terrain and demand

The base is the one of [41, 42], on the Connecting Ridge between Shackleton and de Gerlache craters, on NASA Goddard's 5 m terrain tile from the Lunar Orbiter Laser Altimeter (Measured). Its sites are the habitat and control centre (BASE), a cargo depot, a power plant, a construction site, an oxygen pilot plant, a science site and a permanently shadowed crater; landers use two pads 1 km from the base. Driving times, energy and routes come from the driving tables of [41].

Demand follows the landings and bulk flows of [40], scaled by a factor k so that the two cargo-class vehicles are busy a set share of the time: about 10, 30 and 60 % (k = {{P1-K-10}}, {{P1-K-30}} and {{P1-K-60}}, Assumed; calibrated on five seeds to within one percentage point of the target). We call these the three loads. The two cargo-class vehicles are the only ones that can lift most of the cargo, so the share of the whole fleet's time spent working is much lower: a share reported in Section 5.1. Landed cargo belongs to the owner who ordered it, a third each. Bulk regolith moves in 500 kg lots (Assumed), split among owners in the same shares. A landed item becomes a team item with probability 0.10 (Assumed; 0, 0.10 and 0.25 in Section 4.2), drawn from its own random stream, and then weighs between the largest single vehicle's capacity and the largest pair's, 2,000–2,800 kg. Items heavier than any pair can lift go on a separate list, “needs heavy-lift equipment”, and are never offered to the robots; they are reported and excluded from every delay. Each run simulates one year from an empty base.

3.3 The link to Earth

Earth is in view of the base when any part of Earth's disc is above the terrain horizon of a 2.5 m antenna at BASE, the criterion of the published visibility maps [42]. The windows for 2028–2031 are those of [42]; for start years from 2032 we use the same pipeline's windows extended to 2046 (Modelled). A relay, where a regime has one, adds link time as an on–off process with a stated coverage, independent of the direct link, with the gap pattern of [42] (Assumed). Earth's visibility from the base differs by year: in the start years we simulate it ranges from {{P1-VIS-MIN}} to {{P1-VIS-MAX}} of the year, and in {{P1-H2-N-LOWVIS-YEARS}} of them it is below 60 %, the threshold we fixed for the availability test (Section 4.5).

3.4 The robots' radio network

Two robots can talk directly when their antennas see each other over the terrain and are within range. We computed line of sight for every pair of 2,421 points (the sites, the pads and samples along every route) on the 5 m terrain with the Moon's curvature, the same arithmetic as [41]. Antennas are 2.5 m high on rovers and 1.0 m on small robots and hoppers (Assumed), and a 10 m mast at BASE carries the base computer (Assumed, after NASA Glenn's surface relay concept as in [42]). The range is 5 km and the clearance margin 0 m (both Assumed; 2, 10 km and unlimited range in Section 4.2). A message can travel over several hops through robots or the mast.

Line of sight is rare on this ridge. Two rover antennas 2–5 km apart see each other in {{P1-MESH-LOS-25-2TO5}} of point pairs, and the mast sees rovers directly only within {{P1-MESH-MAST-MAX-KM}} of the base. Rovers nevertheless form one connected network from 2 km range upward, and every point reaches the mast in at most {{P1-MESH-MAX-HOPS-5KM}} hops at 5 km range; small robots leave {{P1-MESH-SMALL-ISOLATED}} points cut off. The robots' positions are interpolated along their committed drives, and the network is evaluated whenever a decision is made.

3.5 Messages

Every hop of a message is lost independently with probability p (Bernoulli loss, Assumed) or in bursts (a two-state Gilbert–Elliott channel with mean burst length 10 messages and the same long-run loss, Assumed), and takes 10 ms (Assumed). The on-Moon regimes run at p = 0.10 unless stated; Section 4.2 varies it from 0 to 0.40. A robot whose message was lost is reconsidered 3 minutes later (Assumed), with at most one such retry pending at a time. We count transmissions, messages delivered and lost, and messages per robot-hour.

3.6 Decision regimes

A regime says who decides which vehicle takes which job, where that decision is made, and when it can be made. Table 3 lists them. The owners-alone regimes use the in-house dispatcher of [40]: each owner assigns only its own cargo to its own vehicles, and its own pairs may lift together. When two owners' vehicles want the same apron at the same time, the newcomer waits 2 h of hand coordination (Assumed), and only while Earth is in view. The pooled regimes use the marketplace of [40], which offers every job to every vehicle in auction rounds and forms team lifts from any pair; they differ in where it runs.

A dispatcher on Earth acts only while Earth is in view of the base. Under strict fallback, vehicles finish their current job and wait when Earth sets. Under plan ahead, the dispatcher uploads at loss of signal a queue per vehicle for the items already waiting, and a standing rule for cargo that will land during the blackout: oldest first, nearest free vehicle, one item per trip. Items created on the Moon during a blackout, such as returns, wait for Earth.

On the Moon, the elected leader regime runs the same marketplace on the base computer at the mast, over the robots in its part of the network. The base computer fails at random (mean time between failures 2,000 h, repair 24 h, both Assumed); then, after a 30-minute election (Assumed), the robot that reaches most others leads until the base computer returns. We took the 30 minutes from CADRE's wake cycle [31]; CADRE's design in fact replaces a lost leader within 10 s [71], so the engine's value is slow (Section 3.13). Status and assignment messages travel over the lossy network. The auction and learned regimes are described next.

3.7 The auction among the robots

Each connected part of the network runs the marketplace's own rounds. A part that contains the mast knows every waiting item; any other part knows only the items at sites where one of its robots stands. In a round, each robot computes its best bid, the marginal cost in hours of adding the job to its plan, weighted by the item's urgency as in [40], and floods it to the robots it can reach, losing it hop by hop with the channel's probability. The true best bid always wins its item. A robot that did not hear the best bid and believes its own is best wins in parallel if its items do not overlap with the winner's; if they do, CBBA's rule settles the conflict when the winner's rebroadcast reaches it within the remaining rounds [4], and otherwise the robot drives to the pad for nothing. The number of rounds is at most the larger of the number of items and of robots, times the network's diameter in hops, so that a perfect network never cuts the auction short.

With bundle depth greater than one, a CBBA-like variant, robots also bid for up to depth − 1 later jobs from the end of their commitments. A claim starts when the robot comes free if its items are still waiting, and is dropped otherwise. Claims are hidden from robots in the same part of the network. With perfect links and depth 1, the auction reproduces the marketplace's event trace exactly (Section 3.11).

3.8 Team lifts across owners

A team item is lifted by two vehicles together. In the pooled regimes, the auction pairs any two vehicles that can join a team lift and together can carry the item; under the owners-alone regimes the pair must have one owner. The pair agrees by a two-phase commit over the radio: the proposer sends a proposal, the partner answers yes, and the proposer sends a commit. A lost proposal or answer means no team in this round. A lost commit sends the proposer alone to the pad, where it waits the timeout, 1 h (Assumed; 0.5 and 2 h in Section 4.2), and gives up; the item returns to the pool and the failed meeting is counted with its waiting time. Coupling at the pad takes 1 h, as in [40]. Some message is always the last one, so no protocol over a lossy channel can guarantee that both robots know the other will come (the Two Generals problem); the commit step trades failures of one kind for another, and Section 5.7 measures the balance. An exploratory variant lets the base computer arbitrate meetings within its part of the network.

3.9 Robots that nobody hears

Line of sight on the ridge is so sparse that a robot finishing a delivery at the oxygen plant, the science site, the shadowed crater or pad B can end up where no other robot and no mast hears it. Without a rule it is never reached again. Our lost-link rule (Assumed) sends an idle robot that nobody hears to the nearest site that sees the mast at its antenna height (to BASE for owners alone on the Moon), where it waits to be heard. Section 5.5 reports what switching the rule off costs in the E41 experiment (Section 4.2). We added the rule during development, after a five-seed run in which the elected leader without it delivered {{P1-RALLY-OFF-DEV-T}} of cargo in a year against {{P1-RALLY-ON-DEV-T}} with it (Section 4.6). Section 4.2 includes the rule switched off, and a second 10 m mast at pad B, as network-design variants.

3.10 Learned bids

The learned regimes keep the auction unchanged and add a correction to each bid: the robot bids b′ = b − u·g(x), where b is the hand-built bid, u converts hours of urgent wait into the bid's units as in [40], and g is a learned function of {{P1-N-FEATURES}} features that a robot knows locally. They are the planned hours of the job, the item's age, priority and slack to its target time (24 h for urgent items, as pre-registered), the load as a share of capacity, the hours to drive to the pad and the battery's state of charge. They also include the hours until the robot's next dark period or until its battery would run out (whichever comes first), whether it is in the mast's part of the network, how many robots it hears, how many claims it holds, the number of items in the bundle, and the hours until the next published landing at the item's pad or the robot's site. The last two time features let a robot anticipate what the hand-built bid ignores. The network never assigns anything, so it cannot create a conflict or an infeasible plan that the auction would not.

We trained two forms of g: a multilayer perceptron with two hidden layers of {{P1-MLP-HIDDEN}} units ({{P1-MLP-N-PARAMS}} parameters), and a linear model with {{P1-LIN-N-WEIGHTS}} weights. Training used seeds 1000 and up, selection used tuning seeds 2000 and up, and the evaluation seeds 1–30 were used once (Section 4.3). The first stage imitated the full-information marketplace by dataset aggregation [24]: each of {{P1-IMIT-N-ROUNDS}} training rounds ({{P1-IMIT-N-ROWS}} robot-item rows) was labelled with the item the marketplace would give each robot. Section 5.11 reports what that stage produced and how often the hand-built bids already chose the marketplace's item. The marketplace uses the same bid rule with full information, so imitating it reproduces the hand-built bid.

The second stage searched for g on the objective itself with the cross-entropy method: candidates were scored by mean urgent wait at the middle load and message loss 0.10 on {{P1-CEM-N-TRAIN-SEEDS}} training seeds, with common random numbers across candidates, and each generation's best was scored on {{P1-CEM-N-TUNE-SEEDS}} tuning seeds; the generation with the best tuning score was kept. The linear model searched its {{P1-LIN-N-WEIGHTS}} weights over {{P1-CEM-LIN-GENS}} generations of {{P1-CEM-LIN-POP}} candidates; the network searched only its {{P1-CEM-MLP-N-SEARCHED}} output weights over {{P1-CEM-MLP-GENS}} generations of {{P1-CEM-MLP-POP}}, with its hidden layers left at their initial values because imitation never moved them. Every candidate's score is recorded in the policy files. We decided before the main runs to carry both models into every experiment (Section 4.6).

3.11 Checks

Each regime was checked against cases whose answer is known. The pooled dispatcher on Earth with coverage 1.0 reproduces the full-information marketplace's event trace and summary exactly; so does the elected leader with perfect radio and no base failures; so does the auction with perfect links and depth 1; and owners alone with full coverage, no hand-coordination time and no own-pair lifts reproduce the in-house dispatcher of [40]. With every new feature off, {{P1-CHECK-N-ROWS}} result rows of [41, 42] rerun with no difference in any numeric column, and the test suite ({{P1-CHECK-N-TESTS}} tests) passes. On {{P1-BOUNDS-N}} small random instances the sequential auction, with one item per robot, had a total cost of at most {{P1-BOUNDS-SSI-MAX}} times the optimum from the Hungarian method; the published factor of two [2] is for robots that chain several tasks on one path and is not a proven bound for this case, so the ratio is reported as measured. The CBBA-like variant's score was at least {{P1-BOUNDS-CBBA-MIN-D1}} of the brute-force optimum at depth 1, where Proposition 1(a) guarantees one half, and at least {{P1-BOUNDS-CBBA-MIN-DEEP}} at depths {{P1-BOUNDS-CBBA-DEPTHS}}, where Proposition 1(c) guarantees one third if gains diminish, which we did not verify for that score. No item was ever assigned twice. The depth-1 result checks the implementation and says nothing new about the algorithm. {{P1-CHECKS-SENTENCE}}

3.12 What the robots say to each other: the interaction protocol

Sections 3.5 to 3.8 treat a message as an event that arrives or is lost. For a vehicle built by another company to take part, the message itself has to be fixed: its fields, its units, its size, who may send it, and what a receiver does with a version it does not know. We wrote this down as the Lunar Rover Interaction Protocol, LRIP v0.1: a schema file, a codec that follows it and a set of test packets (Data and code availability). It is a design of ours and not a standard. The results of Sections 5.1 to 5.7 do not depend on it, because the engine counts messages and LRIP gives them bytes. Five rules shaped it. Every layer below the application is taken from an existing standard. There is one schema with two encodings, bit-packed for a narrow-band channel and CBOR [61] for a broadband one. Every message is authenticated, and a message that gives a right is signed. No message can command another owner's vehicle; a vehicle can only inform, ask and agree. What a vehicle does without a link is fixed in advance (Section 3.13).

Table A1 in Appendix A sets the layers beside the standards they reuse. A packet in the narrow-band profile is a Space Packet [55] whose application identifier is the message type. It has a packet header of {{P1-LRIP-SPP-B}}, an LRIP header of {{P1-LRIP-HDR-B}} (schema version, message type, sender, and the time the content is valid for, in tenths of a second), the content, a security header of {{P1-LRIP-SEC-B}} (which key, and a counter that only rises) and an authentication code of {{P1-LRIP-MAC-B}}: AES-CMAC [63] cut to the shortest length that the space-link security standard allows [56]. That is {{P1-LRIP-OVERHEAD-B}} of overhead, or {{P1-LRIP-OVERHEAD-SIG-B}} when an Ed25519 signature [62] of {{P1-LRIP-SIG-B}} replaces the code. The security fields copy the layout of the space-link security protocol but sit in the message and not in the frame, so they are not that protocol [56]. The counter and the code let a receiver reject forged and replayed messages from outside the site. They do not stop a vehicle that holds the site key from lying about itself; detecting that is a subject of the companion papers [44, 45].

{{P1-LRIP-PATTERN-SENTENCE}} Table A2 lists them with their sizes. The split follows one rule. State that is refreshed anyway (where a vehicle is, how it is, what it intends, that it is alive) is published to whoever listens, without acknowledgement, and the newest value replaces the old. Anything that changes who may be where or who does what is a request with a response and a timeout: a bid answered by an award, a request for a stretch of road answered by a grant, a capability query answered by a manifest. The three responses are signed. An award and a grant give a right, to a job or to a stretch of road, that a third vehicle must be able to verify, and they are {{P1-LRIP-AWARD-B}} and {{P1-LRIP-GRANT-B}} on the air; the manifest is what every later choice of bidders rests on. An alert that orders all vehicles to stop or closes a landing zone is signed by the base ({{P1-LRIP-ALERT-SIGNED-B}}). The task messages follow the announce, bid, award and report sequence known as the contract-net protocol; we could not open its FIPA specification and do not cite it as a source. The message set is a superset of the two reports of the MassRobotics standard [65], and its names map onto the topics of VDA 5050: the position report is its visualization, the status report its state, a task its order, the manifest its factsheet, the heartbeat its connection [64].

The protocol fixes the bytes between vehicles and leaves each owner's on-board software alone, a choice that follows from the radio. The middleware under ROS 2 (DDS) spends 20 B on its wire header before any content, and by default every participant announces itself to every other every 30 s [68], which a shared channel of a few kilobits a second cannot carry. MQTT-SN was built for constrained links, with a header of 2 or 4 B and a gateway that holds messages for sleeping clients [70], and Zenoh has been measured against MQTT, Kafka and DDS by its own developers and a university group [69]; either could carry the broadband profile. Neither survives an outage of hours. The Bundle Protocol does [60], at about {{P1-LRIP-BUNDLE-B}} of overhead per bundle, so it carries what must arrive (awards, grants, alerts) and not the once-a-second report.

A vehicle describes itself in a capability manifest, a small document signed by its owner that any other party can fetch: identity, size and limits, energy, radios, navigation accuracy, the message versions it speaks, what it does when it loses the link, and a list of named, versioned capabilities with typed parameters. The idea is that of a tool description in the Model Context Protocol, where a server lists each tool with a name and a schema for its inputs and announces when the list changes [67], and of the factsheet of VDA 5050 [64]. On the wire every key and every listed string is a small integer from a published registry, decimals are fixed-point integers and the whole is CBOR [61]. {{P1-LRIP-MANIFEST-SIZE-SENTENCE}} The status report carries a 16-bit checksum of the current manifest, so a change prompts a new query.

A task names a capability, a version and parameter values. A vehicle can do it if its manifest lists that capability with the same major version and a minor version at least the task's, if every numeric parameter of the task lies within the manifest's minimum and maximum, and if every list parameter is a subset of the manifest's list. A parameter that the manifest does not mention counts as a refusal; capabilities and parameters that the reader does not know are ignored. {{P1-LRIP-CANDO-SENTENCE}} In the auction the rule is an optional filter: a robot whose manifest cannot do a load does not bid for it. {{P1-LRIP-CAPFILTER-SENTENCE}} The filter therefore changes nothing in this paper's experiments, which run without it. It matters when a vehicle joins whose class the dispatcher has never seen.

Versions follow four rules, each covered by a test in the repository. Every message carries the schema's major and minor version. A minor version may only append fields, use spare bits, or add enumeration values and message types, so a receiver decodes the fields it knows and skips the rest, since the packet length says how much; an unknown message type is counted and dropped. An unknown enumeration value is read as the most cautious one known: an unknown alert type as a hazard, an unknown mode as a fault. A different major version is refused. VDA 5050 rejects an order that carries a field the robot cannot use [64]; LRIP skips the field, because a rover out of contact cannot ask again.

Two implementations interoperate if they agree on bytes, and the test for that is a file. {{P1-LRIP-VECTORS-SENTENCE}} A second implementation conforms when it decodes every packet to the recorded values and encodes the recorded values to the recorded bytes. Our codec decodes all {{P1-LRIP-N-VECTORS}}, which the script that builds this paper checks on every build. No second, independent implementation exists yet, so interoperability between implementations is designed for and not tested.

3.13 When a robot fails or loses the link

Two rules say what happens when a robot stops answering. They are design rules of the protocol; only the last step of the second is in the engine's main runs. First, every vehicle sends a heartbeat (the HELLO message, or any other) at a fixed period, and a neighbour that misses k in a row marks it silent; the base, or the group if the base is out of reach, marks it failed after a second, longer timeout or a sighting. A failed vehicle's job goes back to auction with its epoch number raised by one. If the old holder returns it drops the job, unless it already carries the cargo, in which case it says so and the new award is cancelled; the epoch keeps two robots from holding one job once their messages meet. The dead vehicle's road reservation is not released: its last reserved stretch stays closed until the vehicle is seen or towed, so that safety never rests on the failure detector being right [45]. Proposition 4 (Appendix B) gives the expected extra wait of the job. A rover that fails for good is not simulated in this paper; the engine's failures are those of the base computer (Section 3.6).

Second, a vehicle that loses the link without failing follows a fixed ladder. It finishes the stretch of road it has reserved and never stops inside a single-lane segment, stops at the next safe point and holds there for {{P1-E54-HOLD-S}}, then retraces its path at half speed to the last point where it had a link, and after {{P1-E54-RDV-H}} there drives to a meeting point in view of the mast. The others keep its reserved stretch closed until its timeout, and its awarded job returns to the pool when the lease ends. {{P1-LRIP-LADDER-SENTENCE}} The rally rule of Section 3.9 is the last step of this ladder and the only one inside the engine; the return to the last link point is tested at the level of messages in E54 (Section 4.8).

These decisions sit on the Moon for three reasons, each with a number. The first is reaction time. In NASA's simulator study of driving the Lunar Terrain Vehicle from Earth, the mean speed fell from 3.24 km/h with no delay to 2.56 km/h at 4 s of round-trip delay, 2.03 km/h at 6 s and 1.76 km/h at 8 s, with workload rising [75]. VIPER's plan is waypoint driving with a round trip of 6 to 10 s, 0.8 cm/s made good and about four people on console for one rover [76]. Stopping and giving way cannot wait that long. The second is the blackout. A decision that only Earth may take, needed at a random moment, waits {{P1-PROP4-EARTH-H}} on average at this base, against {{P1-PROP4-TOTAL-MIN}} for a hand-over decided on the Moon under the assumptions of Proposition 4. The third is people: Proposition 5 puts numbers, as a model, on how many operators a fleet needs at each level of autonomy. Dispatch, right of way and the re-auction of a failed robot's job therefore belong on the Moon. Recovering a broken vehicle, and anything else that takes judgment, can wait for people on Earth while Earth is in view, with a safe default when it is not: hold at a safe point.

CADRE is the one design of this kind built for flight, and it differs from ours in ways that matter here [71, 72]. It has one owner, so no private costs and no question of whose job comes first; we simulate three owners, and two and five outside the training range. Its three rovers are identical; ours are eight vehicles in six classes. It plans on an elected leader with a greedy priority scheduler and has no auction; we compare a leader with an auction and with dispatch from Earth. It assumes a slow two-way channel between every rover and the leader at all times, and has no mode for a partition that lasts hours; our robots lose messages, split into groups and drive to a rally point when unheard. Its site sees Earth through the lander for its one lunar day; ours loses Earth for about eleven days at a time. Its leader is replaced within 10 s, where our engine takes 0.5 h, a value we set before reading [71]. With a base-computer failure every 2,000 h that is {{P1-LEADER-N-FAIL-YEAR}} elections and {{P1-LEADER-ELECTION-H-YEAR}} of election a year, so we expect the value to matter little, but we did not rerun the leader regime with 10 s. CADRE's evidence is flight hardware and flight software under test; ours is simulation.

4. Experiment design

4.1 Questions and hypotheses

We pre-registered the hypotheses [48] in a dated file in the repository before the main runs [43]. The confirmatory family, tested at the middle load with Holm's correction, is:

  • H1, sharing. Pooled regimes with full Earth coverage cut the mean urgent wait relative to owners alone with full coverage, at 30 and 60 % load.
  • H2, availability. A pooled dispatcher on Earth with strict fallback keeps urgent cargo waiting longer than the full-information marketplace, by more than 10 % of the marketplace's mean (the lower end of the 95 % interval must clear that margin), in each start year whose base visibility is below 60 % and pooled over years. Secondary: planning ahead recovers part; relay 0.8 comes within 10 % of the marketplace by an equivalence test.
  • H3a, decentralisation. At message loss up to 0.10, the robots' auction is within ±10 % of the full-information marketplace (two one-sided tests [46, 47]). Exploratory: deeper bundles do worse at loss 0.25–0.40 (H3b); the elected leader matches the auction unless the network splits (H3c).
  • H4, learning. At 30–60 % load, learned bids beat hand-built bids on the held-out seeds; at 10 % load the difference is within ±2 %. The linear model and every test outside the training range are reported whichever way they come out.
  • H6, team lifts. Allowing cross-owner pairs raises the share of items delivered; at loss 0.25 and above, failed meetings rise without the commit-and-timeout step.

H5 (payments) and H7–H10 (safety, Mars, sizing) belong to the companion papers [44, 45]. Two further hypotheses, H13 on the protocol's load and H14 on the lost link, come from a second pre-registration written for the protocol experiments [54] and are stated in Section 4.8.

4.3 Seeds, start years and common random numbers

A seed fixes the landings, the manifests, the item masses, the team draws, the base computer's failures and the message losses, so all regimes face the same year when they share a seed. Evaluation uses two blocks. Block one is seeds 1–30 with start years 2028 to 2031 in rotation (start year 2028 plus the seed modulo 4), on the link windows of [42]. Block two is start years 2032, 2035, 2038, 2041 and 2044, each with seeds 1–10, on the extended windows. E40 uses both blocks, 80 runs per cell; E41 and E42 use block one. Training used seeds 1000 and up and tuning seeds 2000 and up; no evaluation seed was used for either.

4.4 Outcomes

The primary outcome is the mean waiting time of urgent cargo in a run, in hours, from the moment the item is ready to its pickup. Items still waiting at the end of the year count with their wait so far (censored at the horizon); cargo on the heavy-lift list is excluded. The secondary outcomes are the 95th percentile of urgent wait, the share of urgent items picked up within 24 h, tonnes delivered and failed team-lift meetings; the message and driving measures are reported in [44]. Because censored means mix waiting with not moving at all, we also report the mean over delivered items only, labelled as such.

4.5 Statistics

Every comparison is paired by seed and start year. {{P1-PAIRING-SENTENCE}} We report the mean paired difference with a percentile bootstrap 95 % interval from 20,000 resamples of the pairs [53]. The confirmatory family {H1, H2, H4 (linear), H4 (network), H6} at the middle load is corrected with Holm's procedure [52] (Table 7); H3a is an equivalence claim tested separately (Section 5.4); all other intervals are per comparison and unadjusted. A claim of no practical difference needs two one-sided tests at a margin of ±10 % of the full-information marketplace's mean (±2 % for H4 at light load), using the 90 % interval [46, 47]. For H2 the comparison is made in each start year below 60 % visibility and pooled; the block of start years is reported, and the pooled interval includes the variation between years. The rule for adding seeds (blocks of 10 up to 60 if the half-width exceeded 10 % of the mean) was fixed in advance. {{P1-SEEDS-ADDED}}

4.6 Choices made after looking at results

Five changes were made to the registered plan [43]. First, the owner of the study regrouped the planned papers; the hypotheses of this paper were unchanged. Second, after a five-seed development run showed that the gap between owners alone and the full-information marketplace was almost all cargo that owners alone cannot move, we replaced H2's yardstick (“half that gap”) with the comparison inside the pooled fleet given above; the original yardstick is still reported. Third, after the learned models had been evaluated once on seeds 1–30, we decided to run both in every experiment instead of choosing one, and froze both. The lost-link rule (Section 3.9), the hopper refuelling variant, the decision to let owners alone pair their own robots, and the owners-alone regime with full coverage (which H1 names but the first cell list lacked) were set during development, before any main run of the cells they affect. A fourth change was made after the main runs and before the final analysis, when two defects were found in the runs. Regimes that never wait for Earth give the same run for a seed whatever the start year, so their second block (five start years, seeds 1–10) repeated runs; every comparison between two such regimes therefore uses the first block only, as the registered design states, and an interim statement that the auction beat the marketplace, which counted the repeats as independent runs, is withdrawn. The auction had ordered robots by a per-process hash, so its runs under message loss were valid draws but not bit-reproducible; the order was fixed and the affected runs were repeated, with the old rows archived and compared with the new ones. Both are dated in the pre-registration file [43]. The fifth change was made after the first full analysis and the second draft of this paper, when a defect in the engine's event handling was found while timing the largest cells of a later experiment: {{P1-FIXPOINT-SENTENCE}}

4.7 Compute

Runs were made on one laptop with 8 GB of memory, one process at a time per experiment, in chunks of at most 540 s that resume from the last finished run. A simulated year took {{P1-RUNTIME-RANGE}} depending on the regime and load, with at most {{P1-PEAK-MB}} of memory. The learned models' training took {{P1-TRAIN-WALL-H}} in all.

4.8 The protocol experiments (E53 and E54)

Two experiments outside the engine test the protocol. They were pre-registered separately on 11 November 2026, before either had a result [54]. That file numbers its hypotheses H9 to H16; H13 and H14 belong to this paper, and its H9 and H10 are not the H9 and H10 of the first pre-registration [43].

  • H13, protocol load. At one position report a second, 20 rovers and authentication on, the busiest link carries no more than the {{P1-E53-NB-KBPS}} assumed for the radio in the companion paper [44]; at ten reports a second it carries more.
  • H14, lost link. At message loss 0.25 and with a 14-day Earth blackout, store-and-forward with the lost-link rule loses fewer jobs than drop-and-retry, by a paired interval that excludes zero, and leaves no reservation stuck past its timeout.

Both sit in that file's confirmatory family together with three hypotheses that are tested outside this paper, so the family's Holm correction is not computed here. H13 is decided by a count and has no sampling test; H14's interval is reported unadjusted.

E53 counts bytes. Each rover sends a position report at 0.1, 1 or 10 Hz, status and intent reports at 0.1 Hz, a heartbeat whenever it would otherwise send less than one message a second, and the event messages (reservation requests, grants and releases; bids, awards and task reports; landing alerts) at the rates per robot-hour measured in the companion paper's instrumented runs [44]. Sizes are the codec's, either with the {{P1-LRIP-MAC-B}} code and signed awards and grants, or with no security at all. For the mesh, 5, 10, 20, 40 and 80 rovers are placed at positions drawn from the fleet's own trajectories of July 2028, {{P1-E53-DRAWS}} draws for each fleet size, with 2.5 m antennas, the 10 m mast and line of sight within 5 km as in Section 3.4. Reports travel one hop and are not relayed. Events travel to the base along shortest paths, and every hop is another transmission. The load of the busiest link is the largest sum of transmissions that any node, the mast included, can hear. It is compared with the {{P1-E53-NB-KBPS}} narrow-band channel and a {{P1-E53-BB-KBPS}} broadband one, as raw load and as airtime on a scheduled channel (efficiency {{P1-E53-ETA-SCHED}}, Assumed) and on slotted random access (efficiency {{P1-E53-ETA-ALOHA}}; Proposition 2). A second geometry, added after the pre-registration and not used for H13, places the rovers uniformly over all route samples. Nothing is transmitted: there is no radio model beyond line of sight, no interference and no channel-access protocol.

E54 replays one month's messages. {{P1-E54-DESIGN-SENTENCE}} A link exists in a 60 s step when two nodes are in line of sight within 5 km at their recorded positions. E54 is not a run of the engine. Inside the engine a lost message is retried three minutes later and the dispatcher knows at once that it failed, so a job cannot be lost there; the replay asks what would have happened to the same month's messages under each transport. Two approximations follow. The return to the last link point is applied to the contact plan and not driven in the engine, so the engine's later dispatch does not see the detour. A returning vehicle is given a direct link to the base, which is optimistic when its last link was through another vehicle. {{P1-E54-LEDGER-DESIGN}}
