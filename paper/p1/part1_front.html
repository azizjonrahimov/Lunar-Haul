Working Together Without Earth: Job Sharing, Team Lifts and Learned Bidding Among Lunar Robots of Different Owners

Azizjon Rahimov
Roatify Research · Preprint, October 2026 · Code, data and pre-registration: github.com/azizjonrahimov/Lunar-Haul · Live site: azizjonrahimov.github.io/Lunar-Haul

Abstract. The rovers, cargo vehicles and small robots planned for the lunar south pole belong to different companies, each with its own operators on Earth. Sharing jobs and vehicles among them shortens waits, but a shared dispatcher on Earth can act only while Earth is above the base's horizon, which at our site leaves gaps of about two weeks. We asked whether the gain from sharing survives when the decisions move onto the robots, which hear one another only where terrain allows and lose messages. We simulated a base on the Connecting Ridge with six real 2028–2032 vehicle classes owned by three companies, a robot radio mesh computed from 5 m laser-altimeter terrain and the base's real Earth-link windows, under {{P1-N-REGIMES}} decision regimes, in {{P1-N-RUNS-ALL}} pre-registered simulated years. At {{P1-LOAD-MAIN}} load, a pooled dispatcher on Earth that stops when Earth sets kept urgent cargo waiting {{P1-H2-STRICT-MINUS-O}} longer on average than one that never stops (95 % CI {{P1-H2-STRICT-MINUS-O-CI}}); planning ahead before each blackout left {{P1-PLAN-MINUS-O}} of that. An auction run by the robots themselves, at {{P1-LOSS-MAIN}} message loss, cut the wait by a further {{P1-H3A-PCT-ABS}}. A small learned correction to the robots' bids, a linear model with {{P1-LIN-N-WEIGHTS}} weights, cut it by {{P1-H4-LIN-PCT-30-ABS}} relative to hand-built bids; a {{P1-MLP-N-PARAMS}}-parameter network trained the same way did not improve on them.

Keywords: lunar south pole, multi-robot task allocation, sequential single-item auction, consensus-based bundle algorithm, multi-owner fleets, cooperative transport, intermittent communication, Earth visibility, learned bidding, discrete-event simulation, pre-registration

1. Introduction

The first vehicles of a lunar south-pole base will not share an owner. NASA's Lunar Terrain Vehicle task orders of May 2026 went to two companies, Astrolab and Lunar Outpost, for two different rovers [37]. Cargo rovers able to move a tonne or more exist so far only as company designs, small rovers are sold by a third group of firms, and propulsive hoppers by a fourth. Each company plans, drives and supervises its own vehicles from its own control room on Earth. A crate that one company's rover could carry may wait for the owner's own rover to come free, and a crate too heavy for any single rover needs two rovers that may belong to different companies.

Earth is a poor place to coordinate them from. Seen from the south pole, Earth stays within a few degrees of the horizon and terrain hides it for about half of every month [42]. In our previous paper [42] a rover antenna at our base on the Connecting Ridge saw some part of Earth's disc 59 % of the time, in windows of about 16 days separated by gaps of about 11 days, and the oxygen plant 1.2 km away never saw it. NASA's relay programme puts direct-to-Earth coverage at candidate south-pole sites at 17–43 % of a year and coverage with one relay at 80–86 % [38]. A dispatcher on Earth, however good, is blind and mute for days at a time unless someone pays for the relay, and the relay does not reach the oxygen plant either.

Pooling vehicles across owners pays. In our earlier simulation of this base [40], one marketplace that offered every job to every vehicle kept urgent cargo waiting far less than owners working alone. Ride-hailing gives the same result with real data: splitting demand among operators that do not coordinate raises the fleet needed, and the smallest operator pays most [28]. That marketplace, however, assumed a dispatcher that sees every vehicle and every crate at every moment. On the Moon the information sits on the robots, and the robots can talk only to the robots they can see over the terrain. Our question in this paper is whether the gain from sharing survives when decisions move off Earth and onto the robots, under radio links limited by terrain and with messages that get lost.

Each part of this question has been studied alone. Auctions among robots are the standard way to share tasks without a central computer, and their performance under message loss has been measured: the best decentralised allocators lose about 11–13 % of makespan between perfect delivery and 40 % message loss, and settings tuned for perfect links can lose up to 65 % under loss [10, 11]. Those studies use grids or open plains with random or bursty loss, robots of one owner, and compare each algorithm with itself under perfect links. NASA's CADRE rovers will coordinate on the Moon through a leader elected among them [31, 32]. Learned bids inside an auction were published in 2026 [18]. We did not find a study that compares a dispatcher on Earth, limited to the base's real Earth-link windows, with allocation by the robots on the Moon, for vehicles of several owners, over a radio mesh computed from real terrain, and measures the difference in cargo delay (Section 2.7).

We built that comparison on the simulation of [40, 41, 42]. The fleet is six vehicle classes from 2028–2032 programmes, from 80 kg crew rovers to a 2,000 kg cargo rover, split among three owners. Which robot hears which comes from line of sight over NASA's 5 m terrain of the ridge, with a 10 m mast at the base. Earth's visibility from the base comes from the windows of [42], extended to 2046. The decision regimes span the range from owners working alone with conflicts settled by hand on Earth, through one pooled dispatcher on Earth with and without a relay, to three ways of deciding on the Moon: a computer at the base elected as leader, an auction among the robots with hand-built bids, and the same auction with learned bids. The pooled marketplace of [40], given full information and no dependence on Earth, is the yardstick. Crates too heavy for one rover can be lifted by two, from different owners, if both agree over the radio. We wrote the hypotheses, the comparisons and the statistics down before the main runs [43] and changed them three times, each time before the runs they affect (Section 4.6).

The answer depends on what is shared. At {{P1-LOAD-MAIN}} load, most of the gap between owners working alone and the pooled fleet ({{P1-H1-CAPACITY-SHARE}} of it) has nothing to do with where decisions are made: some owners' vehicles cannot lift their own cargo, and only pooling lets another owner's rover or a cross-owner pair carry it (Section 5.2). Among pooled fleets, where the decision is made matters a great deal. A dispatcher on Earth that stops when Earth sets kept urgent cargo waiting {{P1-H2-STRICT-OVER-O-RATIO}} times as long as one that never stops, and the difference passed the pre-registered test in each of the {{P1-H2-N-LOWVIS-YEARS}} start years in which the base saw Earth less than 60 % of the time (Section 5.3). Uploading plans before each blackout recovered most of the loss, and so did an 80 % relay. Deciding on the Moon recovered all of it: the robots' own auction, at {{P1-LOSS-MAIN}} message loss, kept urgent cargo waiting {{P1-H3A-PCT-ABS}} less than the full-information marketplace (Section 5.4), and a learned correction to the bids took another {{P1-H4-LIN-PCT-30-ABS}} off (Section 5.6).

Two pre-registered expectations failed, and we report them as they came out. We expected the robots' auction to come within 10 % of the full-information marketplace; it did better than the marketplace by more than that margin, so the equivalence test fails in the favourable direction, and the marketplace turned out to be a reference rather than a bound. We expected a small neural network to carry any gain from learning; it did not beat the hand-built bids at any load, and a linear model with the same inputs carried all of it. We also expected learning to help nothing at light load, and the linear model helped there too.

Our contributions are:

  • A measured cost of keeping the dispatcher on Earth. For one south-pole base, the urgent-cargo delay of a pooled fleet dispatched from Earth only while Earth is in view, against the same fleet dispatched without interruption, per start year from 2028 to 2046, with the share that planning ahead and a relay recover.
  • A comparison of decision places on a realistic robot network. Owners alone, a pooled dispatcher on Earth, an elected leader on the Moon and an auction among the robots, on the same seeds, over a radio mesh computed from 5 m terrain, with message loss, bundle depth and radio range varied one at a time.
  • Cross-owner team lifts under message loss. How much cargo only becomes movable when two owners' robots lift together, and what a meeting protocol with a commit step and a timeout costs when messages are lost.
  • An honest test of learned bidding. A learned correction to the auction's bids, trained by imitation and by search on the simulated objective, evaluated once on held-out seeds against hand-built bids, a linear model with the same inputs and six conditions outside its training range.

Several things are outside this paper. Owners here report their costs honestly and accept the assignments they win; payments, gain sharing and owners who misreport are the subject of a companion paper [44]. Rules of the road, separation and the safety check that every vehicle runs before it moves are the subject of another [45]; here vehicles do not interfere with one another on the road. The radio model is geometric: a link exists when two antennas see each other within a set range, with no link budget, and the range and clearance are provisional values that we vary. Robots that nobody hears drive to a rally point that the base's mast can see; that rule is our assumption, and without it the robots' network falls apart on this terrain (Section 3.9). The full-information marketplace uses the same bid rule as the hand-built auction with perfect knowledge; it is not an optimal dispatcher, and two of our regimes beat it. One base layout, one terrain tile and one demand model are simulated, at three levels of demand of which the lightest is the closest to today's plans.
