Working Together Without Earth: Job Sharing, Team Lifts, Learned Bidding and a Message Protocol for Lunar Robots of Different Owners

1. Introduction

The first vehicles of a lunar south-pole base will not share an owner. NASA's Lunar Terrain Vehicle task orders of May 2026 went to two companies, Astrolab and Lunar Outpost, for two different rovers [37]. Cargo rovers able to move a tonne or more exist so far only as company designs, small rovers are sold by a third group of firms, and propulsive hoppers by a fourth. Each company plans, drives and supervises its own vehicles from its own control room on Earth. A crate that one company's rover could carry may wait for the owner's own rover to come free, and a crate too heavy for any single rover needs two rovers that may belong to different companies.

Earth is a poor place to coordinate them from. Seen from the south pole, Earth stays within a few degrees of the horizon and terrain hides it for about half of every month [42]. In our previous paper [42] a rover antenna at our base on the Connecting Ridge saw some part of Earth's disc 59 % of the time, in windows of about 16 days separated by gaps of about 11 days, and the oxygen plant 1.2 km away never saw it. NASA's relay programme puts direct-to-Earth coverage at candidate south-pole sites at 17–43 % of a year and coverage with one relay at 80–86 % [38]. A dispatcher on Earth, however good, is blind and mute for days at a time unless someone pays for the relay.

Pooling vehicles across owners pays. In our earlier simulation of this base [40], one marketplace that offered every job to every vehicle kept urgent cargo waiting far less than owners working alone. Ride-hailing gives the same result with real data: splitting demand among operators that do not coordinate raises the fleet needed, and the smallest operator pays most [28]. That marketplace, however, assumed a dispatcher that sees every vehicle and every crate at every moment. On the Moon the information sits on the robots, and the robots can talk only to the robots they can see over the terrain. Our question in this paper is whether the gain from sharing survives when decisions move off Earth and onto the robots, under radio links limited by terrain and with messages that get lost.

Each part of this question has been studied alone. Auctions among robots are the standard way to share tasks without a central computer, and their performance under message loss has been measured: the best decentralised allocators lose about 11–13 % of makespan between perfect delivery and 40 % message loss, and settings tuned for perfect links can lose up to 65 % under loss [10, 11]. Those studies use grids or open plains with random or bursty loss, robots of one owner, and compare each algorithm with itself under perfect links. NASA's CADRE rovers, built and not yet flown, are designed to coordinate on the Moon through a leader elected among them [31, 71]. Learned bids inside an auction were published in 2026 [18]. We did not find a study that compares a dispatcher on Earth, limited to the base's real Earth-link windows, with allocation by the robots on the Moon, for vehicles of several owners, over a radio mesh computed from real terrain, and measures the difference in cargo delay (Section 2.7).

We built that comparison on the simulation of [40, 41, 42]. The fleet is six vehicle classes from 2028–2032 programmes, from 80 kg crew rovers to a 2,000 kg cargo rover, split among three owners. Which robot hears which comes from line of sight over NASA's 5 m terrain of the ridge, with a 10 m mast at the base. Earth's visibility from the base comes from the windows of [42], extended to 2046. The decision regimes span the range from owners working alone with conflicts settled by hand on Earth, through one pooled dispatcher on Earth with and without a relay, to three ways of deciding on the Moon: a computer at the base elected as leader, an auction among the robots with hand-built bids, and the same auction with learned bids. The pooled marketplace of [40], given full information and no dependence on Earth, is the yardstick. Crates too heavy for one rover can be lifted by two, from different owners, if both agree over the radio. We wrote the hypotheses, the comparisons and the statistics down before the main runs [43] and changed them five times, three times before the runs they affect and twice after the runs, to correct defects found in them; Section 4.6 lists those changes and four further departures from the registered analysis.

The answer depends on what is shared. In the fleet we simulate, one owner has a third of the cargo and no vehicle that can lift most of it, and by the end of a year owners working alone had left {{P1-A-BACKLOG-T-30}} undelivered at {{P1-LOAD-MAIN}} load. The gain from pooling is that cargo. On the cargo that both deliver, owners alone with Earth always in view waited {{P1-H1-DELIV-DIFF}} longer than the pooled fleet ({{P1-H1-DELIV-DIFF-CI}}); the much larger difference in the primary outcome, {{P1-H1-DIFF-30}}, counts urgent cargo that had not been delivered by the end of the year with its wait up to then, so its size depends on the one-year horizon (Section 5.2). Among pooled fleets, where the decision is made matters a great deal. A dispatcher on Earth that stops when Earth sets kept urgent cargo waiting {{P1-H2-STRICT-OVER-O-RATIO}} times as long as one that never stops, and the difference passed its test, on the yardstick as we revised it after a five-seed development run and before the main runs, in each of the {{P1-H2-N-LOWVIS-YEARS}} start years in which the base saw Earth less than 60 % of the time (Section 5.3). Uploading plans before each blackout recovered most of the loss, and so did an {{P1-RELAY08-SHARE}} relay. Deciding on the Moon left none of it that the data could detect: the robots' own auction, at {{P1-LOSS-MAIN}} message loss, {{P1-H3A-INTRO-CLAUSE}} (Section 5.4). {{P1-B117A-INTRO-SENTENCE}} A learned linear correction to the bids, registered as the secondary model, cut the wait by {{P1-H4-LIN-PCT-30-ABS}} relative to the auction; the network that the registration names as the learned regime did not (Section 5.6). {{P1-B117C-INTRO-SENTENCE}}

Decisions made on the Moon travel as messages, and no standard says what two vehicles of different owners tell each other: the space-link standards and NASA's LunaNet specification stop below that layer [39]. For this version of the paper we wrote the layer down, implemented its encoding and counted its bytes. The Lunar Rover Interaction Protocol (LRIP v0.2) is {{P1-LRIP-N-MSG}} message types and a capability manifest, carried over the existing standards (Section 3.12). The protocol does not run inside the simulation engine: no simulated vehicle exchanges its messages. A position report with its authentication code is a packet of {{P1-PROP6-SECURED-B}} before link framing and coding. {{P1-LRIP-INTRO-SENTENCE}} In a replay of one month's messages, {{P1-E54-ABSTRACT-CLAUSE}}; and {{P1-E54-AOI-CLAUSE}} (Section 5.9). Six propositions in Appendix B state what can be proved, each with its assumption, and Table 12 marks every claim of the paper as proved, tested, calculated from assumed inputs, assumed or not tested. None of this was run on radio hardware, and the protocol has one implementation.

Three pre-registered expectations failed, and we report them as they came out. We expected the robots' auction to come within 10 % of the full-information marketplace; {{P1-H3A-SENTENCE}} We expected a small neural network to carry any gain from learning; it did not beat the hand-built bids at any load, and a linear model with the same inputs, which the registration lists only as a secondary report, carried all of it; a control run afterwards found that at the middle load the hand-built rule, once tuned, does better than either (Section 5.6). We also expected learning to help nothing at light load, {{P1-H4-LIGHT-CLAUSE}}.

Our contributions are:

  • A measured cost of keeping the dispatcher on Earth. For one south-pole base, the urgent-cargo delay of a pooled fleet dispatched from Earth only while Earth is in view, against the same fleet dispatched without interruption, per start year from 2028 to 2046, with the share that planning ahead and a relay recover.
  • A comparison of decision places on a realistic robot network. Owners alone, a pooled dispatcher on Earth, an elected leader on the Moon and an auction among the robots, on the same seeds, over a radio mesh computed from 5 m terrain, with message loss, bundle depth and radio range varied one at a time.
  • Cross-owner team lifts under message loss. How much cargo only becomes movable when two owners' robots lift together, and what a meeting protocol with a commit step and a timeout costs when messages are lost.
  • A test of learned bidding that reports its failure. A learned correction to the auction's bids, found by search on the simulated objective from a start at which imitation had left it at zero, evaluated against hand-built bids on seeds that no training or selection used, as a network (the registered model) and as a linear model with the same inputs (the secondary one), and in five conditions outside the training range; and two controls run afterwards, the linear model's weights removed one at a time and the hand-built rule tuned by the same search, which show where the linear model's gain comes from and that a tuned hand-built rule exceeds it at the load both were tuned on and, at twice that load, closes part of the gap but not all of it.
  • A protocol between vehicles of different owners, written down, with its encoding implemented and its bytes counted. {{P1-LRIP-N-MSG}} message types and a capability manifest over existing space-link standards, with byte counts from a working codec, golden packets as the interoperability test, the load offered to the radio by 5 to 80 rovers as a design calculation, and a message-level replay of one month under three transports.
  • Guarantees with their assumptions. Six propositions, of which four are proofs (a bound on one greedy auction round in score below a ceiling, which is not a bound on cost or waiting; the bit rate a fleet needs; the age of a relayed report; hand-over after a failure) and two are counts on inputs we chose (operator workload, the size of a position report), and a table that marks every claim as proved, tested, calculated from assumed inputs, assumed or not tested.

Several things are outside this paper. Owners here report their costs honestly and accept the assignments they win; payments, gain sharing and owners who misreport are the subject of a companion paper [44]. Rules of the road, separation and the safety check that every vehicle runs before it moves are the subject of another [45]; here vehicles do not interfere with one another on the road. The radio model is geometric: a link exists when two antennas see each other within a set range, with no link budget, and the range and clearance are provisional values that we vary. Robots that nobody hears drive to a rally point that the base's mast can see; that rule is our assumption, and without it the robots' network falls apart on this terrain (Section 3.9). The full-information marketplace uses the same bid rule as the hand-built auction with perfect knowledge; it is not an optimal dispatcher, and the learned linear bids beat it. The protocol is counted and replayed, not transmitted and not run in the engine: sizes come from the codec and loads from positions on the terrain mesh, with no radio hardware, no link budget and no channel-access protocol. The ownership split, three owners with a third of the cargo each and one of them without a cargo rover, is an input that we did not vary in the main experiments. One base layout, one terrain tile and one demand model are simulated, at three levels of demand of which the lightest is the closest to today's plans.
