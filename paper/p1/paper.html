Working Together Without Earth: Job Sharing, Team Lifts, Learned Bidding and a Message Protocol for Lunar Robots of Different Owners

Azizjon Rahimov
Roatify Research · Preprint, October 2026, revised November 2026 · Code, data and pre-registration: github.com/azizjonrahimov/Lunar-Haul · Live site: azizjonrahimov.github.io/Lunar-Haul

Abstract. Rovers planned for the lunar south pole belong to different companies, and a dispatcher on Earth acts only while Earth is above the base's horizon. We ask whether the gain from sharing survives when decisions move onto the robots. We simulated a ridge base with six vehicle classes of three owners, one without a cargo rover, under thirteen decision regimes, in 5,480 simulated years, at most 4,699 distinct. At 30 % load, a pooled dispatcher on Earth that stops when Earth sets kept urgent cargo waiting 89.9 h longer for delivery than one that never stops; planning ahead left 7.3 h. The robots' auction, at 0.1 message loss, came within 0.8 h of the full-information marketplace, not distinguishable from zero but too wide to show equivalence. The registered learned bid, a network, did not beat hand-built bids; a linear correction, registered as secondary, cut the urgent wait by 16.5 %. The gain from sharing is cargo that owners alone had not delivered by year-end; on cargo both delivered, owners alone with Earth always in view waited 5.8 h longer. The robots' 15 message types are written down and their encoding implemented: an authenticated position report is a 41 B packet before link framing and coding; by calculation 20 rovers reporting once a second offer 8.4 kbit/s, which fits a 16 kbit/s channel only if the base schedules it and reports travel one hop. Replaying a month's messages, drop-and-retry capped at five tries lost 10 of 6,028 awards at 25 % loss, store-and-forward 2, and none when unheard vehicles were assumed to return to their last link point. An always-up relay equals the marketplace run for run; the auction was not distinguishable from it; only an 80 % relay was worse than the auction, by 3.8 h (2.0 to 5.7 h).

Keywords: lunar south pole, multi-robot task allocation, multi-owner fleets, cooperative transport, intermittent communication, learned bidding, interoperability protocol

1. Introduction

The first vehicles of a lunar south-pole base will not share an owner. NASA's Lunar Terrain Vehicle task orders of May 2026 went to two companies, Astrolab and Lunar Outpost, for two different rovers [37]. Cargo rovers able to move a tonne or more exist so far only as company designs, small rovers are sold by a third group of firms, and propulsive hoppers by a fourth. Each company plans, drives and supervises its own vehicles from its own control room on Earth. A crate that one company's rover could carry may wait for the owner's own rover to come free, and a crate too heavy for any single rover needs two rovers that may belong to different companies.

Earth is a poor place to coordinate them from. Seen from the south pole, Earth stays within a few degrees of the horizon and terrain hides it for about half of every month [42]. In our previous paper [42] a rover antenna at our base on the Connecting Ridge saw some part of Earth's disc 59 % of the time, in windows of about 16 days separated by gaps of about 11 days, and the oxygen plant 1.2 km away never saw it. NASA's relay programme puts direct-to-Earth coverage at candidate south-pole sites at 17–43 % of a year and coverage with one relay at 80–86 % [38]. A dispatcher on Earth, however good, is blind and mute for days at a time unless someone pays for the relay.

Pooling vehicles across owners pays. In our earlier simulation of this base [40], one marketplace that offered every job to every vehicle kept urgent cargo waiting far less than owners working alone. Ride-hailing gives the same result with real data: splitting demand among operators that do not coordinate raises the fleet needed, and the smallest operator pays most [28]. That marketplace, however, assumed a dispatcher that sees every vehicle and every crate at every moment. On the Moon the information sits on the robots, and the robots can talk only to the robots they can see over the terrain. Our question in this paper is whether the gain from sharing survives when decisions move off Earth and onto the robots, under radio links limited by terrain and with messages that get lost.

Each part of this question has been studied alone. Auctions among robots are the standard way to share tasks without a central computer, and their performance under message loss has been measured: the best decentralised allocators lose about 11–13 % of makespan between perfect delivery and 40 % message loss, and settings tuned for perfect links can lose up to 65 % under loss [10, 11]. Those studies use grids or open plains with random or bursty loss, robots of one owner, and compare each algorithm with itself under perfect links. NASA's CADRE rovers, built and not yet flown, are designed to coordinate on the Moon through a leader elected among them [31, 71]. Learned bids inside an auction were published in 2026 [18]. We did not find a study that compares a dispatcher on Earth, limited to the base's real Earth-link windows, with allocation by the robots on the Moon, for vehicles of several owners, over a radio mesh computed from real terrain, and measures the difference in cargo delay (Section 2.7).

We built that comparison on the simulation of [40, 41, 42]. The fleet is six vehicle classes from 2028–2032 programmes, from 80 kg crew rovers to a 2,000 kg cargo rover, split among three owners. Which robot hears which comes from line of sight over NASA's 5 m terrain of the ridge, with a 10 m mast at the base. Earth's visibility from the base comes from the windows of [42], extended to 2046. The decision regimes span the range from owners working alone with conflicts settled by hand on Earth, through one pooled dispatcher on Earth with and without a relay, to three ways of deciding on the Moon: a computer at the base elected as leader, an auction among the robots with hand-built bids, and the same auction with learned bids. The pooled marketplace of [40], given full information and no dependence on Earth, is the yardstick. Crates too heavy for one rover can be lifted by two, from different owners, if both agree over the radio. We wrote the hypotheses, the comparisons and the statistics down before the main runs [43] and changed them five times, three times before the runs they affect and twice after the runs, to correct defects found in them; Section 4.6 lists those changes and four further departures from the registered analysis.

The answer depends on what is shared. In the fleet we simulate, one owner has a third of the cargo and no vehicle that can lift most of it, and by the end of a year owners working alone had left 513 t undelivered at 30 % load. The gain from pooling is that cargo. On the cargo that both deliver, owners alone with Earth always in view waited 5.8 h longer than the pooled fleet (3.7 to 7.6 h); the much larger difference in the primary outcome, 1,232 h, counts urgent cargo that had not been delivered by the end of the year with its wait up to then, so its size depends on the one-year horizon (Section 5.2). Among pooled fleets, where the decision is made matters a great deal. A dispatcher on Earth that stops when Earth sets kept urgent cargo waiting 7.0 times as long as one that never stops, and the difference passed its test, on the yardstick as we revised it after a five-seed development run and before the main runs, in each of the six start years in which the base saw Earth less than 60 % of the time (Section 5.3). Uploading plans before each blackout recovered most of the loss, and so did an 80 % relay. Deciding on the Moon left none of it that the data could detect: the robots' own auction, at 0.1 message loss, kept urgent cargo waiting 5.6 % less than the full-information marketplace, a difference whose 90 % interval (−17 % to +5 %) includes zero (Section 5.4). A learned linear correction to the bids, registered as the secondary model, cut the wait by 16.5 % relative to the auction; the network that the registration names as the learned regime did not (Section 5.6).

Decisions made on the Moon travel as messages, and no standard says what two vehicles of different owners tell each other: the space-link standards and NASA's LunaNet specification stop below that layer [39]. For this version of the paper we wrote the layer down, implemented its encoding and counted its bytes. The Lunar Rover Interaction Protocol (LRIP v0.2) is 15 message types and a capability manifest, carried over the existing standards (Section 3.12). The protocol does not run inside the simulation engine: no simulated vehicle exchanges its messages. A position report with its authentication code is a packet of 41 B before link framing and coding. By the sizes and rates we assumed, twenty rovers reporting once a second offer 8.37 kbit/s, inside the 16 kbit/s we had budgeted, and 67.4 kbit/s at ten reports a second, which does not fit. This is a design calculation and not a test, and it holds only on a channel that the base schedules, with reports travelling one hop (Section 5.8). In a replay of one month's messages, drop-and-retry capped at five tries lost 10 of 6,028 awards at 25 % message loss; store-and-forward that retries for the whole lease lost 2, and none when unheard vehicles were assumed to return to their last link point; and the base's picture of a vehicle was 88 min old on average without the return to the last link point and 9.5 s with it, a gain counted without the jobs the return would interrupt (Section 5.9). Six propositions in Appendix B state what can be proved, each with its assumption, and Table 12 marks every claim of the paper as proved, tested, calculated from assumed inputs, assumed or not tested. None of this was run on radio hardware, and the protocol has one implementation.

Three pre-registered expectations failed, and we report them as they came out. We expected the robots' auction to come within 10 % of the full-information marketplace; the test could not show it. The difference was −0.84 h with a 90 % interval of −2.6 to 0.7 h, which reaches past the margin of ±1.5 h on at least one side, so the auction may be better or worse than the marketplace by more than 10 %, and the data cannot tell. We expected a small neural network to carry any gain from learning; it did not beat the hand-built bids at any load, and a linear model with the same inputs, which the registration lists only as a secondary report, carried all of it. We also expected learning to help nothing at light load, and there the data could show neither a gain nor its absence: the gain's interval (−0.1 to 2.2 h) includes zero and reaches past the ±2 % margin.

Our contributions are:

  • A measured cost of keeping the dispatcher on Earth. For one south-pole base, the urgent-cargo delay of a pooled fleet dispatched from Earth only while Earth is in view, against the same fleet dispatched without interruption, per start year from 2028 to 2046, with the share that planning ahead and a relay recover.
  • A comparison of decision places on a realistic robot network. Owners alone, a pooled dispatcher on Earth, an elected leader on the Moon and an auction among the robots, on the same seeds, over a radio mesh computed from 5 m terrain, with message loss, bundle depth and radio range varied one at a time.
  • Cross-owner team lifts under message loss. How much cargo only becomes movable when two owners' robots lift together, and what a meeting protocol with a commit step and a timeout costs when messages are lost.
  • A test of learned bidding that reports its failure. A learned correction to the auction's bids, found by search on the simulated objective from a start at which imitation had left it at zero, evaluated against hand-built bids on seeds that no training or selection used, as a network (the registered model) and as a linear model with the same inputs (the secondary one), and in five conditions outside the training range.
  • A protocol between vehicles of different owners, written down, with its encoding implemented and its bytes counted. 15 message types and a capability manifest over existing space-link standards, with byte counts from a working codec, golden packets as the interoperability test, the load offered to the radio by 5 to 80 rovers as a design calculation, and a message-level replay of one month under three transports.
  • Guarantees with their assumptions. Six propositions, of which four are proofs (a bound on one greedy auction round in score below a ceiling, which is not a bound on cost or waiting; the bit rate a fleet needs; the age of a relayed report; hand-over after a failure) and two are counts on inputs we chose (operator workload, the size of a position report), and a table that marks every claim as proved, tested, calculated from assumed inputs, assumed or not tested.

Several things are outside this paper. Owners here report their costs honestly and accept the assignments they win; payments, gain sharing and owners who misreport are the subject of a companion paper [44]. Rules of the road, separation and the safety check that every vehicle runs before it moves are the subject of another [45]; here vehicles do not interfere with one another on the road. The radio model is geometric: a link exists when two antennas see each other within a set range, with no link budget, and the range and clearance are provisional values that we vary. Robots that nobody hears drive to a rally point that the base's mast can see; that rule is our assumption, and without it the robots' network falls apart on this terrain (Section 3.9). The full-information marketplace uses the same bid rule as the hand-built auction with perfect knowledge; it is not an optimal dispatcher, and two of our regimes beat it. The protocol is counted and replayed, not transmitted and not run in the engine: sizes come from the codec and loads from positions on the terrain mesh, with no radio hardware, no link budget and no channel-access protocol. The ownership split, three owners with a third of the cargo each and one of them without a cargo rover, is an input that we did not vary in the main experiments. One base layout, one terrain tile and one demand model are simulated, at three levels of demand of which the lightest is the closest to today's plans.

2. Related work

2.1 Sharing tasks among robots

Gerkey and Matarić classified multi-robot task allocation by whether a robot does one task or several at a time, whether a task needs one robot or several, and whether tasks are assigned once or over time [1]. Our problem has single-task robots, tasks that need one robot or two, and assignment over time as crates land. For the instantaneous single-robot case the Hungarian method gives the optimum in polynomial time, and greedy assignment is within a factor of two of it. The time-extended version is strongly NP-hard. Gerkey and Matarić also judged that for fewer than about 200 robots a centralised assignment over a broadcast network is “likely the better choice” [1], which is the case for one base with a dozen robots, provided a central computer can hear them all.

Auctions are the standard way to share tasks without one. In a sequential single-item auction every robot bids its cost of adding each open task to its plan, the lowest bid wins one task, and the round repeats. Its total cost is at most twice the optimum when travel costs obey the triangle inequality, and in experiments it comes close to combinatorial auctions [2, 3]. The consensus-based bundle algorithm (CBBA) lets each robot build a bundle of several tasks greedily and then settle conflicts with its neighbours only; it is conflict-free and tolerates inconsistent information [4]. Later papers from its authors' group report that it reaches the sequential greedy solution, and that this solution guarantees half the optimal score when each added task is worth no more than the one before [5, 74]; we could not read the original proof [4] and quote the bound as those two papers state it. Our own derivation (Proposition 1, Appendix B) gives one half when each robot takes one task or the limit on bundle length does not bind, and one third when it binds. These are bounds on a score, which for our cost auction means value below a ceiling bid; they are not bounds on cost or on waiting, where a greedy round can be arbitrarily worse than the best assignment. An asynchronous version drops the global rounds [6]. Market-based architectures such as TraderBots let robots trade tasks to raise their own profit, with local leaders planning centrally when that pays [7]. Our on-Moon auction is a sequential single-item auction whose winning bids are rebroadcast, with CBBA's rule for settling conflicts and an optional bundle depth (Section 3.7).

2.2 Allocation when messages are lost

Otte, Kuhlman and Sofge compared six auctions under random and bursty message loss, in simulation and on quadrotors [8]. The ranking of the auctions changed with the loss rate, single-round auctions did worst because a robot that misses the only round sits idle, and rebroadcasting earlier winners in later rounds helped every multi-round auction. Nayak and colleagues compared five decentralised allocators under three loss models and found that all degrade, and that the best algorithm at high communication is not the best at none [9]. Lott and Honary measured the bundle depth of CBBA-type allocators under 25 % loss: the asynchronous CBBA's penalty in total travel grew from 19.7 % at depth 1 to 64.7 % at depth 12, and the best depth moved from 12 with perfect links to 2 under loss [10]. Their second benchmark of six allocators over 25 communication conditions found the best methods losing about 11–13 % of makespan between perfect delivery and 40 % loss [11]. Algorithms for very low communication exist [12].

These studies share a design. Robots move on a grid or an open plane, links fail at random or in bursts, every robot belongs to one team, and each algorithm is compared with itself under perfect links, so the cost of decentralisation relative to a central dispatcher is not measured. Lott and Honary also showed that settings chosen under one communication condition can be wrong under another, and that tuning and evaluation need separate scenarios [10]; we follow that rule (Section 4.3).

2.3 Tasks that need two robots

Assigning tasks that need several robots at once is a set-partitioning problem and strongly NP-hard [1]; by the abstracts of two later analyses, which is all we read of them, it is in general hard to approximate [14, 15]. A later taxonomy, also read in abstract, adds the dependencies between schedules: when two robots must meet, the task's value depends on both robots' plans [13]. Market methods handle complex tasks by letting robots bid on decompositions [16], and a coupled-constraint extension of CBBA handles tasks whose value depends on when or whether another task is done [5]. Two-rover cargo carrying has been demonstrated on lunar-analogue terrain: two 800 kg rovers carried a 475 kg, 5.5 m load with a mean separation error of 9.2 cm, over 13.8 KB/s of traffic between them, in the unit that paper gives [17]. Those rovers belonged to one team and coordinated at the level of control. With a dozen robots and lifts by pairs there are few pairs to enumerate. The hard part is the meeting: both robots must arrive, and under message loss one can wait for a partner that never comes. We found no study of lifts by robots of different owners, or of what a meeting protocol costs under message loss at the level of a logistics operation.

2.4 Learned allocation

Learned allocators either output assignments directly, such as graph-attention policies trained by reinforcement learning or imitation ([21]; [22], read in abstract), or learn scores that a classical solver then assigns. In the second group, a learned incentive feeds the Hungarian method [20], a graph network approximates CBBA's score [19], and a 2026 study replaced CBBA's hand-built bid with a policy trained by proximal policy optimisation while keeping the auction and consensus phases [18]. That study is the closest prior work to our learned bids. We read only its abstract, which describes cooperative robots and does not mention message loss; we could not check its communication model. Ride-hailing dispatch at scale adds a learned value of a driver's position to a combinatorial matching, and its deployed gains are in the low single digits of percent: by its abstract, over 1.3 % more driver income in A/B tests and up to 5.3 % in major metrics after deployment [23]. Neural solvers for routing problems generalise poorly beyond the sizes they were trained on [25], and operations researchers have asked for evaluations against strong classical heuristics on the same instances and hardware [26]. Imitation of an expert by dataset aggregation (DAgger) avoids the compounding errors of plain cloning [24]. We ran only the first iteration of that procedure, found that it left nothing to imitate, and did not run the aggregation loop; the learned bids come from a search on the simulated objective alone (Section 3.10). We keep the auction unchanged and test a network against a linear model with the same inputs.

2.5 Fleets of several owners

Robotics allocation usually assumes cooperative robots of one owner. Transport research has measured what fragmentation among owners costs. Splitting the demand for mobility on demand among platforms that do not coordinate raises the cost of rebalancing, and unequal market shares make it grow faster [27]. With data from five cities, Kondor and colleagues found that each extra operator raises the fleet needed by a factor that ranges from 2.5 % of demand in Manhattan to 67 % in Curitiba, and that in Singapore with shares of 50, 35 and 15 % the smallest operator's fleet grows by 48 % against 8.5 % for the largest [28]. In freight, joint route planning among carriers saves about 20–30 % [51], and carriers rank a trusted coordinator and a fair split of the benefits as the main obstacles [29]. Open-RMF lets robots of unrelated vendors share a traffic schedule and bid for tasks across fleets without changing their own fleet managers [30]; it is the closest working system to what we simulate, and it assumes a building network that is always up.

2.6 Lunar surface autonomy and networks

CADRE, NASA's three-rover technology demonstration, has not flown: its lander, Intuitive Machines' third mission, was planned for the first quarter of 2027 according to a report of 15 September 2026 [73]. Its design runs a central planner on a leader elected among the rovers and the lander's base station, with execution on every rover [31, 71]. The planner schedules tasks greedily in order of priority. There is no auction, a choice its authors make to keep messages few on lossy surface links [71]. The rovers are awake for 25 minutes in every 30, and while they are awake the loss of the leader costs at most 10 s before planning resumes on a new one [71]. CADRE has one owner and three identical rovers of under 10 kg that share at least 1 Mbit/s of mesh radio, and its team reports packet loss from motor interference while the rovers drive [72]; they explore, and there is no cargo. By its abstract, a 2024 lunar variant of the consensus-based auction models payload, priority, lunar night and cooperative tasks for one owner's robots [33], and an auction hierarchy has been simulated for regolith mining [34]. In the DARPA Subterranean Challenge, robots relayed data for one another through mesh radios, dropped relays and carried data out of range [35]; the winning team deployed single-robot autonomy only, citing the field testing that multi-robot coordination would need [36]. Lunar surface traffic has been raised as a regulatory question without algorithms [50].

NASA's LunaNet specification standardises interoperability among lunar users at the network layer, with Internet protocols and the Bundle Protocol version 7 for delay-tolerant networking; the radio link between two surface vehicles is left to bilateral agreement [39]. Delay-tolerant networking became an operational NASA service in 2026 [49]. The relay programme states its case as coverage: 17–43 % a year at candidate south-pole sites from direct-to-Earth links alone, 80–86 % with one relay [38]. None of these documents says how vehicles of different owners should share work, or what happens to a shared operation while the base cannot see Earth.

What two vehicles of different owners say to each other is the layer these documents leave open. The space-link standards fix what lies below it: packets [55], frames for proximity and long-haul links [58, 59], link security [56], delay-tolerant bundles [60] and, since 2024, Wi-Fi 6 and LTE as surface radios [57]. LunaNet's own messages go from a service provider to a user (almanac, alerts, search and rescue), and its security document was still a placeholder in version 5 [39]. The closest precedent for mixed makers is the factory floor. VDA 5050, at version 3.0.0 since March 2026, defines order, state, connection and factsheet topics between a fleet controller and mobile robots of any vendor, over a broker that is always reachable [64]; the MassRobotics standard reduces the agreed minimum to an identity report and a status report [65]. MAVLink makes the same split for drones, a broadcast stream plus command-and-acknowledge exchanges, with an optional 13 B signature [66]. CADRE avoids the question by having one owner: its rovers synchronise state to the leader and do not negotiate [71]. The protocol of Section 3.12 takes its message set from these precedents and its capability description from VDA 5050's factsheet and from the tool descriptions of the Model Context Protocol [67]. It differs in three conditions: the parties are peers with different owners, the link can be gone for hours, and every message is authenticated on a channel of a few kilobits a second.

3. Model

The model extends the discrete-event simulation of [40, 41, 42]: time jumps from one event, such as a landing, the end of a drive or a lost message being sent again, to the next. Everything that was in those papers and is not described here is unchanged, and with the new features switched off the engine reproduces their results exactly (Section 3.11). Every input carries a flag at first use, as in [41, 42]: Measured (flight, laboratory or survey data), Spec (a published design value or company claim), Modelled (produced by a model, including ours) or Assumed (our choice, varied where it matters). Results produced by this study are never flagged; they come from the result files listed under Availability.

3.1 Base, terrain and demand

The base is the one of [41, 42], on the Connecting Ridge between Shackleton and de Gerlache craters, on NASA Goddard's 5 m terrain tile from the Lunar Orbiter Laser Altimeter (Measured). Its sites are the habitat and control centre (BASE), a cargo depot, a power plant, a construction site, an oxygen pilot plant, a science site and a permanently shadowed crater; landers use two pads 1 km from the base. Driving times, energy and routes come from the driving tables of [41].

Demand follows the landings and bulk flows of [40], scaled by a factor k so that the two cargo-class vehicles are busy a set share of the time: about 10, 30 and 60 % (k = 0.857, 2.806 and 6.220, Assumed; calibrated on five seeds to within one percentage point of the target). We call these the three loads. Landed items come in the five classes of [40] (all Assumed): crew consumables of 60–400 kg, 34 % of items; science payloads of 10–150 kg, 20 %; equipment of 300–2,000 kg, 26 %; infrastructure of 2,000–6,000 kg, 14 %; and base elements of 6,000–15,000 kg, 6 %. Urgent cargo in this paper is the first class, crew consumables, bound for the base or the depot; the others are normal priority, except base elements, which are deferrable. The two cargo-class vehicles are the only ones that can lift most of the cargo, so the share of the whole fleet's time spent working is much lower: a share reported in Section 5.1. Landed cargo belongs to the owner who ordered it, a third each. Bulk regolith moves in 500 kg lots (Assumed), split among owners in the same shares. A landed item becomes a team item with probability 0.10 (Assumed; 0, 0.10 and 0.25 in Section 4.2), drawn from its own random stream, and then weighs between the largest single vehicle's capacity and the largest pair's, 2,000–2,800 kg. Items heavier than any pair can lift go on a separate list, “needs heavy-lift equipment”, and are never offered to the robots; they are reported and excluded from every delay. Each run simulates one year from an empty base, so every regime is in its start-up year and cargo not delivered by day 365 is counted with its wait up to then (Section 4.4).

3.3 The link to Earth

Earth is in view of the base when any part of Earth's disc is above the terrain horizon of a 2.5 m antenna at BASE, the criterion of the published visibility maps [42]. The windows for 2028–2031 are those of [42]; for start years from 2032 we use the same pipeline's windows extended to 2046 (Modelled). A relay, where a regime has one, adds link time as an on–off process with a stated coverage, independent of the direct link, with the gap pattern of [42] (Assumed). Earth's visibility from the base differs by year: in the start years we simulate it ranges from 54.7 % to 62.6 % of the year, and in six of them it is below 60 %, the threshold we fixed for the availability test (Section 4.5).

3.4 The robots' radio network

Two robots can talk directly when their antennas see each other over the terrain and are within range. We computed line of sight for every pair of 2,421 points (the sites, the pads and samples along every route) on the 5 m terrain with the Moon's curvature, the same arithmetic as [41]. Antennas are 2.5 m high on rovers and 1.0 m on small robots and hoppers (Assumed), and a 10 m mast at BASE carries the base computer (Assumed, after NASA Glenn's surface relay concept as in [42]). The range is 5 km and the clearance margin 0 m (both Assumed; 2, 10 km and unlimited range in Section 4.2). A message can travel over several hops through robots or the mast.

Line of sight is rare on this ridge. Two rover antennas 2–5 km apart see each other in 9.31 % of point pairs, and the mast sees rovers directly only within 2 km of the base. Rovers nevertheless form one connected network from 2 km range upward, and every point reaches the mast in at most 4 hops at 5 km range; small robots leave four points cut off. The robots' positions are interpolated along their committed drives, and the network is evaluated whenever a decision is made.

3.5 Messages

Every hop of a message is lost independently with probability p (Bernoulli loss, Assumed) or in bursts (a two-state Gilbert–Elliott channel with mean burst length 10 messages and the same long-run loss, Assumed), and takes 10 ms (Assumed). The on-Moon regimes run at p = 0.10 unless stated; Section 4.2 varies it from 0 to 0.40. A robot whose message was lost is reconsidered 3 minutes later (Assumed), with at most one such retry pending at a time. We count transmissions, messages delivered and lost, and messages per robot-hour.

3.6 Decision regimes

A regime says who decides which vehicle takes which job, where that decision is made, and when it can be made. Table 3 lists them. The owners-alone regimes use the in-house dispatcher of [40]: each owner assigns only its own cargo to its own vehicles, and its own pairs may lift together. When two owners' vehicles want the same apron, the loading area of a site, at the same time, the newcomer waits 2 h of hand coordination (Assumed), and only while Earth is in view. The pooled regimes use the marketplace of [40], which offers every job to every vehicle in auction rounds and forms team lifts from any pair; they differ in where it runs.

A dispatcher on Earth acts only while Earth is in view of the base. Under strict fallback, vehicles finish their current job and wait when Earth sets. Under plan ahead, the dispatcher uploads at loss of signal a queue per vehicle for the items already waiting, and a standing rule for cargo that will land during the blackout: oldest first, nearest free vehicle, one item per trip. Items created on the Moon during a blackout, such as returns, wait for Earth.

On the Moon, the elected leader regime runs the same marketplace on the base computer at the mast, over the robots in its part of the network. The base computer fails at random (mean time between failures 2,000 h, repair 24 h, both Assumed); then, after a 30-minute election (Assumed), the robot that reaches most others leads until the base computer returns. We took the 30 minutes from CADRE's wake cycle [31]; CADRE's design in fact replaces a lost leader within 10 s [71], so the engine's value is slow (Section 3.13). 3.7 The auction among the robots

Each connected part of the network runs the marketplace's own rounds. A part that contains the mast knows every waiting item; any other part knows only the items at sites where one of its robots stands. In a round, each robot computes its best bid and floods it to the robots it can reach, losing it hop by hop with the channel's probability. The bid is that of [40]: the cost of the vehicle's and its operator's hours for the job, plus the shippers' value of the time until delivery, minus an urgency credit of 6 h for an urgent item, 2 h for a normal one and 0.08 h for every hour the item has waited (all Assumed), and the lowest bid wins. The auction is modelled at the level of rounds and not message by message: the true best bid of a connected part always wins its load, whatever is lost. A robot that did not hear the best bid and believes its own is best wins in parallel if its items do not overlap with the winner's; if they do, CBBA's rule settles the conflict when the winner's rebroadcast reaches it within the remaining rounds [4], and otherwise the robot drives to the pad for nothing. So in this model message loss can add a parallel winner, which takes at once a load that a later round might have given to another robot, or a wasted trip, and it can lose a status or assignment message, which is retried three minutes later (Section 3.5). It cannot keep the best bid of a round from winning. The closeness of the auction to the marketplace is to that extent built in, and an implementation in which the best bid itself can go unheard could do worse (Section 7). The number of rounds is at most the larger of the number of items and of robots, times the network's diameter in hops, so that a perfect network never cuts the auction short.

With bundle depth greater than one, a CBBA-like variant, robots also bid for up to depth − 1 later jobs from the end of their commitments. A claim starts when the robot comes free if its items are still waiting, and is dropped otherwise. The items of a claim are withheld from the other robots of the same part of the network, which do not bid for them. With perfect links and depth 1, the auction reproduces the marketplace's event trace exactly (Section 3.11).

3.8 Team lifts across owners

A team item is lifted by two vehicles together. In the pooled regimes, the auction pairs any two vehicles that can join a team lift and together can carry the item; under the owners-alone regimes the pair must have one owner. The pair agrees by a two-phase commit over the radio: the proposer sends a proposal, the partner answers yes, and the proposer sends a commit. A lost proposal or answer means no team in this round. A lost commit sends the proposer alone to the pad, where it waits the timeout, 1 h (Assumed; 0.5 and 2 h in Section 4.2), and gives up; the item returns to the pool and the failed meeting is counted with its waiting time. Coupling at the pad takes 1 h, as in [40]. Some message is always the last one, so no protocol over a lossy channel can guarantee that both robots know the other will come (the Two Generals problem); the commit step trades failures of one kind for another. No message is sent twice inside the handshake, so how often each kind occurs follows from the number of messages (Section 5.7). An exploratory variant lets the base computer arbitrate meetings within its part of the network, repeating its message to each vehicle every three minutes within the timeout.

3.9 Robots that nobody hears

Line of sight on the ridge is so sparse that a robot finishing a delivery at the oxygen plant, the science site, the shadowed crater or pad B can end up where no other robot and no mast hears it. Without a rule it is never reached again. Our lost-link rule (Assumed) sends an idle robot that nobody hears to the nearest site that sees the mast at its antenna height (to BASE for owners alone on the Moon), where it waits to be heard. Section 5.5 reports what switching the rule off costs in the E41 experiment (Section 4.2). We added the rule during development, after a five-seed run in which the elected leader without it delivered 58 t of cargo in a year against 1,487 t with it (Section 4.6). Section 4.2 includes the rule switched off, and a second 10 m mast at pad B, as network-design variants.

3.10 Learned bids

The learned regimes keep the auction unchanged and add a correction to each bid: the robot bids b′ = b − u·g(x), where b is the hand-built bid, u converts hours of urgent wait into the bid's units as in [40], and g is a learned function of thirteen features that a robot knows locally. They are the planned hours of the job, the item's age, priority and slack to its target time (24 h for urgent items, as pre-registered), the load as a share of capacity, the hours to drive to the pad and the battery's state of charge. They also include the hours until the robot's next dark period or until its battery would run out (whichever comes first), whether it is in the mast's part of the network, how many robots it hears, how many claims it holds, the number of items in the bundle, and the hours until the next published landing at the item's pad or the robot's site. The last two time features let a robot anticipate what the hand-built bid ignores. The network never assigns anything, so it cannot create a conflict or an infeasible plan that the auction would not.

We trained two forms of g: a multilayer perceptron with two hidden layers of 32 units (1,537 parameters), and a linear model with 13 weights. Training used seeds 1000 and up and selection used tuning seeds 2000 and up; no training or selection used the evaluation seeds 1–30 (Section 4.3). Those seeds were not used only once: the frozen policies were run on them in a first evaluation, in E40 before the engine fix and in E40 after it (Section 4.6). The first stage was meant to imitate the full-information marketplace by dataset aggregation [24]. We ran its first iteration only: 37,941 rounds of the hand-built auction (624,640 robot-item rows) were labelled with the item the marketplace would give each robot. No correction improved held-out agreement over the hand-built bids, so the aggregation loop, its iterations 1 to 4, was not run, and imitation left both models at zero correction (Section 5.11). The marketplace uses the same bid rule with full information, so imitating it reproduces the hand-built bid. All of the learned policies' behaviour therefore comes from the second stage.

The second stage searched for g on the objective itself with the cross-entropy method: candidates were scored by mean urgent wait at the middle load and message loss 0.10 on four training seeds, with common random numbers across candidates, and each generation's best was scored on three tuning seeds; the generation with the best tuning score was kept. The linear model searched its 13 weights over 6 generations of 10 candidates; the network searched only its 32 output weights over 5 generations of 12, with its hidden layers left at their initial values because imitation never moved them. The linear search kept its last generation, so it may not have converged when it stopped. Every candidate's score is recorded in the policy files. We decided before the main runs to carry both models into every experiment (Section 4.6).

3.11 Checks

Each regime was checked against cases whose answer is known. The pooled dispatcher on Earth with coverage 1.0 reproduces the full-information marketplace's event trace and summary exactly; so does the elected leader with perfect radio and no base failures; so does the auction with perfect links and depth 1; and owners alone with full coverage, no hand-coordination time and no own-pair lifts reproduce the in-house dispatcher of [40]. With every new feature off, 20 result rows of [41, 42] rerun with no difference in any numeric column, and the test suite passed (547 tests at that point; the suite holds 658 tests as collected on 11 November 2026, which were not rerun in full for this revision). 3.12 What the robots say to each other: the interaction protocol

Sections 3.5 to 3.8 treat a message as an event that arrives or is lost. For a vehicle built by another company to take part, the message itself has to be fixed: its fields, its units, its size, who may send it, and what a receiver does with a version it does not know. We wrote this down as the Lunar Rover Interaction Protocol, LRIP, now at version 0.2: a schema file, a codec that follows it and a set of test packets (Data and code availability). It is a design of ours and not a standard. What exists is the encoding and the checks a receiver makes on one packet: the schema, a bit-packed and a CBOR codec with the authentication code and the signature, a receiver that applies the rules given below, the manifest and its matching rule, and the test packets. What does not exist is any exchange of these messages between simulated vehicles or over a radio link, the request and response timeouts, the timing of the heartbeat, the change from one key epoch to the next, and the first steps of the lost-link ladder. The results of Sections 5.1 to 5.7 do not depend on it, because the engine counts messages and LRIP gives them bytes. Five rules shaped it. Every layer below the application is taken from an existing standard. There is one schema with two encodings, bit-packed for a narrow-band channel and CBOR [61] for a broadband one. Every message is authenticated, and a message that gives a right is signed. Every position is tied to a declared frame, datum and time scale. No message can command another owner's vehicle; a vehicle can only inform, ask and agree. What a vehicle does without a link is fixed in advance (Section 3.13).

Table A1 in Appendix A sets the layers beside the standards they reuse. This paper describes version 0.2. Its position report carries east, north and height in the site grid, heading, speed, a one-sigma horizontal value, the navigation source, a moving flag and, in the six bits that version 0.1 left spare, a code for the 95 % horizontal uncertainty on a logarithmic scale; it is still 13 B of content and a packet of 41 B. The frame, the datum and the time scale of a vehicle's positions are not repeated in every report. The vehicle declares them in its heartbeat, HELLO, which grows from 30 B to 32 B and is sent at least every 10 s, and in its manifest, which grows by 9 B. A receiver refuses a position report from a sender that has no authenticated declaration under its current key epoch. Packets of version 0.1 still encode and decode bit for bit. A covariance is not carried: the report states its uncertainty as the one-sigma value and the 95 % code only. A packet in the narrow-band profile is a Space Packet [55] whose application identifier is the message type. All sizes in this paper are of that packet, before the link frame and channel coding that carry it; a Proximity-1 frame adds 12 B to each (Section 5.8). It has a packet header of 6 B, an LRIP header of 8 B (schema version, message type, sender, and the time the content is valid for, in tenths of a second), the content, a security header of 6 B (which key, and a counter that only rises) and an authentication code of 8 B: AES-CMAC [63] cut to the shortest length that the space-link security standard allows [56]. That is 28 B of overhead, or 84 B when an Ed25519 signature [62] of 64 B replaces the code. The security fields copy the layout of the space-link security protocol but sit in the message and not in the frame, so they are not that protocol [56]. The counter and the code let a receiver reject forged and replayed messages from outside the site. In our implementation the decoder applies these checks on receipt. It refuses a packet that carries no authentication code, a message that the schema marks as signed and that carries no signature (TASK_AWARD, RESERVE_GRANT and CAP_MANIFEST), a counter that is not above the highest it has accepted from that sender, a validity time more than 10 s from its own clock when it is given one, and a key epoch other than its own; an older heartbeat does not replace a newer declaration, one with invalid codes revokes it, and short or malformed input is refused with one kind of error. These checks were added after a code review on 13 November 2026, and no packet size or test packet changed with them; before, the decoder verified a code or signature where one was present and applied the declaration rule to position reports, and the counter and freshness rule had been applied only in the simulation model of the companion paper [45]. They are exercised on packets that the codec itself builds, each time this paper is built; the codec has not been run over a radio link. They do not stop a vehicle that holds the site key from lying about itself; detecting that is a subject of the companion papers [44, 45].

Of the 15 messages, nine are published (POSITION, STATUS, INTENT, ALERT, TASK_ANNOUNCE, TASK_STATE, RESERVE_RELEASE, MAP_NOTICE and HELLO), three are requests (TASK_BID, RESERVE_REQ and CAP_QUERY) and three are the responses to them (TASK_AWARD, RESERVE_GRANT and CAP_MANIFEST). Table A2 lists them with their sizes. The split follows one rule. State that is refreshed anyway (where a vehicle is, how it is, what it intends, that it is alive) is published to whoever listens, without acknowledgement, and the newest value replaces the old. Anything that changes who may be where or who does what is a request with a response and a timeout: a bid answered by an award, a request for a stretch of road answered by a grant, a capability query answered by a manifest. The three responses are signed. An award and a grant give a right, to a job or to a stretch of road, that a third vehicle must be able to verify, and as packets they are 92 B and 94 B; the manifest is what every later choice of bidders rests on. An alert that orders all vehicles to stop or closes a landing zone is signed by the base (98 B). The task messages follow the announce, bid, award and report sequence known as the contract-net protocol; we could not open its FIPA specification and do not cite it as a source. The message set is a superset of the two reports of the MassRobotics standard [65], and its names map onto the topics of VDA 5050: the position report is its visualization, the status report its state, a task its order, the manifest its factsheet, the heartbeat its connection [64].

The protocol fixes the bytes between vehicles and leaves each owner's on-board software alone, a choice that follows from the radio. The middleware under ROS 2 (DDS) spends 20 B on its wire header before any content, and by default every participant announces itself to every other every 30 s [68], which a shared channel of a few kilobits a second cannot carry. MQTT-SN was built for constrained links, with a header of 2 or 4 B and a gateway that holds messages for sleeping clients [70], and Zenoh has been measured against MQTT, Kafka and DDS by its own developers and a university group [69]; either could carry the broadband profile. Neither survives an outage of hours. The Bundle Protocol does [60], at about 48 B of overhead per bundle by our count of its blocks, so it carries what must arrive (awards, grants, alerts) and not the once-a-second report.

A vehicle describes itself in a capability manifest, a small document signed by its owner that any other party can fetch: identity, size and limits, energy, radios, navigation accuracy, the message versions it speaks, what it does when it loses the link, and a list of named, versioned capabilities with typed parameters. The idea is that of a tool description in the Model Context Protocol, where a server lists each tool with a name and a schema for its inputs and announces when the list changes [67], and of the factsheet of VDA 5050 [64]. On the wire every key and every listed string is a small integer from a published registry, decimals are fixed-point integers and the whole is CBOR [61]. As minified JSON the two example manifests are 1,235 and 1,245 B; in the compact form they are 292 B (small rover) and 289 B (hopper), and the small rover's is a signed packet of 376 B. The design note had estimated 247 and 251 B. The measured sizes are larger mainly because the implementation keeps the owner and model names as text, so that a manifest from an owner the reader has never met still decodes: with the two names replaced by registry numbers the same encoder gives 266 and 268 B. The status report carries a 16-bit checksum of the current manifest, so a change prompts a new query.

A task names a capability, a version and parameter values. A vehicle can do it if its manifest lists that capability with the same major version and a minor version at least the task's, if every numeric parameter of the task lies within the manifest's minimum and maximum, and if every list parameter is a subset of the manifest's list. A parameter that the manifest does not mention counts as a refusal; capabilities and parameters that the reader does not know are ignored. For the small rover above (transport.carry version 1.0, at most 200 kg, interface tray-A), the rule accepts a carrying task of 150 kg and refuses one of 250 kg, one that asks for version 1.1 or 2.0, and one that needs an interface the manifest does not list. The hopper's manifest lists transport.hop and no transport.carry: it is refused for every carrying task and accepted for a hop with 5 kg. A vehicle with no manifest is refused. In the auction the rule is an optional filter: a robot whose manifest cannot do a load does not bid for it. With manifests generated from the vehicle classes, switching the filter on changed none of the 128 summary values of a 45-day run of the auction (one run, seed 3, message loss 0.10), because those manifests state exactly the capacity the engine already checks; with no manifests at all, deliveries fell below 10 % of that run's. This is not the check that the companion papers quote [44, 45], in which every engine option added for the protocol is switched off and the run is compared with a stored run of the code as it was before those options (commit 0210c22): none of 144 summary values differs, in one scenario over 30 days on seed 2, and with one option switched on 14 of them differ, so the comparison can fail. That check was rebuilt after a code review on 13 November 2026; before, it compared a run with itself. The filter therefore changes nothing in this paper's experiments, which run without it. It is meant for a vehicle whose class the dispatcher has never seen; that case was not run.

Versions follow five rules, each covered by a test in the repository. Every message carries the schema's major and minor version. A minor version may only append fields, use spare bits, or add enumeration values and message types, so a receiver decodes the fields it knows and skips the rest, since the packet length says how much; an unknown message type is counted and dropped. An unknown enumeration value is read as the most cautious one known: an unknown alert type as a hazard, an unknown mode as a fault. A different major version is refused. A field added in a minor version is absent from packets of earlier ones, so a packet of version 0.1 decodes exactly as that version defined it; the frame, datum and time-scale codes of version 0.2 are the exception to the cautious reading, since an unknown code there is refused. VDA 5050 rejects an order that carries a field the robot cannot use [64]; LRIP skips the field, because a rover out of contact cannot ask again.

Two implementations interoperate if they agree on bytes, and the test for that is a file. The release holds 45 golden packets: every message in the narrow-band profile with authentication (12 with the 8 B code, 3 signed), the same 15 without security, and 15 in the broadband profile, each as hexadecimal bytes with the field values it must decode to, and the two example manifests in compact form. The keys in the file are test keys. The broadband vectors carry the 8 B code; the 16 B code that Table A1 gives for broadband has no vector yet. A second implementation conforms when it decodes every packet to the recorded values and encodes the recorded values to the recorded bytes. Our codec decodes all 45, which the script that builds this paper checks on every build. No second, independent implementation exists yet, so interoperability between implementations is designed for and not tested.

3.13 When a robot fails or loses the link

Two rules say what happens when a robot stops answering. They are design rules of the protocol; only the last step of the second is in the engine's main runs. First, every vehicle sends a heartbeat (the HELLO message, or any other) at a fixed period, and a neighbour that misses k in a row marks it silent; the base, or the group if the base is out of reach, marks it failed after a second, longer timeout or a sighting. A failed vehicle's job goes back to auction with its epoch number, a counter of how often the job has been awarded, raised by one. If the old holder returns it drops the job, unless it already carries the cargo, in which case it says so and the new award is cancelled; the epoch keeps two robots from holding one job once their messages meet. A silent vehicle's road reservation is not released, whether it failed or only lost the link. After the reservation's timeout and a grace period the base marks the segment unconfirmed. Another vehicle may then enter it only at caution speed, the speed from which it can stop within its own perception range [45], and reports what it finds; the segment is confirmed clear when the holder is heard or seen. Safety therefore never rests on the failure detector being right. An earlier version of this section released the reservation at its timeout, which is unsafe if the vehicle is disabled inside the segment, and that rule is withdrawn. Proposition 4 (Appendix B) gives the expected extra wait of the job. A rover that fails for good is not simulated in this paper; the engine's failures are those of the base computer (Section 3.6).

Second, a vehicle that loses the link without failing follows a fixed ladder. It finishes the stretch of road it has reserved and never stops inside a single-lane segment, stops at the next safe point and holds there for 600 s, then retraces its path at half speed to the last point where it had a link, and after 1 h there drives to a meeting point in view of the mast. Its reserved stretch is treated as above, unconfirmed and entered only at caution speed, and its awarded job returns to the pool when its lease ends (the lease is the time for which an award holds without being renewed). The example manifest of Section 3.12 carries these times for its vehicle: 30 s without the base before it treats itself as cut off, 600 s held before it returns, 3,600 s at the last link point before it leaves for the meeting point. The rally rule of Section 3.9 is the last step of this ladder and the only one inside the engine; the return to the last link point is tested at the level of messages in E54 (Section 4.8).

These decisions sit on the Moon for three reasons. The first two rest on published measurements and on this base's Earth windows; the third is a model with assumed inputs. The first is reaction time. In NASA's simulator study of driving the Lunar Terrain Vehicle from Earth, the mean speed fell from 3.24 km/h with no delay to 2.56 km/h at 4 s of round-trip delay, 2.03 km/h at 6 s and 1.76 km/h at 8 s, with workload rising [75]. VIPER's plan is waypoint driving with a round trip of 6 to 10 s, 0.8 cm/s made good and about four people on console for one rover [76]. Stopping and giving way cannot wait that long. The second is the blackout. A decision that only Earth may take, needed at a random moment, waits 54 h on average at this base, against about 1.9 h for a hand-over decided on the Moon when the job waits for its lease to end, under the assumptions of Proposition 4. The third is people: Proposition 5 relates the operators a fleet needs to its level of autonomy, with request rates that we chose to match published supervision ratios, so it shows consistency with those ratios and predicts nothing. Dispatch, right of way and the re-auction of a failed robot's job therefore belong on the Moon. Recovering a broken vehicle, and anything else that takes judgment, can wait for people on Earth while Earth is in view, with a safe default when it is not: hold at a safe point.

CADRE is the one design of this kind built for flight, and it differs from ours in ways that matter here [71, 72]. It has one owner, so no private costs and no question of whose job comes first; we simulate three owners, and two and five outside the training range. Its three rovers are identical; ours are eight vehicles in six classes. It plans on an elected leader with a greedy priority scheduler and has no auction; we compare a leader with an auction and with dispatch from Earth. It assumes a slow two-way channel between every rover and the leader at all times, and has no mode for a partition that lasts hours; our robots lose messages, split into groups and drive to a rally point when unheard. Its site sees Earth through the lander for its one lunar day; ours loses Earth for about eleven days at a time. Its leader is replaced within 10 s, where our engine takes 0.5 h, a value we set before reading [71]. With a base-computer failure every 2,000 h that is 4.3 elections and 2.2 h of election a year, so we expect the value to matter little, but we did not rerun the leader regime with 10 s. CADRE's evidence is flight hardware and flight software under test; ours is simulation.

4. Experiment design

4.1 Questions and hypotheses

We pre-registered the hypotheses [48] in a dated file in the repository before the main runs [43]. The confirmatory family, tested at the middle load with Holm's correction, is:

  • H1, sharing. Pooled regimes with full Earth coverage cut the mean urgent wait relative to owners alone with full coverage, at 30 and 60 % load.
  • H2, availability. A pooled dispatcher on Earth with strict fallback keeps urgent cargo waiting longer than the full-information marketplace, by more than 10 % of the marketplace's mean (the lower end of the 95 % interval must clear that margin), in each start year whose base visibility is below 60 % and pooled over years. Secondary: planning ahead recovers part; relay 0.8 comes within 10 % of the marketplace by an equivalence test.
  • H3a, decentralisation. At message loss up to 0.10, the robots' auction is within ±10 % of the full-information marketplace (two one-sided tests [46, 47]). Exploratory: deeper bundles do worse at loss 0.25–0.40 (H3b); the elected leader matches the auction unless the network splits (H3c).
  • H4, learning. At 30–60 % load, learned bids beat hand-built bids on the held-out seeds; at 10 % load the difference is within ±2 %. The registered learned regime is the network. The registration says that the linear model on the same features is “also reported”, with every test outside the training range, whichever way they come out.
  • H6, team lifts. Allowing cross-owner pairs raises the share of items delivered (no team share is named); at loss 0.25 and above, failed meetings rise without the commit-and-timeout step.

H5 (payments) and H7–H10 (safety, Mars, sizing) belong to the companion papers [44, 45]. Two further hypotheses, H13 on the protocol's load and H14 on the lost link, come from a second pre-registration written for the protocol experiments [54] and are stated in Section 4.8.

4.3 Seeds, start years and common random numbers

A seed fixes the landings, the manifests, the item masses, the team draws, the base computer's failures and the message losses, so all regimes face the same year when they share a seed. Evaluation uses two blocks. Block one is seeds 1–30 with start years 2028 to 2031 in rotation (start year 2028 plus the seed modulo 4), on the link windows of [42]. Block two is start years 2032, 2035, 2038, 2041 and 2044, each with seeds 1–10, on the extended windows. E40 uses both blocks, 80 runs per cell; E41 and E42 use block one. Training used seeds 1000 and up and tuning seeds 2000 and up; no evaluation seed was used for either.

4.4 Outcomes

The primary outcome is the mean waiting time of urgent cargo in a run, in hours, from the moment the item is ready to its delivery. The registration names it “mean waiting time of urgent cargo” without naming the end point [43]; the engine has always measured it to delivery, and an earlier version of this paper wrote “to its pickup”, which was an error of the text and not a change of the measure. Items not delivered by the end of the year count with their wait so far (censored at the horizon), so an undelivered item adds the time from its arrival to day 365 and the hours of any comparison with owners alone scale with the one-year horizon; cargo on the heavy-lift list is excluded. A run holds few urgent items: on average 15 at 10 % load (from 0 to 31 over all E40 runs), 52 at 30 % load (from 21 to 102 over all E40 runs) and 122 at 60 % load (from 61 to 184 over all E40 runs), counted in the full-information marketplace's block-one runs; one run at light load had none and has no value of the outcome. The light-load results therefore rest on means of about 15 items a run. The secondary outcomes are the 95th percentile of urgent wait, the share of urgent items delivered within 24 h, tonnes delivered and failed team-lift meetings; the message and driving measures are reported in [44]. Because censored means mix waiting with not moving at all, we also report the mean over delivered items only, labelled as such.

4.5 Statistics

Every comparison is paired by seed and start year. The pairing holds for demand, landings and initial faults: in the engine's phase-5 checks (D10, Section 3.11) every regime's E40 run had the same demand as the full-information marketplace's on the same seed and start year across 880 regime pairs; the per-job intervention and fault redraws are shared in draw order and diverge once the allocations differ, in every regime. How much the pairing buys was measured at 10 % load, on the mean delay per delivery and not on the primary outcome, over the 80 rows of each cell, which include the repeated runs of the regimes that never wait for Earth. Against the marketplace, pairing cut the variance of the paired difference to 0.00–0.52 of the unpaired sum for B_EARTH plan_ahead, B_EARTH_RELAY 0.8, B_EARTH_RELAY 1.0, C_AUCTION, C_LEADER, C_LEARNED_LIN, C_LEARNED_MLP, and only to 0.90–0.99 for A_EARTH plan_ahead, A_EARTH strict, A_MOON, B_EARTH strict, whose delay varies mostly between start years; their paired intervals are little narrower than unpaired ones would be. A bootstrap over pairs treats the runs of one start year as independent, so for H2 we also give the interval obtained by resampling whole start years (Section 5.3). We report the mean paired difference with a percentile bootstrap 95 % interval from 20,000 resamples of the pairs [53]. The registered confirmatory family is {H1, H2, H3a, H4, H6}. The family we correct with Holm's procedure [52] at the middle load is {H1, H2, H4 (linear), H4 (network), H6} (Table 7): H3a is an equivalence claim and is tested outside it (Section 5.4), and H4 has two rows, of which the linear one was added after its result on the evaluation seeds had been seen (Section 4.6); all other intervals are per comparison and unadjusted. A claim of no practical difference needs two one-sided tests at a margin of ±10 % of the full-information marketplace's mean (±2 % for H4 at light load), using the 90 % interval [46, 47]. For H2 the comparison is made in each start year below 60 % visibility and pooled; the block of start years is reported. The rule for adding seeds (blocks of 10 up to 60 if the half-width exceeded 10 % of the mean) was fixed in advance. The rule was not applied: no seeds were added in any cell, and every cell holds the 30 seeds of the design (and the 50 runs of the second block where it applies). Read on each cell's mean of the primary outcome, the 95 % half-width exceeded 10 % of the mean in 29 of the 40 E40 cells: 12 of 12 at 10 % load, 10 of 15 at 30 % load and 7 of 13 at 60 % load (three cells lie within half a percentage point of the threshold, so the count depends that much on the resampling). Read on the paired differences, against 10 % of the reference regime's mean, it was exceeded for H1, H2, H3a and H4 (linear) (for H3a a 90 % half-width of 1.64 h against 1.49 h). By the rule ten more seeds at a time should have been run in those cells. We did not extend any sample after seeing results, so every interval in this paper is from the fixed design of 30 seeds, or of 80 runs where a regime depends on Earth (Section 7).

4.6 Choices made after looking at results

Five changes were made to the registered plan [43]. First, the author regrouped the planned papers; the hypotheses of this paper were unchanged. Second, after a five-seed development run showed that the gap between owners alone and the full-information marketplace was almost all cargo that owners alone cannot move, we replaced H2's yardstick (“half that gap”) with the comparison inside the pooled fleet given above; the original yardstick is still reported. Third, after the learned models had been evaluated once on seeds 1–30, and the network had shown no gain and the linear model a gain of 16.8 % there [43], we decided to run both in every experiment instead of choosing one, and froze both. The confirmatory test of the linear model was added to the Holm family at that point, with its result on those seeds already known; the registration requires any headline about learned bids to say that the linear model was the secondary model, and this paper's do. The lost-link rule (Section 3.9), the hopper refuelling variant, the decision to let owners alone pair their own robots, and the owners-alone regime with full coverage (which H1 names but the first cell list lacked) were set during development, before any main run of the cells they affect. A fourth change was made after the main runs and before the final analysis, when two defects were found in the runs. Regimes that never wait for Earth give the same run for a seed whatever the start year, so their second block (five start years, seeds 1–10) repeated runs; every comparison between two such regimes therefore uses the first block only, as the registered design states, and an interim statement that the auction beat the marketplace, which counted the repeats as independent runs, is withdrawn. The auction had ordered robots by a per-process hash, so its runs under message loss were valid draws but not bit-reproducible; the order was fixed and the affected runs were repeated, with the old rows archived and compared with the new ones. Both are dated in the pre-registration file [43]. The fifth change was made after the first full analysis and the second draft of this paper, when a defect in the engine's event handling was found while timing the largest cells of a later experiment: The dispatcher's consolidation hold re-posted a wake-up for every held lane on every call, so the event queue grew with fleet size and, because one dispatch call does not reach a fixed point, the duplicates also changed assignments. The fix, pre-registered as change 5 before any rerun row, posts one wake-up per lane and lot and repeats dispatch at the same instant until nothing changes. Every block of E40, E41 and E42 was rerun with it; the 4,052 earlier rows are archived and reproducible with the fix off, and every number from E40, E41 and E42 in this paper is from the rerun rows. Against the archived row of the same cell, seed and start year, the primary outcome moved by −0.27 h on average and 2.4 h in absolute value in E40 (2,749 rows with a value; at most 153 h in one run, the plan-ahead dispatcher on Earth at 60 % load), by +0.21 h and 6.9 h in E42 (1,170 rows; at most 256 h) and by −0.10 h and 1.6 h in the 132 rows of E41 that existed before the fix (at most 18 h). An earlier version of this section quoted smaller figures; they were for the mean delay per delivery of all cargo and not for the primary outcome. Table C1 in Appendix C gives every tested comparison before and after the fix, as change 5 promised. No verdict changed. The paired differences moved from −0.38 (−1.99 to 1.16) to −0.84 (−2.91 to 0.95) h for H3a, from −2.49 (−4.13 to −1.01) to −2.32 (−3.94 to −0.64) h for H4's linear model, from +91.3 (83.3 to 100.0) to +89.9 (81.8 to 98.7) h for H2 and from +4.2 (3.4 to 5.1) to +5.4 (3.5 to 8.5) percentage points for H6.

Four further departures from the registered analysis were made after results had been seen, and none changes a verdict. The first Holm family leaves out H3a and splits H4, as Section 4.5 states. The second pre-registration's family is corrected over three of its five hypotheses, those with a sampling test (Section 4.8). The sign test used for H14 in that family was added after its results, and its p is one-sided where every other p in this paper is two-sided (Section 5.9). H6 is tested at team share 0.25, a cell the registration does not name, and on a share of mass (Section 5.7). The evaluation of the learned bids made before the main runs, which predated both engine fixes, was rerun in full on the corrected engine (Section 5.6).

4.7 Compute

Runs were made on one laptop with 8 GB of memory, one process at a time per experiment, in chunks of at most 540 s that resume from the last finished run. A simulated year took 0.3 s to 71 s depending on the regime and load, with at most 360 MB of memory. The learned models' training took 1.8 h in all.

4.8 The protocol experiments (E53 and E54)

Two experiments outside the engine bear on the protocol. They were pre-registered separately on 11 November 2026, before either had a result [54]. That file numbers its hypotheses H9 to H16; H13 and H14 belong to this paper. Its H9 and H10 are not the H9 and H10 of the first pre-registration [43], and we write them H9′ and H10′, as the companion papers do.

  • H13, protocol load. At one position report a second, 20 rovers and authentication on, the busiest link carries no more than the 16 kbit/s assumed for the radio in the companion paper [44]; at ten reports a second it carries more. We report it as a design calculation and not as a test (Section 5.8).
  • H14, lost link. At message loss 0.25 and with a 14-day Earth blackout, store-and-forward with the lost-link rule loses fewer jobs than drop-and-retry, by a paired interval that excludes zero, and leaves no reservation stuck past its timeout. The second clause is not testable as written (Section 5.9).

Both sit in that file's confirmatory family with H9′, H10′ and H15a, which are reported in the companion paper [45]. The registration asks for Holm's procedure [52] across all five. It is applied at α = 0.05 to the three that have a sampling test, H10′, H14 and H15a; H9′ and H13 are comparisons with a threshold and are listed without a test, which is a change from the registered family. For the correction H14 is tested by an exact sign test on the lost jobs per seed. That test was added after the results were seen; the registered rule, a paired interval that excludes zero, gives the same verdict [54].

E53 counts bytes. Each rover sends a position report at 0.1, 1 or 10 Hz, status and intent reports at 0.1 Hz, a heartbeat whenever it would otherwise send less than one message a second, and the event messages (reservation requests, grants and releases; bids, awards and task reports; landing alerts) at the rates per robot-hour measured in the companion paper's instrumented runs [44]. Sizes are the codec's, either with the 8 B code and signed awards and grants, or with no security at all. For the mesh, 5, 10, 20, 40 and 80 rovers are placed at positions drawn from the fleet's own trajectories of July 2028, 30 draws for each fleet size, with 2.5 m antennas, the 10 m mast and line of sight within 5 km as in Section 3.4. Reports travel one hop and are not relayed. Events travel to the base along shortest paths, and every hop is another transmission. The load of the busiest link is the largest sum of transmissions that any node, the mast included, can hear. It is compared with the 16 kbit/s narrow-band channel and a 1,000 kbit/s broadband one, as raw load and as airtime on a scheduled channel (efficiency 0.8, Assumed) and on slotted random access (efficiency 0.37; Proposition 2). A second geometry, added after the pre-registration and not used for H13, places the rovers uniformly over all route samples. Nothing is transmitted: there is no radio model beyond line of sight, no interference and no channel-access protocol.

E54 replays one month's messages. The month is July 2028 as the engine recorded it for the auction at the middle load, with the fleet of eight vehicles and the road rules of [45], on 30 seeds (199 jobs and 88 single-lane road reservations a seed). Three ways of carrying the messages are compared at per-hop loss 0.10, 0.25 and 0.40. Drop and retry sends end to end along the current shortest path, again every 180 s, at most 5 times and never past the message's lifetime. Store and forward moves a message hop by hop with custody [60]: a lost transmission is repeated after 10 s while the link lasts, a message with no path waits in a 64 MB store, and it expires at its lifetime. Both sets of retry settings are Assumed and were not swept: the baseline's five tries end 12 minutes into a lifetime of 60 minutes, while store and forward repeats for all of it. The third adds the lost-link return of Section 3.13: a vehicle that has had no path to the base for 600 s retraces its path at half speed to the last point where it had one. The replay follows the recorded drive, in which the vehicle did not stop at the next safe point, so the stretch it retraces is the one driven in those 600 s, an upper reading of the rule. The messages are generated from the recorded run: an award to each vehicle of a job (200.9 awards a seed, the 199.3 jobs and one more for each of the 1.63 team jobs of two vehicles; its lifetime is the lease of 1 h, the time after which an awarded job returns to the pool; an award that expires undelivered is a lost job, auctioned again), a reservation grant with every award (lifetime 60 s; 6,028 grants against the 2,628 single-lane reservations of the recorded runs, so more than half of them belong to jobs with no such reservation), a report at the end of each job, and a status report every 10 s for the base's age of information. A 14-day Earth blackout is imposed on days 8 to 22, as H14 names it. It can affect only messages addressed to Earth, which here are the daily records of who did what (the ledger); awards, grants, reports and status travel between the base and the vehicles and do not depend on it. A link exists in a 60 s step when two nodes are in line of sight within 5 km at their recorded positions. E54 is not a run of the engine. Inside the engine a lost message is retried three minutes later and the dispatcher knows at once that it failed, so a job cannot be lost there; the replay asks what would have happened to the same month's messages under each transport. Three limits follow. The return is applied by editing the contact plan while the recorded job timeline stays: for the radio a returning vehicle stands at its last link point, and for the logistics it is driving its job at the same time. The replay therefore counts what the return gains in contact and not what it costs in interrupted jobs. That cost is not small: the rule produced 87.3 returns a seed against 199.3 jobs, and in the recorded month a vehicle had a path to the base for only 57 % of its driving time, so the ladder of Section 3.13 read literally would interrupt a large share of drives. The engine's later dispatch does not see the detour either. A returning vehicle is given a direct link to the base, which is optimistic when its last link was through another vehicle. A reservation is counted as unconfirmed when its holder has no path to the base 60 s after the timeout: the base cannot confirm that the segment is clear. It does not mean that a vehicle is still in the segment; in the replay every vehicle leaves on schedule. The Earth-bound ledger, one bundle of 10 kB a day (Assumed), is replayed separately over 61 days with lifetimes of 14, 25.8 and 30 days.

5. Results

5.1 What each regime delivers

At every load the regimes fall into three groups (Table 5). Owners working alone keep urgent cargo waiting longest, whether they are coordinated from Earth or on the Moon: 1,217 to 1,257 h on average at the middle load. Pooled regimes that lose contact with Earth part of the time come next, from 17.6 h with an 80 % relay to 104.9 h with strict fallback. Tonnes delivered are almost the same within the pooled group (1,454 to 1,471 t) and much lower for owners alone (943 to 956 t).

The fleet is lightly used even at the middle load. 5.2 Sharing: capacity and dispatch (H1)

Pooling cut the mean urgent wait by 1,232 h at the middle load (95 % CI 1,076 to 1,401 h, paired, n = 30), comparing the pooled dispatcher and owners alone, both with Earth always in view. H1 is supported (Holm-adjusted p < 0.001). At 60 % load the same comparison cut the wait by 1,077 h (95 % CI 984 to 1,175 h, n = 30, p < 0.001), so H1's second half, that pooling helps at 60 % load too, is supported (Table 7; the 60 % rows lie outside the Holm family of Section 4.5).

The size of that difference follows from the scenario and from the one-year horizon, and it should not be read as hours that pooling saves on each item. Owner C has a third of the cargo and no cargo rover: its small rovers and hoppers cannot lift its crates or its regolith lots, and alone it delivered 1.6 t in a year against 491 t in the pooled fleet. Without cross-owner pairs no owner can lift most team items. By the end of a year owners alone had left 513 t waiting. An urgent item that was not delivered counts with the time from its arrival to day 365, about half a year on average, so the 1,232 h would roughly double in a two-year run. The ownership split was not varied in E40 to E42. Two comparisons on delivered items only say what pooling changes for cargo that does move; both condition on delivery and so compare different sets of items. In the H1 pair itself, owners alone with Earth always in view waited 5.8 h longer than the pooled dispatcher that is always in contact (3.7 to 7.6 h, n = 30). In a second comparison, which adds the loss of Earth, owners alone with strict fallback waited 100 h longer than the full-information marketplace (91 to 109 h, n = 80), where the censored difference of the same pair is 1,242 h. A delivered-only difference and a censored one are means over different items and do not add, so neither pair yields a share of the gap. Split as designed in Section 4.2, single-robot jobs pooled across owners, together with the pooled dispatcher, account for 844 h of the censored difference (68 %; 95 % CI 732 to 955 h) and lifts by pairs of different owners' rovers for 389 h (32 %; 301 to 483 h). The pooled side of the first part is the robots' auction, whose difference from the full-information marketplace is given in Section 5.4. By owner, pooling cut the mean urgent wait by 404 h for owner A, 631 h for owner B and 2,575 h for owner C, the owner without a cargo rover.

5.3 A dispatcher on Earth (H2)

A pooled dispatcher on Earth that stops when Earth sets kept urgent cargo waiting 89.9 h longer than the full-information marketplace, pooled over all start years (95 % CI 81.8 to 98.7 h, n = 80): 104.9 h against 15.0 h, both means over the 80 matched rows, in which the marketplace's seeds 1 to 10 appear six times (Table 5 shows its 30 distinct runs). That interval resamples the 80 pairs as if they were independent, although most of the variance lies between the nine start years; resampling whole start years gives 77.2 to 102.3 h, and the per-year tests rest on 7 to 10 runs each. The margin, as revised in change 2 after a five-seed development run of the same cells and before the main runs, was 10 % of the marketplace's mean, 1.5 h; the lower end of the interval clears it by a wide distance in each of the six start years whose base visibility is below 60 % (2029, 2031, 2032, 2038, 2041 and 2044), where the year means of the difference range from 74 h to 113 h (Figure 1). H2 as revised is supported on either interval (Holm-adjusted p < 0.001).

The backlog at the end of a blackout averages 47.8 t under strict fallback against 2.3 t for the marketplace, and the share of urgent items delivered within a day falls from 81 % to 39 % (again over the 80 matched rows). Planning ahead recovers most of it: uploading per-vehicle queues and a standing rule for blackout landings leaves 7.3 h of extra wait (95 % CI 5.2 to 9.4 h). A relay that is up 80 % of the time leaves 2.6 h (95 % CI 1.6 to 3.6 h; 90 % interval, used for the equivalence test, 1.7 to 3.4 h). That is outside the 10 % margin we set for it, so the secondary expectation that an 80 % relay comes within 10 % of the marketplace fails.

The original yardstick of H2, half the gap between owners alone and the marketplace, would have been 621 h, larger than anything Earth's availability could cost a pooled fleet. Against it H2 would not have been met: the difference is 89.9 h. We replaced it before the main runs because it measures fleet capacity (Section 4.6).

5.4 Deciding on the Moon (H3a)

The robots' own auction, at message loss 0.10 over the terrain-limited network, kept urgent cargo waiting 0.84 h less than the full-information marketplace (−5.6 %; 90 % CI −2.6 to 0.7 h, n = 30). We had registered the expectation that it would come within ±10 % of the marketplace, ±1.5 h. The 90 % interval is not inside that margin, so equivalence is not shown and H3a is not supported as stated; the interval includes zero, so the auction is shown neither better nor worse than the marketplace. Because the auction, the leader and the marketplace never wait for Earth, a seed gives the same run in every start year; this comparison therefore uses the 30 runs of block one only (change 4 of the pre-registration [43]). An earlier interim reading that counted the repeated runs as independent suggested the auction beat the marketplace; on independent runs that difference is not distinguishable from zero.

The comparison is confounded by the lost-link rule. The on-Moon regimes send an idle robot that nobody hears back to a site the mast sees, 809 times a year in the auction's runs, and the marketplace has no such move. Where the radio reaches every robot the returns fall to 11 a year and the auction's wait is 15.2 h, 0.3 h above the marketplace's and 1.2 h (−0.2 to 2.7 h) above its own default cell (Section 5.5). The auction's small advantage in the point estimate is therefore more likely an effect of that repositioning than of where the decision is made. The marketplace was not run with the same move, so the two cannot be separated here.

Two direct comparisons were not registered and are reported because a reader will look for them. The relay that is always up gives the same run as the full-information marketplace for every seed, so the auction's difference from it is the one above. Against the relay that is up 80 % of the time, on the 30 seeds of block one, the auction's urgent wait was 3.8 h shorter (95 % CI 2.0 to 5.7 h), and against the plan-ahead dispatcher on Earth 8.2 h shorter (5.0 to 11.7 h).

The elected leader, which runs the marketplace itself on the base computer and loses messages on the way, came within 0.26 h of it (−1.8 to 1.2 h).

5.5 Message loss, bundle depth and radio range (H3b, H3c)

With no message loss the four on-Moon regimes kept urgent cargo waiting 14.6 h (the elected leader), 14.3 h (the auction), 13.8 h (the learned network bids) and 10.8 h (the learned linear bids), against 14.9 h for the full-information marketplace on the same seeds; the learned linear bids lay below it by 4.2 h (2.7 to 5.7 h) and the others did not differ from it. Raising the loss to 0.40 added 1.0 h (−0.4 to 2.7 h) for the elected leader, 3.9 h (2.0 to 5.9 h) for the auction, 3.7 h (2.3 to 5.3 h) for the learned network bids and 4.3 h (2.4 to 6.4 h) for the learned linear bids, that is 0.18, 0.67, 0.67 and 1.00 % of the loss-free wait per point of loss. The best allocators in Lott and Honary's benchmark lose about 0.3 % of makespan per point [11]; makespan and cargo wait are different quantities, so the two slopes are set side by side and not compared. The auction and the learned network bids rose above the marketplace at loss 0.40 (one-sided 95 % lower bound of the paired difference above zero); the elected leader and the learned linear bids stayed level with or below it through loss 0.40. Bursty loss at a mean rate of 0.25, against steady loss at the same rate, cut the wait of the auction by 1.4 h (95 % CI 0.4 to 2.4 h) and cut the wait of the learned linear bids by 1.2 h (95 % CI 0.4 to 2.2 h) and left the others within their intervals of zero.

Bundles of up to five jobs hurt the auction: against depth 1, depth 5 raised the urgent wait by 6.0 h (95 % CI 4.4 to 7.8 h) at loss 0.25 and raised it by 10.0 h (95 % CI 7.7 to 12.6 h) at loss 0.40, and depth 2 raised it by 3.7 h (95 % CI 1.8 to 5.6 h) at loss 0.25 and raised it by 5.0 h (95 % CI 2.3 to 7.6 h) at loss 0.40. Stale claims, bids on items that another robot had already taken by the time the bundle was accepted, rose from 0 to 314 per year at depth 5 and loss 0.40. H3b, that depth 5 is worse than depth 1 at loss 0.25 to 0.40, is supported. For the learned bids at loss 0.40, depth 5 raised the wait of the learned network bids by 9.8 h (95 % CI 7.6 to 12.0 h) and raised the wait of the learned linear bids by 8.7 h (95 % CI 6.4 to 11.1 h). The design has no cell with depth 2 or 5 and no loss, so it cannot say how much of this penalty is due to loss and how much to bundling under this demand; the stale claims are almost the same at loss 0.25 and 0.40 (85 and 86 a year at depth 2, 313 and 314 at depth 5), which points to bundling. The finding of Lott and Honary that the best depth falls under loss [10] can therefore be neither confirmed nor contradicted here.

Radio range mattered less than loss: against the default 5 km range, a 2 km range lowered the auction's urgent wait by 0.1 h (95 % CI −0.2 to 0.0 h), not distinguishable from zero, a 10 km range left it unchanged on every seed and a radio that reaches every robot raised it by 1.2 h (95 % CI −0.2 to 2.7 h), not distinguishable from zero. With the lost-link rule off the wait rose by 3,267.5 h (95 % CI 3,011.7 to 3,540.4 h), and the share of robot-time out of reach of every mast went from 5.1 % to 83.1 % (809 returns to a mast per year with the rule on). A second mast at pad B raised the wait by 1.5 h (95 % CI 0.1 to 3.2 h) and cut that share from 5.1 % to 5.0 %. H3c, that the elected leader matches the auction unless the mesh partitions, was tested in ten cells by equivalence within ±10 % (90 % CI of the ratio): the leader matched the auction under loss 0 and a perfect radio; it differed under loss 0.40 (faster by 2.6 h, 95 % CI 0.7 to 4.5 h) and the lost-link rule off (faster by 871.6 h, 95 % CI 571.3 to 1,149.8 h); under loss 0.10, loss 0.25, bursty loss, a 2 km range, a 10 km range and the second mast the interval was too wide to decide either way. E41's default auction cell reran E40's auction at 30 % on the same 30 seeds and reproduced every run exactly.

5.6 Learned bids (H4)

The learned linear correction cut the mean urgent wait relative to the hand-built auction by 2.3 h at the middle load (16.5 %; 95 % CI 0.6 to 3.9 h, n = 30) and by 0.9 h at light load (7.9 %; −0.1 to 2.2 h). The linear model is the secondary model of the registration, and its row entered the Holm family after its result on these seeds had been seen (Section 4.6). For it H4's first part is supported (Holm-adjusted p = 0.017); its second part, no difference beyond ±2 % at light load, fails. At 60 % load the linear model cut the wait by 31.5 h (95 % CI 25.2 to 38.2 h) and the network's difference, −1.3 h (95 % CI −6.0 to 3.1 h), includes zero (n = 30; Table 7).

The network did not improve on the hand-built bids. At the middle load its difference from the auction was −0.25 h (95 % CI −1.6 to 1.1 h), and at light load −1.06 h (−3.0 to 0.4 h); neither interval excludes zero. H4 as registered is about the network, the registered learned regime, with the linear model as a secondary report; for the network it is not supported.

The largest weights lower the bid, and so favour the item, for the planned hours of the job (+1.93 h per unit, the feature scaled by 1/3), the load as a share of capacity (+1.28 h per unit) and the number of robots heard (+1.04 h per unit), and raise it for the slack to the item's target time (−1.96 h per unit, the feature scaled by 1/10), the hours to drive to the pad (−1.41 h per unit) and the battery's state of charge (−1.39 h per unit); the other weights in each direction are smaller than these. We have not interpreted these weights: no weight was removed in turn to see what it contributes, the policy is not monotone in the inputs an operator would expect it to be (Section 5.11), and the search kept its last generation, so it may not have converged. The evaluation of the learned bids that was first made on 27 October 2026, before the two engine fixes of changes 4 and 5, was rerun on the corrected engine on 12 November 2026 with the same seeds and the same policies; the earlier values are kept in the old file. On its 30 seeds the linear correction changed the urgent wait against hand-built bids by −2.49 h (−4.15 to −0.82 h; −17.7 %) at 30 % load and by −30.3 h (−36.0 to −24.5 h; −40.4 %) at 60 %, in agreement with E40; the network's difference was −0.16 h (−1.61 to 1.30 h) and +5.4 h (−2.9 to 13.6 h), not distinguishable from zero at either load. Ten further runs per load check whether other cargo pays for the gain. At 60 % load it does not: the urgent wait and the delays of normal-priority and bulk cargo and tonne-days waiting all fell with the learned bids (−15 % for tonne-days), each with an interval below zero, and tonnes delivered did not change. At 30 % load the same check does not come out clean. On its 10 seeds the urgent wait did not move measurably (−1.23 h, −3.91 to 1.45 h), so on that smaller sample the gain itself is not distinguishable from zero, and the delay of bulk cargo rose (+0.23 h, 0.02 to 0.44 h); the delay of all cargo did not move measurably (+0.18 h, −0.04 to 0.39 h). At the middle load the learned bids therefore make bulk cargo wait a little longer, and we do not claim that their gain for urgent cargo is free for other cargo there. 5.7 Team lifts across owners (H6)

Allowing pairs of different owners raised the share of team items delivered from 12 % to 93 % at team share 0.10 (paired difference 82 percentage points, 95 % CI 78 to 86, n = 30) and from 11 % to 96 % at team share 0.25 (paired difference 86 percentage points, 95 % CI 83 to 88, n = 30) at message loss 0.10. The starting share is an input and not a finding: of the 12.4 team items a year at team share 0.10, 1.5 (12 %) and 28.4 team items a year at team share 0.25, 3.2 (11 %) weigh no more than the one same-owner pair can lift (FLEX with CLV-1, 2,080 kg), by the mass range we assumed, and the same-owner auction delivered 1.5 and 3.0. H6 is registered on “the share of items delivered” and names no team share [43]. The engine's measure is a share of mass, the kilograms delivered over the kilograms ready: it rose by 1.7 percentage points (1.5 to 2.0) at team share 0.10 and 5.4 percentage points (3.5 to 8.5) at team share 0.25. Counted in items, cross-owner pairs delivered 11.8 more items a year (8.0 to 15.7) on 2,920 at team share 0.10 and 80.7 more items a year (28.0 to 168.7) on 2,853 at team share 0.25. Every interval excludes zero, so H6's first part is supported in kilograms and in items. The Holm family tests it on the mass share at team share 0.25, a cell the registration does not name (adjusted p < 0.001, Table 7); 0.10 is the design's default. The gain held at loss 0.25 and 0.40 (Table 8).

Failed meetings rose with message loss: at team share 0.25 the auction with the commit step and a 1 h timeout had on average 4.0 failed meetings per year at loss 0.10, 12.1 at 0.25 and 25.2 at 0.40. Without the commit step it had more at loss 0.25 (15.2) and more at 0.40 (35.4): paired by seed, the step cut failed meetings by 3.1 (0.6 to 5.8) at loss 0.25 and 10.2 (4.5 to 16.0) at loss 0.40. That is the direction the second part of H6 names, but it is a property of the model and not an independent measurement. No message is repeated inside a handshake. With the commit step a meeting fails only when the proposal and the answer arrive and the commit is lost; without it a meeting fails whenever the single proposal is lost. If a message gets through with probability s, the two cases have probability s2(1 − s) and 1 − s, and the second is the larger for every s below one. We therefore report the second part of H6 as following from the message count and not as supported by a test. What the runs do measure is the step's price: the urgent wait with it was 1.8 h (0.1 to 3.6 h) longer at loss 0.25 and 9.1 h (6.8 to 11.5 h) longer at loss 0.40, a cost that excludes zero at both losses, because a meeting that the step refuses is a lift that waits. The timeout sets the waiting and cannot buy fewer failures in this model, where a partner that missed the commit never comes: the hours robots spent waiting at meeting points are the failed meetings times the timeout, 6.5 with a 0.5 h timeout, 12.1 with 1 h and 27.7 with 2 h at loss 0.25. The exploratory variant in which the base computer arbitrates each meeting had no failed meetings at either loss. It repeats its message to each vehicle up to 20 times within the timeout, where the commit variant repeats nothing, so that comparison is one of retry budgets and not of protocols.

5.8 What the protocol costs the radio (E53, H13)

A rover that reports its position once a second offers 0.418 kbit/s (52.3 B/s) with authentication: the position report, a packet of 41 B, and status and intent reports and the heartbeat that carries the declaration once every ten seconds. These loads are a calculation on a traffic model, sizes times assumed rates, with the sizes of version 0.2. Event messages add less than a thousandth of that. At 0.1 Hz the heartbeat also fills the silence and the load falls only to 0.277 kbit/s; at 10 Hz it is 3.37 kbit/s (Table 9). With authentication on and 20 rovers, the busiest link carried 8.37 kbit/s at one position report a second (95th percentile over the 30 draws; mean 7.96 kbit/s), inside the 16 kbit/s of the earlier budget, and 67.4 kbit/s at ten a second, above it. H13 is on the predicted side of the threshold in both clauses, and we report it as a design calculation and not as a test [54]. Its first clause is arithmetic on the sizes and rates we assumed and holds in any geometry: reports are not relayed, so no node can hear more than the fleet's sum, 20 × 0.418 kbit/s = 8.37 kbit/s, which is the 95th percentile above. The design note from which the second pre-registration took its parameters had already computed 49 B/s per robot, 7.9 kbit/s for 20 robots and 65.6 kbit/s for their position reports alone at ten a second, for the first version of the protocol; E53 gave 7.86 kbit/s with that version's sizes, and the heartbeat that carries the declaration in version 0.2 raises it to the 8.37 kbit/s above. That each clause held in every one of the 30 draws carries no information for the first clause, which no draw could have failed. H13 has no sampling test, and Holm's correction of the second pre-registration's family does not include it. Without authentication the same link carries 5.46 kbit/s and 44.3 kbit/s: the security header and code add 53 % to the load at 1 Hz and change neither side of the comparison.

5.9 Lost links and lost jobs (E54, H14)

At per-hop loss 0.25, drop and retry lost 10 of 6,028 awards, 0.33 jobs a seed (95 % CI 0.17 to 0.53). Store and forward lost 2, and none when unheard vehicles were assumed to return to their last link point. The paired difference between the third and the first is −0.33 jobs a seed (95 % CI −0.53 to −0.17, n = 30), which excludes zero: the first clause of H14 holds against this baseline. The baseline decides much of that. Drop and retry gives up after five tries, 12 minutes into a lease of 60 minutes, while store and forward can repeat 360 times in the same hour; neither setting was swept. Paths are almost always one hop (a mean of 1.01 to 1.03 hops to the base in E53), so custody at relays plays little part, and five independent losses in a row have probability p5: 6,028 × 0.255 = 5.9 of the 10 lost awards, 62 of the 85 lost at loss 0.40 and 0.06 of the 4 at 0.10. The others are vehicles that had no path for the whole lease, of which store and forward itself lost 2. The comparison is therefore mostly one of five tries in 12 minutes against 360 tries in an hour, and a baseline that retried every 10 s without a cap was not run. The 14-day Earth blackout that H14 names cannot affect this clause (Section 4.8). H14's second clause, that the rule leaves no reservation stuck past its timeout, is not testable as written, and we report no verdict on it [54]. The zero that the results file holds for it is set by the rule's definition: in the file's words the clause holds by rule, not by measurement, and only the first clause is measured. The rule as first written released a silent vehicle's reservation at its timeout and is withdrawn: releasing the segment is unsafe if the vehicle is disabled inside it. Under the rule of Section 3.13 the segment is marked unconfirmed and may be entered only at caution speed. What the replay gives is a count. Without the return to the last link point, 2,566 of the 2,628 reservations in the 30 seeds ended while their holder had no path to the base 60 s after the timeout. In the replay every vehicle had left its segment on schedule, but the base cannot know that, so these segments stay unconfirmed until the holder is heard again: 7,349 h in all, 245 h a seed. How many stay unconfirmed when vehicles return to their last link point was not counted, and what crossing an unconfirmed segment at caution speed costs in delay is not priced. In the family of the second pre-registration, the difference in lost jobs is tested by an exact sign test over the 30 seeds: fewer with store and forward and the return in 9 seeds, more in none, the same in 21 (one-sided p = 0.002; two-sided p = 0.004). Holm's correction over the three hypotheses of that family with a sampling test gives an adjusted p of 0.004 on the one-sided p and 0.008 on the two-sided one, so the first clause of H14 stands after the correction, against the baseline described above. Of the other two, H15a is supported (adjusted p = 0.003) and H10′ is not supported as a whole (adjusted p = 0.87; 0.93 before the position experiment behind it was regenerated after a correction to its positioning code, found in a code review on 13 November 2026); both are reported in the companion paper [45].

One further difference is a consequence of two settings and not a finding. A reservation grant lives 60 s and drop and retry repeats after 180 s, so it has one try on one hop and loses grants at the per-hop loss rate itself: 9.5 %, 25.6 % and 39.9 % at loss 0.10, 0.25 and 0.40. With custody, 2 of 6,028 grants were lost at loss 0.25 (0.03 %), and 3 with the return to the last link point. These are counts of messages: a grant was generated for every award, and more than half of the jobs have no single-lane reservation (Section 4.8), so a lost grant here is not a vehicle stopped before a segment. The return to the last link point changes what the base knows, not what the radio loses. Without it the base's picture of a vehicle was on average 88 min old (5,264 s) under either transport, and the worst-placed vehicle went unheard for 31 h. With it the mean age was 9.5 s and the worst vehicle's 39.9 s: the return was made 87.3 times a seed and removed 365 vehicle-hours of isolation a seed. The end-of-job reports show the cost of holding a message: drop and retry lost 32 % of them, store and forward lost 1.4 % and delivered the rest 543 s late on average, because a vehicle that ends a job out of reach waits for a contact, and with the return to the last link point the delay fell to 3.4 s and no report was lost. 5.10 Sensitivities

Letting the hoppers refuel every month changed nothing that matters: the full-information marketplace's urgent wait moved by −0.61 h (−2.1 to 0.4 h).

5.11 What did not work

Labelling the hand-built auction's rounds with the full-information marketplace's choice, the first iteration of the imitation stage, taught the bidding models nothing, because the marketplace uses the same bid rule: the hand-built bids already chose the marketplace's item in 92.9 % of held-out rounds, and every disagreement we examined (318 of 318 at 30 % load and 404 of 404 at 60 %) was an item the marketplace gave to another robot in the same round. These figures are a record of training: the first imitation round on the tuning seeds, with the engine of 27 October 2026; they were not rerun. On the corrected engine, on the evaluation seeds at 30 % load, the hand-built bids chose the marketplace's item in 88.35 % of rounds, the network's bids in 86.37 % and the linear bids in 70.76 %. The network's best tuning score came in its first generation; the linear model's came in its last. The network searched on its output layer did not beat the hand-built bids. The relay at 80 % coverage missed its equivalence margin. Bundle depth 5 made the auction worse at message loss 0.25 and 0.40 (Section 5.5). Of the 121 phase-5 checks of Section 3.11 tagged to this paper, 6 failed: four are the pairing check D10 (pairing by seed reduces the variance of the difference from the full-information marketplace) for owners alone from Earth with plan ahead, owners alone from Earth, strict, owners alone on the Moon and the strict dispatcher on Earth, whose delay varies mostly between start years (Section 4.5); two are the monotonicity check L5 (the learned bid is monotone in approach time, mass and battery charge) for the network and the linear model. The other 100 that ran passed; 15 were not run, because the check does not apply to this model or was left out, each with its reason in results/vv_phase5.json; one met its tolerance with a 99 % interval that misses the reference, recorded as a bias flag (D4 class 2). The L5 failures are properties of the trained policies, not of the code: the learned bid was not monotone in approach time, mass and battery for the network in 32 % of the probes and the linear model in 67 % of the probes, as the policies were tuned on urgent wait and not constrained to monotone bids (Section 3.10). After every experiment had run, a verification check found that cargo ids were not unique: 9–18 of about 2,970 items a year reused an earlier id, in every regime (change 10 of the pre-registration). Ids enter the simulator's decisions only through a planning memo within one dispatch call. The fix was tested by rerunning 48 full years (E40, E43: every regime or mode, seeds 1–3), of which 48 gave outcomes identical item by item, compared by content without ids, 39 of them in this paper's experiment; reused ids fell from 670 to 0. No experiment was rerun and no reported number changes; trace and scene files written before the fix still carry the duplicate ids.

6. Discussion

6.1 What the results say about the question

We asked whether the gain from sharing vehicles among owners survives when the decisions move from Earth onto the robots. In this model it does, and the measurements separate three things that behave differently. The first is capability, and it is set by the scenario. In this fleet the gain from pooling is cargo that owners alone had not delivered by the end of the year: owner C's small vehicles cannot lift its crates, and no single owner can pair its robots for most team items (Section 5.2). On cargo that both deliver, owners alone with Earth always in view waited 5.8 h longer. Lifts by pairs of different owners' rovers account for 32 % of the censored difference, and single-robot jobs taken by another owner's rover, together with the pooled dispatcher, for the rest. The design cannot separate the dispatcher's share of that rest, but the pooled regimes that differ only in their dispatcher sit within a few hours of one another (Section 5.1), so it can be little. This part of the gain needs no dispatcher in any particular place. It needs an agreement that lets one owner's rover carry another owner's cargo and lets two owners' rovers lift together. The second part is where the pooled decision is made. Keeping it on Earth cost 89.9 h of mean urgent wait per year when the dispatcher stopped at every blackout, 7.0 times the wait under uninterrupted dispatch, and the cost appeared in every start year in which the base saw Earth less than 60 % of the time (Section 5.3). The Earth-bound dispatcher's loss comes from the blackouts. While Earth is down, nothing is assigned; vehicles finish their current job and wait, and cargo that lands in the meantime queues at the pads (Section 5.3). Uploading plans before each blackout left 7.3 h of it and an 80 % relay left 2.6 h, less, but more than our equivalence margin allows. Moving the decision onto the robots left none that the data could detect, though not none that they could exclude: the auction among the robots, which hear one another only over the terrain and lose one message in ten, kept urgent cargo waiting 0.84 h less than the full-information marketplace, a difference not distinguishable from zero and a small fraction of what the Earth-bound dispatcher lost (Section 5.4). Two things qualify that reading: the auction's robots are sent back towards the mast when unheard and the marketplace's are not, and in the model the best bid of a round always wins whatever is lost (Sections 5.4 and 3.7). The third part is the quality of the bids. A learned linear correction with 13 weights, registered as the secondary model, cut the auction's urgent wait by 16.5 % at the middle load, and the registered network added nothing (Section 5.6). Beyond these, of the crates that need two rovers, 12 % are within the one same-owner pair's capacity by construction, and pairs of different owners' robots delivered 93 %; a commit step before each meeting cost urgent wait, and its lower count of failed meetings follows from the handshake's message count (Section 5.7).

Not every registered expectation held, and the failures say something. The equivalence test of the auction against the marketplace is reported as it came out in Section 5.4: the point estimate is 0.84 h less than the marketplace, the 90 % interval −2.6 to 0.7 h, the margin ±1.5 h. Ten percent of a mean of 14.9 h is a narrow margin in hours, which is why an interval that includes zero can still fail to show equivalence, and why the practical reading is the one in the previous paragraph rather than a verdict on the hypothesis. The 80 % relay missed its margin for the same reason: its loss is small next to the strict dispatcher's and still larger than a tenth of the marketplace's mean. The network's failure to beat the hand-built bids says more about how we trained it than about networks (Section 7). Its training gave it less chance than the linear model: imitation left its hidden layers at their initial values and the search adjusted only its output layer (Section 3.10), so this is no evidence that a network helps as trained here, and no evidence that a network cannot help.

The protocol work puts a size on what moving the decisions costs in messages. By calculation, the radio budget of 16 kbit/s that the companion paper assumed [44] holds for 20 rovers at one authenticated position report a second on a scheduled channel, counting packets before link framing and coding; it fails at ten reports a second, on random access, and when reports are relayed (Section 5.8). Away from the base the limit is reach before it is rate: spread over the routes, fewer than two rovers in three had a path to the base at a fleet of 20. In the replay of a month's messages, either transport rarely lost a job, and the difference between them is mostly the baseline's cap of five tries; the return to the last link point mattered for what the base knows: the base's picture of a vehicle was 88 min old on average without the return to the last link point and 9.5 s with it, a gain counted without the jobs the return would interrupt (Section 5.9). The propositions of Appendix B say how far the rest can be argued without an experiment, and Table 12 says which is which.

6.2 Comparison with earlier studies

Studies of allocation under message loss compare an algorithm with itself under perfect links. The best decentralised allocators in Lott and Honary's benchmark lose 11–13 % of makespan between perfect delivery and 40 % loss [10, 11]. Our comparison is different in kind: the auction at 10 % loss is set against a dispatcher with perfect information, and the quantity is cargo delay, not makespan. On that harder comparison it differed from the reference by −5.6 %. The slope against loss, which is the quantity those benchmarks report, is measured in Section 5.5. NASA's CADRE rovers are designed to coordinate through an elected leader with a greedy scheduler and no auction [31, 71]. Our leader regime is not CADRE's planner: it runs our marketplace on the base computer over the same lossy radio, with a 0.5 h election. It came within 0.26 h of the marketplace, so on this terrain, with a mast that most robots can see, our two on-Moon regimes did not differ measurably at this loss, though equivalence could not be shown (Section 5.5). The marketplace is the auction's own bid rule given perfect information, which is why we call it a reference and not a bound (Section 5.4).

Transport research measures what fragmentation among operators costs when every vehicle can serve every customer: the cost is slack, and the smallest operator pays most of it [27, 28]. On the Moon the fleets are heterogeneous, and fragmentation removes capability, not only slack: what separates owners alone from the pooled fleet here is cargo that owners alone had not delivered by the end of the year, a result of giving one owner no cargo rover. Kondor and colleagues found that the smallest operator pays most for fragmentation [28]; here the owner that pays most is the one without a cargo rover. Owner C's urgent cargo waited 2,575 h longer per year alone than pooled, against 404 h and 631 h for owners A and B (Section 5.2). What an owner lacks on the Moon is a vehicle class, not fleet size. Open-RMF lets robots of unrelated vendors share tasks and a traffic schedule, over a building network that is always up [30]; our regimes with an uninterrupted link to Earth are the lunar version of that assumption, and the strict regime is what happens when it fails. Most of what separates the pooled regimes is when cargo moves, while what separates them from owners alone is whether it moves at all (Section 5.1). Refuelling the hoppers changed nothing because a hopper carries 10 kg and the cargo that waits is heavy (Section 5.10).

Published learned allocators gain a few percent over their baselines: 1.3–5.3 % in deployed ride-hailing dispatch [23], and the learned bid inside CBBA of [18] is evaluated against CBBA's own bid under perfect links. Our 16.5 % is larger, and the reason is the baseline, not the learner. The linear correction beat not only the hand-built bids but the full-information marketplace that uses the same bid rule, so it measures the weakness of that rule under this demand; the hand-built rule was written, not tuned by search on the objective, while the linear model was. A stronger hand-built rule would shrink the gain, which is the comparison that Accorsi and colleagues ask for [26], and which we only partly made by testing the linear model against the network. What we add to that literature is the negative result for the network against a linear model with the same inputs, and the finding that imitation of the reference dispatcher teaches a bidder nothing when the reference uses the same rule. The linear model has 13 weights, which we have not interpreted (Section 5.6). We could have seen that imitation would teach nothing from the definitions, before collecting 624,640 robot-item rows, and report it so others do not repeat it (Section 5.11).

6.3 Implications

For whoever runs a south-pole base, the results order the options. A relay that is always up equals the full-information marketplace run for run, and the robots' auction was not distinguishable from either. Only a relay that is up 80 % of the time did worse than the auction: it left 2.6 h of extra urgent wait against the marketplace, and the auction's wait was 3.8 h shorter than its own (2.0 to 5.7 h; an unregistered comparison, Section 5.4). A dispatcher on Earth with plans uploaded before each blackout is an acceptable interim design at the lightest load, where every pooled regime except the strict one sits within 7.9 h of the others, and today's planned traffic sits at that load. The value of on-Moon dispatch grows with traffic.

For the owners, the agreement to share matters more than the software that shares. In this fleet the gain is cargo that owners alone had not delivered by the end of the year, and it is available with any dispatcher that pools the fleet; what the dispatcher's place and quality add is the smaller sum measured in Sections 5.3 to 5.6. Who pays whom for a lift, and what keeps an owner from misreporting its costs, are contract and market questions, taken up in the companion paper on the vehicles, the network and the market [44]. For the designers of LunaNet [39], the traffic that matters here is robot-to-robot: bids, awards, meeting commitments and relays of all three, which no current specification standardises. Section 3.12 is one way to write that layer down, small enough to count: 0.418 kbit/s per rover at one position report a second. The computer each robot needs is measured in [44]. The gain from learning needed no neural network: it came from a linear model of 13 weights, and the safety check on each vehicle decides whether a move is allowed whatever the bids say [45]; a lunar fleet does not need an opaque planner to get the gain we measured.

6.5 Future work

Four control runs would settle points left open here: drop and retry without a cap on its tries, the marketplace with the same return-to-coverage move as the auction, bundle depths 2 and 5 without message loss, and an ownership split in which every owner can lift its own cargo. Three extensions follow directly. The first is to train the network end to end on the simulated objective and from the linear policy's starting point, so that the question of whether nonlinearity buys anything is answered rather than left open. The second is a second site and a second terrain tile, with a link budget in the radio model, to learn whether the auction's result depends on the Connecting Ridge's mast coverage. The third is a hardware test: the bidding, the meeting protocol and the lost-link rule on rovers of the CADRE class over a real radio on an analogue site, which would measure what our simulation cannot, the message losses and timings of real links. For the protocol the next steps are a second implementation written from the schema alone and run against the conformance vectors, the messages on real radios through a channel emulator, and the lost-link return driven inside the engine. CADRE offers a check that needs nobody's permission: set our leader regime to one owner, three identical rovers, 25-minute wake cycles and a 10 s election, and see whether it reproduces the published behaviours, at most 10 s without a planner after the leader is lost and two-rover operation when one rover is excluded [71]; then run our auction on that case and count its messages against the leader's, which CADRE's authors expect to be fewer; then compare both with flight data once the mission has flown. Beyond these, the companion papers take the roads, the separation rules and the safety check [45], and the payments, the audits and the hardware sizing [44].

7. Limitations

The radio model is geometric. A link exists when two antennas see each other within a set range, with no link budget, no fading and no interference, and the range and clearance are provisional values that Section 5.5 varies. Real links on the ridge will fail in ways this model does not produce, and the message-loss rates we impose stand in for all of them. Nor does the link law enter this paper's runs: a link exists or it does not. As a calculation outside them, the companion paper's link budget [44] gives two rover antennas 5 km apart 1,574 kbit/s over flat ground and 701 kbit/s when the Moon's curvature is counted (5.0 dB more loss), so the 5 km range we assume is a range at which a narrow-band channel closes and a megabit channel may not.

One base, one terrain tile, one demand model. The base layout, the terrain, the cargo mix and the arrival process are those of our earlier papers [40–42]; three levels of demand are simulated, of which the lightest is the closest to today's plans. Nothing here says how the results change at another site, and the mast's coverage of the ridge is part of why the on-Moon regimes work.

The lost-link rule is ours. Robots that nobody hears drive to a rally point that the base's mast can see (Section 3.9). Without that rule the robots' network falls apart on this terrain. The rule is part of every on-Moon result and not of the reference: the marketplace has no such move, and where the radio reaches every robot the auction's small advantage over it disappears (Section 5.4), so the rule looks more like a help to the on-Moon regimes than a cost. The marketplace was not run with the same move. A base would need some such rule, and it need not be ours.

The marketplace is a reference, not a bound. It is the auction's own bid rule given perfect information and no dependence on Earth; it is not an optimal dispatcher, and the learned linear bids beat it. “Within 10 % of the marketplace” therefore means within 10 % of a good hand-built dispatcher, not of the best possible one.

Owners are honest and roads are free. Owners report their costs truthfully and accept the assignments they win, and vehicles do not interfere with one another on the road. Both assumptions are removed in the companion papers [44, 45], and the delay that rules of the road add is measured there.

The network was given a narrow test. Imitation left the network's two hidden layers at their random initial values, and the search on the objective adjusted only its 32 output weights, while the linear model's search covered all 13 of its weights (Section 3.10). The network was therefore a linear model on fixed random features of the inputs, searched with fewer candidates per weight than the linear model. Its failure to beat the hand-built bids is no evidence that a network helps as trained here; it is no evidence that a network cannot help. Two tests would settle it: training all 1,537 weights end to end on the simulated objective, with a gradient-free method such as an evolution strategy or with a policy-gradient method, and starting the network from the learned linear policy, so that it begins at the linear model's result and can only add what nonlinearity buys. Neither was run, and the pre-registration froze both models before the main runs (Section 4.6).

The simulation is checked, not validated against a lunar fleet. The engine's checks (Section 3.11) and those of our earlier papers compare it with closed-form cases, degenerate settings and its own earlier versions. No multi-owner fleet operates on the Moon, so no measured delay exists to validate against, and the absolute waits in Table 5 should be read as model outputs under stated assumptions; the paired differences between regimes are the result.

Censored waits. Cargo not delivered by the end of a simulated year carries the wait it had accumulated by then, so the primary outcome is a censored mean that understates the true wait of what owners alone leave behind, and the delivered-only mean reported in Section 5.2 conditions on delivery and so compares different sets of items. We report both and test neither as a substitute for the other; the treatment is the analogue, for a simulation, of reporting how missing observations were handled.

Three defects were found in the runs and corrected before the final analysis. Regimes that never wait for Earth give the same run for a seed whatever the start year, so their second block repeated seeds; comparisons between such regimes use the first block only (n = 30), and an interim reading that counted the repeats is withdrawn (Section 4.6). The auction's ordering of robots depended on a per-process hash, which made runs under message loss valid draws but not bit-reproducible; the order was fixed and the affected runs repeated, and the repeated runs changed no result. The third, in the engine's event handling, is described with its before-and-after comparison in Section 4.6. All three are recorded with dates in the pre-registration file [43].

The registered seed rule was not applied. No seeds were added in any cell, although the half-width exceeded 10 % of the mean in 29 of the 40 E40 cells (Section 4.5). Every interval is from the fixed design, and we did not extend any sample after seeing a result.

The ownership split and the horizon set the size of the sharing result. Three owners have a third of the cargo each, and one has no vehicle that can lift most of its own; team items are drawn at masses that only one same-owner pair can reach. That split was not varied in E40 to E42. Each run is one year from an empty base, so every regime is in its start-up year, and urgent cargo not delivered by day 365 counts with its wait up to then: the hours of H1 scale with the horizon.

The auction is modelled at the level of rounds. The best bid of each connected part of the network always wins, and a lost bid costs a parallel winner or a wasted trip. A message-level implementation, in which the best bid can go unheard, could do worse. The base computer holds every manifest and is the default leader, so the on-Moon regimes are less distributed than “among the robots” suggests. The engine has no channel capacity: messages are counted and never queue or collide, so the radio limit of Section 5.8 does not feed back into the main results. No vehicle fails in any run.

The learned bids' gain is not shown to be free for other cargo at the middle load. In the evaluation as rerun on the corrected engine, at the middle load a ten-seed check found bulk cargo waiting slightly longer, and the urgent gain itself was not distinguishable from zero on those ten seeds; at 60 % load other cargo gained too (Section 5.6). The cells of Table 6 were rerun on the corrected engine with the rest of that evaluation. The imitation statistics of Section 5.11 are a training record from the earlier engine. The frozen policies were also run three times on the evaluation seeds, not once; no training or selection used those seeds.

The protocol is counted and replayed, not transmitted. E53 counts bytes on a geometric mesh, with no radio hardware, no link budget, no interference and no channel-access protocol; the efficiency of a scheduled channel, 0.8, is assumed and was not swept. The sizes are packets before link framing and channel coding, the traffic model holds 11 of the 15 message types, and its fleet geometry samples where eight vehicles spend their time, which is near the base; drawn from driving steps only, or with status and intent reports also sent once a second, the calculation comes out differently (Section 5.8). The position report carries no covariance, and the frame, datum and time-scale codes it relies on are a site registry that we assumed. The replay E54 was repeated at the packet sizes of version 0.2 and in Proximity-1 frames, and no value and no verdict changed. That is a property of the replay's message set and not a test of version 0.2: it sends task awards, reservation grants and task states, whose sizes version 0.2 does not change, and a size counts only against a node's store of 64 MB. E54 replays one recorded month of one fleet at the level of messages. Its baseline, drop and retry capped at five tries in twelve minutes, is assumed and was not swept, and most of the difference between the transports follows from that cap. Its lost-link return is applied to the contact plan while the recorded jobs stay, so a vehicle is at its last link point for the radio and on its job for the logistics: the return's gain in contact is counted and its cost in interrupted jobs is not. A returning vehicle is given a direct link to the base, which is optimistic. The Earth blackout bears only on the ledger. No reservation is released for a silent holder; the replay counts the segments that would stay unconfirmed, and the delay of crossing them at caution speed is not priced.

The protocol has one implementation. The schema, the codec and the conformance vectors were written together. Until a second implementation, written from the schema alone, decodes the vectors, interoperability is a design aim. The authentication code and the counter let a receiver refuse forged and replayed messages from outside the site, which our decoder has done only since a code review (Section 3.12) and only on packets it built itself, never over a radio link; a vehicle that holds the site key can still lie about itself, and the handling of keys is designed and not tested.

The propositions are about models. Proposition 1 covers one allocation round with comparable bids that reach every bidder, and bounds a score below a ceiling; it gives no bound on cost or waiting. It says nothing about the schedule over a year, about lost messages (measured in Section 5.5) or about owners who shade their bids [44]. For the published 50 % bound of the consensus-based bundle algorithm we read two later papers of its authors' group [5, 74] and not the original proof [4]. Proposition 3(a) assumes independent loss; bursts make ages worse. Propositions 4 and 5 are formulas with assumed inputs: no rover failure is simulated and no operator was observed, and the inputs of Proposition 5 were chosen to match published ratios. Proposition 6 counts the bits of a layout whose ranges we set by its bit widths.

CADRE has not flown, and we have not reproduced it. The comparison in Section 3.13 rests on its team's papers [71, 72]. We did not run its design in our engine, and our leader regime takes 0.5 h to elect where CADRE's design takes at most 10 s.

8. Conclusion

The gain from pooling several owners' vehicles at a lunar south-pole base survives, in this simulation, when dispatch moves from Earth onto the robots. A pooled dispatcher that stops when Earth sets costs 89.9 h of urgent wait to delivery; planning ahead and a relay recover most of that; the robots' auction came within 0.8 h of the full-information marketplace, not distinguishable from zero but too wide to show equivalence. With one owner lacking a cargo rover, the gain from pooling is cargo that owners alone had not delivered by year-end; on cargo both delivered, they waited 5.8 h longer. The registered learned bid, a network, did not beat hand-built bids; a linear correction, registered as secondary, cut the urgent wait by 16.5 %.

By calculation, the periodic reports and the task and road messages of 20 rovers offer 8.4 kbit/s at one authenticated position report a second, which fits a 16 kbit/s channel only if the base schedules it and reports travel one hop. In a replay of one month, drop-and-retry capped at five tries lost 10 of 6,028 awards at 25 % loss, store-and-forward 2, and none when unheard vehicles were assumed to return to their last link point.

An always-up relay equals the marketplace run for run, the auction was not distinguishable from it, and only an 80 % relay was worse than the auction, by 3.8 h (2.0 to 5.7 h). The results hold for one base, one ownership split, a geometric radio and honest owners.

Appendix B. Guarantees

Each proposition is stated with its assumption and proved in a few lines. Propositions 5 and 6 are counts on inputs we chose and not theorems about the Moon; they keep the name for the numbering. The numbers that follow each proof are computed by the build script from the files named in Data and code availability, and the script checks them against the codec, the result files or a seeded simulation where the text says so. Table 12 says which of these are also tested.

Proposition 1 (one auction round). Robots i bid for jobs j. An assignment A is a set of pairs (i, j) in which each job appears at most once and each robot at most L times. Robot i scores the set Ai of its jobs by Si(Ai), with Si of the empty set zero, and the team score is F(A) = Σi Si(Ai). Assume that adding a job never lowers a robot's score and that a job is never worth more to a robot that already holds more (diminishing marginal gain): for B contained in B′, Si(B + j) − Si(B) ≥ Si(B′ + j) − Si(B′) ≥ 0. The greedy rule fixes, among the pairs that keep the assignment allowed, the one that raises F most, and repeats until no allowed pair raises it. Let G be its result and O any allowed assignment. Then (a) if L = 1, F(G) ≥ F(O)/2, and non-negative pair values are all that is needed; (b) if no robot holds L jobs in G, F(G) ≥ F(O)/2; (c) in every case F(G) ≥ F(O)/3.

Proof. Write g1, …, gm for the pairs in the order greedy fixed them, Gk for the first k of them and δk = F(Gk) − F(Gk−1) > 0, so that the δk sum to F(G). For a pair o write Δ(o | A) = F(A + o) − F(A); F is defined for any set of pairs, each robot scoring the jobs paired with it. Scores never fall, so F(O) ≤ F(O ∪ G). Adding the pairs of O that are not in G to G one at a time, each gains at most what it would gain on G alone, because gains diminish. Hence

F(O) ≤ F(G) + Σ Δ(o | G), the sum over the pairs o of O not in G. (1)

Take such a pair o = (i, j). If G + o is still allowed, greedy stopped with o available, so Δ(o | G) ≤ 0. Otherwise let k be the first step after which o is no longer allowed. Before any step up to k, o was allowed, so greedy's choice at that step gained at least Δ(o | G), again because gains diminish. Step k blocked o in one of two ways. Either gk took job j: charge o to gk; O holds job j once, so a greedy pair receives at most one charge of this kind. Or gk was the L-th job of robot i: then o was allowed at each of the L steps that gave robot i a job; O gives robot i at most L pairs, so those blocked in this way can be charged one each to robot i's L greedy pairs. Both cannot happen at one step, since a pair of robot i that takes job j is o itself. Every greedy pair is thus charged at most twice, once through its job and once through its robot, and each charge is no more than its δ. With (1), F(O) ≤ F(G) + 2F(G), which is (c). If no robot is full in G, only charges through jobs occur and F(O) ≤ 2F(G), which is (b). For (a), with one job per robot F is the plain sum of pair values w(i, j) ≥ 0 and (1) is not needed. Each pair of O is in G, or has a value that is not positive, or was blocked by a greedy pair worth at least as much. A greedy pair shares its robot with at most one pair of O and its job with at most one, and a greedy pair that is itself in O shares neither with another pair of O. Hence F(O) ≤ 2F(G). ∎

One half cannot be raised in (a): with two robots, two jobs and values w(1, a) = 1 + ε, w(1, b) = w(2, a) = 1 and w(2, b) = 0, greedy scores 1 + ε and the best assignment 2. For (c) we know no instance below one half and no proof above one third. That the robots' exchange of bids reaches the greedy result at all is the convergence that the algorithm's authors state: under diminishing gain the largest bid in the network cannot be beaten later, so every robot agrees on it after as many message rounds as the network's diameter, and the argument repeats for the next pair [74]. We take that from them.

What the proposition covers in this paper is narrow. The auction of Section 3.7 at bundle depth 1 fixes the lowest bid first and gives each free robot at most one job in a round. With a ceiling C at or above every bid and the value C minus the bid, lowest bid first is largest value first, so (a) applies: the round's total value is at least half the best possible. The guarantee is in value below the ceiling, depends on the free constant C, and gives no bound in cost: with costs c(1, a) = 0, c(1, b) = c(2, a) = 1 and c(2, b) = M, lowest bid first pays M where the best assignment pays 2, for any M. Diminishing gain was not verified for the engine's bid, and the small instances use a toy score, not that bid. Also, the factor of two in cost published for sequential auctions [2] is for robots that chain several tasks on one path and does not cover one job per robot. It treats each load as one job, and a load of several items can overlap other robots' loads in more than one item, which the proof does not cover. It holds for one round in which every bid reaches every bidder; the schedule over a year and the effect of lost messages are measured in Sections 5.4 and 5.5 and not proved. For bundle depths above 1, which Section 5.5 found worse under loss, (b) and (c) are what we can prove. The 50 % usually quoted for the consensus-based bundle algorithm is as reported in [5, 74]; we could not read the original proof [4]. On the 200 small instances of Section 3.11 the lowest share of the optimum was 85.1 % at depth 1 and 85.3 % at depths 2 and 3.

Proposition 2 (the bit rate a fleet needs). Let each of N rovers send message type m at rate fm as a packet of sm bytes (to which link framing and coding add). The load offered at the sources is L = 8N Σm fmsm bit/s. If every message is transmitted h times on average (its hops) on one shared channel, and the channel carries successful transmissions for a share η of the time, then the channel's bit rate R must satisfy R ≥ Lh/η.

Proof. Over a long time τ the sources generate Lτ bits. Each is transmitted h times on average, so Lhτ bits must be transmitted successfully. The channel carries at most ηRτ. ∎

On a scheduled channel with a share g of the time left as guard intervals, η = 1 − g; E53 uses g = 0.2 (Assumed). On slotted random access in which each of N stations transmits in a slot independently with probability a, a slot carries exactly one transmission with probability Na(1 − a)N−1, largest at a = 1/N, where it equals (1 − 1/N)N−1: 0.377 for N = 20, falling towards 1/e = 0.368 as N grows. E53 uses 1/e. With E53's sizes and rates a rover offers 52.3 B/s, so N = 20 gives L = 8.37 kbit/s. With h = 1: R ≥ 10.46 kbit/s on a scheduled channel (η = 0.8) and 22.7 kbit/s at η = 1/e; with h = 2 on the scheduled channel, 20.9 kbit/s. The condition is necessary and not sufficient: it ignores queues, capture and stations that cannot hear each other.

Proposition 3 (the age of a relayed report). A source sends a report every T seconds. The age at a receiver at time t is t minus the sending time of the newest report it has received [80]. (a) Relay without retry. Each report crosses k hops, each taking D seconds and losing the report independently with probability p, with no retransmission (the engine's loss model of Section 3.5, without its retry). Let q = (1 − p)k. Then the time-average age is kD + T(2 − q)/(2q); the mean age just before a report arrives is kD + T/q; and at almost every time, for every whole number m ≥ 0, the age exceeds kD + mT with probability (1 − q)m. (b) Store and forward. If no report is lost or expires, and a report waits Wj for a contact and takes Dj on hop j, then at all times the age is at most T plus the largest total Σj (Wj + Dj) over the reports.

Proof. (a) A report arrives kD after it was sent, with probability q, independently of the others. Between two arrivals the age rises in a straight line from kD to kD + X, where X = NT and N, the number of reports from one arrival to the next, takes the value n with probability q(1 − q)n−1, with mean 1/q and second moment (2 − q)/q2. The time average of such a sawtooth is kD + E[X2]/(2E[X]) = kD + T(2 − q)/(2q), and its mean peak is kD + E[X] = kD + T/q. The age at t exceeds kD + mT exactly when none of the reports sent in the interval from t − kD − mT (excluded) to t − kD (included) arrived. Unless t − kD is a multiple of T, that interval holds m sending times, and the probability is (1 − q)m. (b) Let Δ be the largest total. A report was sent in the interval of length T that ends at t − Δ, and it has arrived by t, so the newest report received is no older than T + Δ. ∎

So with probability 1 − ε the age in (a) is at most kD + mT for the smallest whole m ≥ 1 with (1 − q)m ≤ ε (Table B1). At per-hop loss 0.40 over three hops the mean age of a 1 Hz report is 4.2 s and it exceeds 29 s one time in a thousand; for a direct neighbour at loss 0.25 the two figures are 0.84 s and 5.0 s. A relay that forwards the newest report first keeps the age lowest in a multi-hop network [81]. E54's ages are of kind (b): the worst vehicle's 31 h without the lost-link rule is its longest stretch with no path to the base, and the rule shortens that stretch to 40 s (Section 5.9). No experiment of this paper relays a report over more than one hop, so Table B1 is an illustration of the formula.

Proposition 4 (hand-over after a failure). A vehicle sends a heartbeat every Thb, and a neighbour declares it silent when k heartbeats in a row have not arrived, that is, a time kThb after the last one it received. (a) A vehicle that fails at a moment uniformly placed between two heartbeats, and whose last heartbeat was received, is declared silent after a delay between (k − 1)Thb and kThb, with mean (k − ½)Thb; if its last heartbeats were lost the delay is shorter. (b) If each heartbeat of a live vehicle is lost independently with probability p, a false declaration begins at a given heartbeat with probability (1 − p)pk. (c) The expected extra wait of the failed vehicle's job is E[ΔT] = E[detection] + RTround + E[d]/v + cTx − Tsaved, for R auction rounds of length Tround, a new winner at distance d driving at speed v, cargo aboard the failed vehicle with probability c and a transfer time Tx, and work Tsaved that the new winner need not repeat. (d) If instead the decision waits until Earth is in view, and Earth is hidden in gaps of length G separated by windows of length W, a failure at a uniformly random time waits G2/(2(G + W)) on average for the next window.

Proof. (a) Let the failure fall a time U after the last heartbeat sent, U uniform between 0 and Thb. The declaration comes kThb after that heartbeat, which is kThb − U after the failure. If the last n heartbeats were lost, the count began nThb earlier. (b) A false declaration begins at a heartbeat when it and the k − 1 before it are lost and the one before those was received. (c) The delays add, and expectation is linear. (d) The failure falls in a gap with probability G/(G + W) and is then uniformly placed in it, with mean remaining time G/2. ∎

The protocol's heartbeat is 1 s: a vehicle sends HELLO whenever it would otherwise send nothing in a second (Table A2). With k = 5 (Assumed), a failed vehicle is marked silent after 4.5 s on average and at most 5 s. One rover watching one live rover raises 0.78 false marks a day at loss 0.10 and 531 at 0.40; keeping them below one a day needs k = 5, 8 and 12 at loss 0.10, 0.25 and 0.40. Being marked silent does not hand the job over. By Section 3.13 the base marks the vehicle failed only after a second, longer timeout or a sighting, and the design gives that timeout no value; a silent vehicle's job returns to the pool when its lease ends, at most 60 min after it was last heard with the lease of E54. With that lease, two auction rounds of 10 s, a new winner 1.5 km away at 4 km/h, and cargo aboard the failed rover with probability 0.5 and a 1 h transfer (all Assumed), the expected extra wait is up to 60 min + 20 s + 22.5 min + 30 min = 112.8 min, about 1.9 h. If a sighting confirmed the failure at once it would be 4.5 s + 20 s + 22.5 min + 30 min = 52.9 min. Detection and auction are seconds in both cases, so a shorter heartbeat would buy nothing; the lease sets the wait. With gaps of 11 days and windows of 16 days at this base [42], the wait for Earth alone is 53.8 h, 29 times the hand-over on the Moon with the lease and 61 times the one after a sighting. Rover failures are not simulated in this paper, so the proposition is a model with assumed inputs. E54 tests only its message side: an award that expires is auctioned again.

Proposition 6 (the size of a position report). With the ranges and steps of Table B3, a position report needs at least 104 bits in any fixed-length code, so its content cannot be shorter than 13 B in whole bytes. LRIP's layout uses exactly 13 B, and the packet with authentication is 41 B, before link framing and coding.

Proof. The eight fields must tell apart the numbers of values in the fourth column of Table B3. A fixed-length code for all their combinations needs at least the base-2 logarithm of the product of those numbers, 103.4 bits, hence 104; coding each field on its own needs the same 104. That many bits do not fit in one byte fewer, so 13 B is the least whole number of bytes, and the layout's 104 bits leave 0 spare, since version 0.2 uses the six bits that version 0.1 left for byte alignment. To the content the narrow-band profile adds the packet header (6 B), the LRIP header (8 B), the security header (6 B) and the code (8 B), 28 B in all, which gives 41 B; with the 16 B code it is 49 B, and with no security 27 B. ∎

What the count proves is limited. For seven of the nine fields (e, n, h, speed, sigma_h, moving and u95_code) the number of values is two to the power of the field's bits, because we set the range by the bit width; for those the lower bound is definitional. Only heading and nav_src have a count that is not a power of two, which is why the joint count is 103.4 bits, which rounds up to the 104 the layout uses. The steps are also not matched to one another: the position step is 1 cm and the step of its own uncertainty 5 cm. A coarser position step or a smaller site grid would save bits, so 13 B is the size of this layout and not a lower bound for a position report in general. As a CBOR array in the broadband profile the golden report is 54 B; that size depends on the values.
