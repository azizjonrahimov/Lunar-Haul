Rules of the Road on the Moon and Mars: Traffic Rules for Surface Robots of Different Owners, Checked and Priced

Azizjon Rahimov
Roatify Research · Preprint, October 2026 · Code, data and pre-registration: github.com/azizjonrahimov/Lunar-Haul · Live site: azizjonrahimov.github.io/Lunar-Haul

Abstract. Rovers and robots of several companies will soon share the roads and landing zones of a lunar south-pole base, and later a Mars base, with no traffic rules written for them. Policy documents ask for coordination but give no operating rules. We propose rules for surface traffic among vehicles of different owners: separation from lunar braking physics, exclusive reservations with an exit bay on single-lane roads, timed landing closures, crew keep-clear zones and a rule for lost radio contact, enforced by a safety program on each vehicle. We checked them three ways on one simulated south-pole base: an exhaustive search of a discrete model, {{P2-E44-N-ENCOUNTERS}} simulated encounters in twelve types, and {{P2-N-RUNS-E43}} runs of a logistics model that prices the rules in cargo delay at one to eight times today's fleet. {{P2-H7-ABSTRACT-CLAUSE}} At today's fleet the rules added {{P2-H8-X1-PCT}} to the delay per delivery and {{P2-DEADLOCKS-TOTAL}} deadlocks, and {{P2-H8-X4-PCT}} at four times the fleet. Owners who refer every conflict to Earth instead saw delay per delivery rise {{P2-UNC-X1-RATIO}}-fold. {{P2-H9-ABSTRACT-CLAUSE}} A base whose robots settle conflicts among themselves pays almost nothing for safety; one that keeps the decision on Earth pays in every blackout. The rules are a proposal for the agreements the Artemis Accords leave open.

Keywords: lunar surface traffic, multi-robot coordination, runtime assurance, formal verification, rules of the road, Mars surface operations

1. Introduction

Within a few years the roads of a lunar south-pole base will carry rovers, cargo vehicles and small robots that belong to different companies. NASA's Lunar Terrain Vehicle task orders of May 2026 went to two companies for two different rovers, cargo rovers are company designs, and small rovers and hoppers come from other firms again [54]. Each will be driven or supervised from its owner's control room on Earth. Where their routes cross, where a road narrows to one lane between a crater rim and a slope, where a lander is about to come down, and where crew are working on foot, somebody has to decide who waits. Today nobody has written down how.

The policy layer exists and is deliberately thin. The Artemis Accords ask signatories to declare safety zones around their activities, to notify one another and to coordinate before entering, and they say that zones are temporary and not exclusive [1]. NASA's own policy analysis adds three facts that shape any traffic rule: transit corridors near the south pole can be as narrow as 20–40 m in places; nobody agrees on how far to stand from a landing, so a simple standard distance is suggested; and once three or more actors operate, some central coordination “may become necessary” [2]. A conference paper of 2025 argues for lunar rules of the road modelled on the maritime rules, without numbers [3]. None of these documents says what a rover should do when it meets another owner's rover on a one-lane road, how close a robot may pass to a crew member, or what happens to a vehicle that loses contact.

Earth has rule sets for machines that share space, and each solves part of the problem. Warehouse fleets move hundreds of robots over a grid with a central planner and a reservation table, and the execution layer that makes those plans safe on real robots is well understood [4, 5, 6]. Autonomous intersection management gives cars time–space reservations at a junction and shows that a vehicle which never assumes an unconfirmed reservation cannot collide because of a lost message [7]. Drone traffic management lets operators that share no controller file their intentions with federated services, check them against one another, and monitor conformance [8, 9, 10]. Runtime assurance puts a small verified safety program between an untrusted planner and the vehicle [11, 12, 13], and responsibility-sensitive safety gives closed-form safe distances for road cars [14]. What none of them covers together is a surface with long one-lane roads, vehicles of very different speeds and braking, landing zones that close for an hour, crew on foot, radio that fails behind terrain, and owners who do not share a planner. Lunar braking is the physical reason the car rules do not transfer: braking force scales with gravity, so a rover at 15 km/h needs about six times the stopping distance it would need on Earth, and more downhill [15, 16].

We also wanted to know what the rules cost, because a rule that stops everything is trivially safe. The safety literature measures the cost of a safety layer as task performance with and without it on the same scenarios, as how often the layer overrides the planner, and as deadlocks it introduces [13, 6]. For a lunar base the natural measure is cargo delay, which our earlier papers computed for the same base, vehicles and demand [40, 41, 42]. Our question in this paper is therefore in three parts. Can a small, shared rule set keep robots of several owners safe on real lunar terrain against any planner? What does it cost in delay at today's fleet and at larger ones? And does it still hold on Mars, where gravity is twice the Moon's, Earth is minutes away and contact is lost for two weeks a year?

We built the rules into the simulation of [40, 41, 42] at three levels. A discrete model with two or three vehicles, one landing and one crew member is searched exhaustively against a planner that may propose anything; removing a rule must produce a counterexample, or the check proves nothing. An encounter simulator runs continuous kinematics at 10 Hz through the same safety program on twelve types of encounter, from following on a 20° slope to crew emerging from behind a boulder and a planner that proposes random or adversarial moves, and bounds the violation rate per type by the rule of three. The logistics model of our earlier papers runs the base for a year with the road layer on and off, with the same seeds, under two coordination modes: one shared reservation table over the robots' radio mesh, and owners who each see only their own reservations and refer conflicts to Earth. The road graph comes from the base's route network on the 5 m terrain, with one caveat we state up front: at the rovers' 20° slope limit the terrain shows no corridor narrower than two vehicles, so the share of single-lane road is an assumed factor that we vary from none to {{P2-SHARE-MAX-PCT}}, marking the narrowest places first (Section 3.1). The hypotheses, comparisons and statistics were written down before the main runs [43].

Our contributions are:

  • A parameterised rule set for lunar and Martian surface traffic among vehicles of different owners, with each parameter traced to a source, a derivation or an assumption to be swept: separation from braking physics, single-lane reservations with an exit bay, landing closures, crew zones, lost-contact behaviour, a priority ladder in which owner identity never counts, and conformance.
  • Three kinds of evidence for the same rules. An exhaustive check of a discrete model against any planner with mutation tests, per-type statistical bounds from simulated encounters, and the delay the rules cost inside a year-long logistics model, all from one implementation of the safety check.
  • A measured price of coordination. The delay cost of the rules at one, two, four and eight times today's fleet, the congestion peak, and the cost of keeping conflicts on Earth instead of settling them on the Moon, on real terrain with the base's real Earth-link windows.
  • The same rules on Mars. Gravity, the Earth round trip and the conjunction changed, nothing else, with the lunar vehicles kept on purpose; every Mars encounter and run is paired with its lunar twin.

Several things are outside this paper. Who gets which job, and how robots of different owners share jobs and lift together, is the subject of a companion paper [44]; here every cell uses the same pooled dispatcher, so only the road layer differs between runs. Payments and owners who misreport are the subject of another [45]; here every vehicle obeys the rules and reports its position honestly. The road graph is the base's route network, not a street plan; single-lane road is an assumed share of it. The rules handle vehicles and crew on foot, not landers in flight beyond the closure window, and not the dust a landing raises. The model checker covers a small model exhaustively and the encounter simulator covers twelve families; neither covers real sensors, and we say so in Section 7.
