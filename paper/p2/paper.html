Rules of the Road on the Moon and Mars: Traffic Rules for Surface Robots of Different Owners, Checked and Priced

Azizjon Rahimov
Roatify Research · Preprint, October 2026, revised November 2026 · Code, data and pre-registration: github.com/azizjonrahimov/Lunar-Haul · Live site: azizjonrahimov.github.io/Lunar-Haul

Abstract. Rovers of several companies will soon share a lunar base's roads without traffic rules. We propose ten rules, enforced by a safety program on each vehicle, and check them in simulation on one base: exhaustive search, 274,580 encounters, 650 logistics runs at up to eight times today's fleet, Mars gravity, and one-month replays with position, clock, message and report errors. The safety program allowed no violation in ten of twelve encounter types, all flat or downhill: a vehicle that stops dead in a one-lane segment defeats the gap rule, and one landing closure in 10,000 left a vehicle inside the zone. Uphill, a braking leader took followers inside the 5 m margin, without contact, until the rule gained a slope term. At today's fleet the rules added no measurable delay (at most 1.4 %) when reservations are filed with the job; referring conflicts to Earth, at an assumed two staff hours, raised delay 5.1-fold. At Mars gravity only flat encounters could be driven; with Earth assumed visible half of each sol, referral cost one-sixth as much. Without satellites, ranging held position error inside the 5 m margin where two beacons were in sight (23 % of driving) but not over the registered radio range (50 m); terrain fixes left 15 m elsewhere, and their stops, not the reservation margins, set the cost: 0.63 h per delivery (3.5 %, a lower bound). With a 0.1 s guard, clocks up to 0.84 s off caused no conflict in 303 reservation pairs, outside the guarantee. A network found slow position drifts 24 s sooner than a chi-square test, but every detector missed at least 37 % of them and none kept its false-alarm rate with noisier sensors, so none qualifies for the safety path. The rules propose what the Artemis Accords leave open.

Keywords: lunar surface traffic, multi-robot coordination, runtime assurance, formal verification, rules of the road, Mars surface operations

1. Introduction

Within a few years the roads of a lunar south-pole base will carry rovers, cargo vehicles and small robots that belong to different companies. NASA's Lunar Terrain Vehicle task orders of May 2026 went to two companies for two different rovers, cargo rovers are company designs, and small rovers and hoppers come from other firms again [54]. Each will be driven or supervised from its owner's control room on Earth. Where their routes cross, where a road narrows to one lane between a crater rim and a slope, where a lander is about to come down, and where crew are working on foot, somebody has to decide who waits. Today nobody has written down how.

The policy layer exists and is deliberately thin. The Artemis Accords ask signatories to declare safety zones around their activities, to notify one another and to coordinate before entering, and they say that zones are temporary and not exclusive [1]. NASA's own policy analysis adds three facts that shape any traffic rule: transit corridors near the south pole can be as narrow as 20–40 m in places; nobody agrees on how far to stand from a landing, so a simple standard distance is suggested; and once three or more actors operate, some central coordination “may become necessary” [2]. The abstract of a 2025 conference paper argues for lunar rules of the road modelled on the maritime rules, without numbers; we could not obtain the paper itself [3]. None of these documents says what a rover should do when it meets another owner's rover on a one-lane road, how close a robot may pass to a crew member, or what happens to a vehicle that loses contact.

Earth has rule sets for machines that share space, and each solves part of the problem. Warehouse fleets move hundreds of robots over a grid with a central planner and a reservation table, and the execution layer that makes those plans safe on real robots is well understood [4, 5, 6]. Autonomous intersection management gives cars time–space reservations at a junction and shows that a vehicle which never assumes an unconfirmed reservation cannot collide because of a lost message [7]. Drone traffic management lets operators that share no controller file their intentions with federated services, check them against one another, and monitor conformance [8, 9, 10]. Runtime assurance puts a small verified safety program between an untrusted planner and the vehicle [11, 12, 13], and responsibility-sensitive safety gives closed-form safe distances for road cars [14]. What none of them covers together is a surface with long one-lane roads, vehicles of very different speeds and braking, landing zones that close for an hour, crew on foot, radio that fails behind terrain, and owners who do not share a planner. Lunar braking is the physical reason the car rules do not transfer: friction braking scales with gravity, so a rover at 15 km/h needs about six times the stopping distance it would need on Earth, and more downhill [15, 16].

We also wanted to know what the rules cost, because a rule that stops everything is trivially safe. The safety literature measures the cost of a safety layer as task performance with and without it on the same scenarios, as how often the layer overrides the planner, and as deadlocks it introduces [13, 6]. For a lunar base the natural measure is cargo delay, which our earlier papers computed for the same base, vehicles and demand [40, 41, 42]. Our question in this paper is therefore in three parts. Can a small, shared rule set keep robots of several owners safe on real lunar terrain against any planner? What does it cost in delay at today's fleet and at larger ones? And does it still hold on Mars, where gravity is 2.3 times the Moon's, Earth is minutes away and contact is lost for two weeks in every 26 months? A fourth part came later, with a second pre-registration [59]: do the rules survive vehicles that do not know exactly where they are, clocks that differ, messages that are lost and a neighbour whose reported position is wrong?

We built the rules into the simulation of [40, 41, 42] at three levels. A discrete model with two or three vehicles, one landing and one crew member is searched exhaustively against a planner that may propose anything; removing a rule clause must produce a counterexample, or the check proves nothing. An encounter simulator runs continuous kinematics at 10 Hz through the same safety program on twelve types of encounter, from following on a downhill slope to crew emerging from behind a boulder and a planner that proposes random or adversarial moves, and bounds the violation rate per type by the rule of three. The logistics model of our earlier papers runs the base for a year with the road layer on and off, with the same seeds, under two coordination modes: one shared reservation table over the robots' radio mesh, and owners who each see only their own reservations and refer conflicts to Earth. The road graph comes from the base's route network on the 5 m terrain, with one caveat we state up front: at the rovers' 20° slope limit the terrain shows no corridor narrower than two vehicles, so the share of single-lane road is an assumed factor that we vary from none to 40 %, marking the narrowest places first (Section 3.1). The hypotheses, comparisons and statistics were written down before the main runs [43].

The results are these. The exhaustive search of the discrete model found no unsafe state, no deadlock and no livelock in 47,085 reachable states over four layouts, every state could reach the goal within 41 steps, and each of the three rule clauses we removed (of R3, R5 and R7) produced a counterexample (Section 5.7). In 274,580 driven encounters of twelve types, all on the flat or downhill, and a rerun of three types on fresh seeds, the safety program allowed no hard violation in ten types, with a 95 % upper bound on the violation probability of 7.6 × 10−4 or less per type under that type's parameter distribution; the exceptions are a vehicle that stops dead inside a one-lane segment (type 9), where the following vehicle came inside the 5 m static margin in 58 of 10,000 encounters and touched it in 7, because the gap rule assumes a leader that brakes rather than stops, and the landing closure (type 6), where the rerun on fresh seeds found one encounter in 10,000 in which a vehicle was still inside the zone when it closed (Section 5.6). In the logistics model, at today's planned fleet, the rules added 0.01 h to the mean delay per delivery of 15.5 h, 0.1 %, with an upper bound of 1.4 %, inside the pre-registered 5 % margin and too small for the runs to tell from zero, and the fleet spent 24 h a year waiting at passing bays (Section 5.2). That figure holds for an oracle dispatcher, reservations filed with the job and kept to the second, and vehicles that never follow the lost-link procedure. The cost rose with the fleet: 3.7 % at twice the fleet and demand, 7.9 % at four times, and 41.7 % at eight times (Section 5.3). No run at any fleet size deadlocked. The same rules without a shared table were another matter: owners who referred every conflict to Earth saw delay per delivery rise from 15.5 h to 79.1 h, and their vehicles stood for 1,969 h a year waiting for a decision that could only come while Earth was in view (Section 5.4). A strict reading of the lost-contact rule, in which a robot that cannot reach the base computer files nothing, raised delay 4.2-fold (Section 5.5). Both figures rest on an assumed two hours of staff time per referred conflict, which was not varied. On Mars, with the lunar vehicles on the same roads, the safety program behaved as on the Moon, the dead-stop exception included, but only on the flat: at Mars gravity the lunar brakes hold no downhill steeper than 7.7°, so no sloped encounter could be driven. Coordination that depends on Earth lost about one-sixth of what it loses on the Moon, the opposite of what we pre-registered for Mars (H9, Section 4.1): with Earth in view for half of every sol, as we assumed, a referred conflict waits hours, not the days of the polar base's window gaps (Section 5.8).

The second set of experiments removes four assumptions of the first: that every vehicle knows where it is, that all clocks agree, that every message arrives and that every reported position is true. They are replays of one recorded month; the encounters and the exhaustive check were not run again. Without a navigation satellite service, ranging to four beacons kept the 95th percentile of position error at 4.2 m, inside the 5 m the rules set aside, where two or more beacons were in line of sight. That is 23 % of the driving, and a reading of the registered “within radio range of the base beacons” that we chose after the results; within radio range as registered, which is all of the driving, the error was 50.1 m and the clause failed in every seed. Terrain fixes left 15 m away from the base, so one clause of the positioning hypothesis failed and the verdict on another depends on the reading (Section 5.9). A reservation widened by the vehicle's own 95 % bound, 39 s at each end with the layered estimate, changed delay per delivery by +0.007 h. The stops for terrain fixes that the estimate needs, 37 h a month or 23 % of the fleet's driving time, raised it by 0.63 h (3.5 %) when a follow-up run put them into the logistics model, and that figure is a lower bound. The count of reservation overlaps, which we first read as a safety result, is fixed by the design and is reported only as a count. With a guard of 0.1 s on each reservation a crystal clock resynced within six hours caused no conflict in 303 conflicting pairs; this is an observation outside the guarantee we prove, since clocks out of radio reach were up to 0.84 s off (Section 5.10).

Message custody kept reservation grants from being lost, against a baseline that by its assumed timing sent each grant once. A silent vehicle's segment is not released: it is marked unconfirmed and may be entered only at caution speed, a rule adopted after an internal review and not yet run, and 2,566 of the month's 2,628 reservations ended with their holder unheard (Section 5.12). A network of 9,529 parameters found wrong position reports sooner and more often than the chi-square test on held-out data at the same nominal false-alarm rate; every detector missed at least 37 % of slow drifts; and when the sensors were noisier than in training no detector kept its false-alarm rate, so none is qualified for the safety path on this evidence (Section 5.11). The protocol's own rule, the same test at its theoretical threshold with three of the last five tests flagged, gave 0.10 false alarms per pair-day on the test set without tuning and 69 with the noisier sensors; and trimmed averaging among rovers never left the range of the honest values while its parameter covered the faulty rovers, but converged in at most 10 % of runs on our sparse mesh, so one registered hypothesis about it failed as written. Seven propositions in Appendix A state what follows from the rules by argument alone, each with its assumption, and Section 8 lists every claim as proved, tested, calculated from assumed inputs, assumed or not yet tested.

Our contributions are:

  • A parameterised rule set for lunar and Martian surface traffic among vehicles of different owners, with each parameter traced to a source, a derivation or an assumption to be swept: separation from braking physics, single-lane reservations with an exit bay, landing closures, crew zones, lost-contact behaviour, a priority ladder in which owner identity never counts, and conformance.
  • Three kinds of evidence for the same rules. An exhaustive check of a discrete model against any planner with mutation tests, per-type statistical bounds from simulated encounters, and the delay the rules cost inside a year-long logistics model, all from one implementation of the safety check.
  • A measured price of coordination. The delay cost of the rules at one, two, four and eight times today's fleet, the congestion peak, and the cost of keeping conflicts on Earth instead of settling them on the Moon, on real terrain with the base's real Earth-link windows.
  • The same rules on Mars. Gravity, the Earth round trip and the conjunction changed, nothing else, with the lunar vehicles kept on purpose; every Mars encounter and run is paired with its lunar twin.
  • The rules under imperfect position, time and reports. A margin rule that sizes each reservation from the vehicle's own position bound, a guard for clocks, and custody with a lost-link rule for messages, each set against the simple method it replaces in replays of one month, with the cost of the terrain-fix stops that positioning needs; and a small learned detector of wrong position reports compared with the chi-square test at the same nominal false-alarm rate, with the loss of calibration of every detector out of distribution reported.
  • Seven propositions and a table of what is proved, tested, calculated and assumed. Each guarantee is stated with its assumption and a short proof (Appendix A), including the assumption whose failure explains the one unsafe encounter type and fails again on uphill grades, and every claim of the paper is marked as proved, tested, observed, calculated from assumed inputs, assumed or not yet tested (Section 8).

Several things are outside this paper. Who gets which job, and how robots of different owners share jobs and lift together, is the subject of a companion paper [44]; here every cell uses the same pooled dispatcher, so only the road layer differs between runs. Payments and owners who misreport are the subject of another [45]; here every vehicle obeys the rules, and a wrong position report is treated as a fault to be detected (Section 5.11), not as a strategy. The road graph is the base's route network, not a street plan; single-lane road is an assumed share of it. The rules handle vehicles and crew on foot, not landers in flight beyond the closure window, and not the dust a landing raises. The model checker covers a small model exhaustively and the encounter simulator covers twelve families; neither covers real sensors, and we say so in Section 7.

The paper holds two bodies of work, and we kept them together because the second tests the assumptions of the first. A reader who wants the rules and their price reads Sections 3.1 to 3.8, 4 and 5.1 to 5.8: the rules, the three models, and experiments E43 to E47 with hypotheses H7, H8 and H9 of the first pre-registration [43]. A reader who wants to know what the rules need from navigation, clocks, the radio and the honesty of reports reads Sections 3.9 to 3.12 and 5.9 to 5.12: experiments E50 to E56, replays of one recorded month, with hypotheses H9′, H10′, H11, H14, H15 and H16 of the second pre-registration [59]. The primes mark its H9 and H10, whose numbers repeat those of [43]; H9 without a prime is always the Mars hypothesis. Section 5.13 lists what failed and what has not been run, Sections 6 and 7 discuss and limit both parts, Section 8 sorts every claim by the kind of support it has, and Appendix A holds the seven propositions.

2. Related work

2.1 Fleets on shared roads

Multi-agent path finding gives the planning side of our problem a mature theory. Conflict-based search finds optimal plans for tens of robots [18]; bounded-suboptimal and prioritised planners reach hundreds; priority inheritance with backtracking and the LaCAM family reach thousands of agents on grids in seconds [21, 22]. Prioritised planning writes each robot's plan into a space–time reservation table that later robots must avoid [4, 52]. It is incomplete in general, but complete on “well-formed” layouts in which every robot can wait somewhere that blocks nobody [19, 20], and the priority-inheritance guarantee needs every edge to lie on a cycle, which a dead-end spur or a one-lane corridor between two bays violates [21]. In lifelong operation, where tasks keep arriving, throughput rises with fleet size up to a congestion peak and then falls [6, 17], and planners with short horizons can deadlock [6]. Two results from this literature matter most here. A plan is safe on real robots only if an execution layer enforces its ordering of who passes first; Hönig and colleagues' action dependency graph does that with light communication and arbitrary vehicle dynamics, and in a warehouse simulation its throughput was 2.5 times that of synchronous execution [5]. And the remedy for narrow roads is structural: a vehicle enters a one-lane segment only when its exit is guaranteed, which breaks the circular wait that every deadlock needs [23]. Our rule R3 is that remedy; Section 3.2 states it and Section 5.3 reports what it cost.

Warehouse fleets are the largest deployed case, with a central job manager allocating tasks and space to hundreds of drive units [24]. Autonomous intersection management is the closest to a rule for conflicting owners: a vehicle requests a time–space reservation from an intersection manager, which accepts it only if no tile is double-booked [7]. Dresner and Stone drew two design rules we adopt. The system must be safe under total communication failure, and a vehicle never assumes a reservation it has not had confirmed, so a lost message can only add delay. They also found that a coarse reservation (the whole intersection as one tile) deadlocked in heavy traffic, and that time buffers proportional to speed beat static ones [7]. All of these systems assume one owner or cooperative vehicles, dense grids, no landing zones, no crew on foot, and no proof over an unknown planner.

2.2 Traffic among operators who share no controller

Drone traffic management is the only deployed template for vehicles of many operators sharing space without a single controller. In NASA's concept every operation is filed in advance as a four-dimensional intent, service suppliers that serve different operators check each new intent against all others with first-come-first-served precedence and priority for public safety, and a vehicle that strays from its intent is broadcast to everyone nearby [8]. The European regulation makes the deconfliction rule explicit: in the wording of its recital 20 as EASA's Easy Access Rules reproduce it (we could not fetch the regulation's own text), authorised operations should be “free of intersection in space and time” [10]. NASA's field tests of the federated exchange measured an average message latency of 189 ms, with 95 % of exchanges within 531 ms but a 95th percentile of 3.9 s for negotiation messages, and attributed operational impact to downed servers and misimplemented specifications rather than to latency [9]. The interoperability standard, by its published scope (we did not read its normative text), adds conformance monitoring and constraint management [25]. What transfers to the Moon is the structure: filed intents, federated checking, a priority ladder, published constraints such as a closed landing zone, and conformance monitoring. What does not transfer is the internet; our robots talk over a terrain-limited radio mesh that fails behind ridges, and the Earth link is seconds away at best and absent for days [42].

2.3 Safety layers that override a planner

Every mainstream safety-layer design shares one pattern: an unverified controller proposes, and a small verified component passes the proposal, changes it minimally or switches to a verified backup, so that the system stays in a set of states from which safety can always be kept. Simplex [11, 53] and the aircraft standard built on it [12] give the switching form; control barrier functions give the minimal-change form as a quadratic program, with a multi-robot version run on real robots [26, 27]; shields give the discrete, temporal-logic form, correcting an action only when it would violate the specification [28]; reachability computes the safe set itself [48]. Responsibility-sensitive safety gives closed-form safe distances for cars from response time and bounded accelerations, with a rule that assigns blame to the vehicle that broke its proper response [14]. Verified ground-robot navigation shows which safety notion is achievable with moving obstacles: passive safety, in which the robot is stopped if a collision happens, unless the other agents also obey rules [29]. A tutorial and taxonomy of these filters is given by Hobbs and colleagues [13], and a comparison on spacecraft docking found the implicit filters, which simulate the backup trajectory online, 11–13 times costlier in computation than explicit switching [30]. Our safety program is a post-posed shield in the Simplex form: it holds the vehicle's reservations and the published constraints, accepts the planner's proposal only if every rule still holds, and otherwise substitutes the safe fallback. Because other vehicles obey the same rules, we can claim more than passive safety on the discrete model, and we claim it only there.

These papers measure the cost of the layer in a small set of ways: task performance with and without it on the same scenarios; how often and how much the layer changes the command; conservatism of the safe envelope; computation time; and deadlocks or freezing the layer introduces [13, 30, 6]. We use the first and the last, in cargo delay and deadlock counts on the same seeds, and report the computation in a companion paper [45].

2.4 Proving rules and bounding what simulation cannot prove

Two kinds of evidence answer different questions. Exhaustive verification proves that the rules, as modelled, never allow a violation and never deadlock, on a discretised model; Amazon's experience with TLA+ shows that such models find bugs that testing misses, with error traces 35 steps long [31, 50], and timed-automata and probabilistic checkers have been applied to intersection protocols [32, 46]. Simulation then measures how the implemented system behaves on continuous dynamics, including what the model abstracted away. Statistical model checking makes that second step a bounded claim: it samples runs of a system too large to search exhaustively and states, with a confidence level, a bound on the probability that a property is violated [47]; our per-type bounds are that procedure with a zero-numerator estimator. The statistics of zero violations are simple and often misread: with no events in n independent trials the one-sided 95 % upper bound on the per-trial rate is 1 − 0.051/n, about 3/n [33]; to demonstrate that a fleet is safer than human drivers would take hundreds of millions of failure-free miles [34]; and the bound holds only for the distribution the generator produces, which is why rare encounters are sampled at boosted rates and reweighted [35] and why stratified encounter types are reported separately. Signal temporal logic turns a pass or fail into a signed margin, so a reader can see how close the system came [36]. A credible claim therefore states the property and the model, the reachable states and the counterexamples found during development, the encounter types with their counts and bounds, near misses and minimum margins, the cost of safety on the same seeds, and what is not covered. Section 4 follows that list. One rule from this literature shapes our implementation: the checked code must be the running code, so our model checker, encounter simulator and logistics model import the same safety check.

2.5 The Moon and Mars

Lunar policy gives the hooks and the numbers but not the rules. The Artemis Accords define safety zones as areas where nominal operations or an anomaly “could reasonably cause harmful interference”, sized by accepted engineering principles, temporary, and to be entered only after notification and coordination [1]. NASA's policy analysis states that zones are not exclusion zones, recalls the Apollo heritage boundary of 2 km around landers because 1–10 cm ejecta reached about 1.5 km, reports transit corridors modelled as narrow as 20–40 m, recommends standard standoff distances until plume models improve, and expects central coordination to become necessary with three or more actors [2]. NASA's logistics guidance lists a lander ejecta separation above 1,000 m [38], and crater rims shelter a rover within about 200 m of a lander [37]. The reference vehicles drive at up to 15 km/h on slopes to 20° [16]; the Apollo 16 crew reported stopping from about 10 km/h in roughly twice the distance of the one-g trainer, because braking force scales with gravity [15]. We found no published requirement for the distance an uncrewed vehicle must keep from crew on foot; the closest analogue is the industrial-truck standard, which, as a vendor's summary reports it, limits speed to 0.3 m/s where personnel detection is muted [58]. Surface radio is immature: the only cellular network flown ran for about 25 minutes without completing a call [49], and the three-rover mesh of CADRE has not yet flown: its lander, IM-3, slipped to the first quarter of 2027, by the lander company's statement reported in September 2026 [39, 77]; the LunaNet specification leaves the surface-to-surface link to bilateral agreement [51]. The abstract of a 2025 conference paper raises priority of passage, passing, signalling and crew clearance as regulatory questions and proposes the maritime rules as a model, qualitatively [3]; the full paper was not available to us. Mars adds gravity of 3.71 m/s2, a round trip to Earth of 6–44 minutes and a solar conjunction of about two weeks every 26 months with no contact at all; we found no surface traffic study for either body.

2.6 Position, time and the integrity of reports

Traffic rules assume that a vehicle knows where it is and when, and that it can believe what its neighbours report. At a lunar base none of the three is given. The lunar navigation service of the LunaNet partners is planned to give a 10-metre-class fix, with the map height as an extra measurement, for part of each day from about 2029 [67, 68]; no satellite of it was in orbit when this was written. Until then a rover has dead reckoning, which drifts by a few percent of the distance driven with visual odometry [60] and by more with slip on slopes and loose soil [61]. Absolute fixes against the orbital map by matching craters are designed for 5 m at three standard deviations in sunlight [62] and have been shown in simulation and field tests in darkness with lamps [63]; the method has flight heritage on Mars, where Perseverance has fixed its own position on board since February 2026 [81]. The map itself is located to 0.15 m horizontally [66]. Two-way ultra-wideband ranging between rovers and anchors reached decimetre accuracy in tests on Earth [64, 65], and needs no shared clock, because the clock offset cancels [71]. Clocks matter elsewhere: the crystal of the one GNSS receiver operated on the lunar surface moved by several parts in 107 with temperature [69], a chip-scale atomic clock holds parts in 1010 [70], and lunar and terrestrial seconds differ by 56 µs a day [79].

For a lost link, aviation files the procedure in advance, announces the loss and has everyone else make room [73, 74], and the interface standard for factory vehicles has a disconnected vehicle keep its order and finish it up to the last released node [75], which is our R7 almost word for word. For wrong reports the classical tool is the innovation test of a tracking filter, and for a value that many nodes average it is the trimmed mean, which tolerates F faulty nodes when the communication graph is robust enough [72]. The one multi-rover autonomy system built for lunar flight, CADRE, has a single owner; it plans centrally on an elected leader and replaces a lost leader within 10 s [76]. Among the works we read we found none that puts position error, clock error or a wrong report into a traffic rule and measures what the rule then costs. That statement is limited by what we read. We did not search, and so cannot rule out prior work in, single-track railway signalling with token blocks and passing loops, autonomous haulage on one-lane mine ramps, zone-controlled factory vehicles, required navigation performance and containment in aviation, misbehaviour detection in vehicular networks, receiver autonomous integrity monitoring, or path finding that is robust to delays. These are the nearest fields to Sections 3.9 to 3.12, and the comparison with them remains to be made. The innovation test, the cumulative-sum test, covariance intersection and two-way time transfer are standard methods, which we state and derive without claiming them.

2.7 Our earlier work and the gap

Our first paper built a discrete-event simulation of cargo movement at a south-pole base and a pooled dispatch marketplace [40]. The second added landing precision, pad standoff, a plume keep-out window and rover autonomy on NASA's 5 m terrain of the Connecting Ridge [41]. The third computed the base's Earth-link windows from ephemerides and terrain and priced their gaps [42]. The companion paper of this series moves dispatch from Earth onto the robots over a radio mesh computed from the same terrain and measures what that costs [44]. In all of them vehicles did not interfere with one another on the road: the road layer of this paper is the first time two vehicles in our model have had to decide who waits.

3. Model

Three models share one implementation of the rules. The logistics model is the discrete-event simulation of our earlier papers [40, 41, 42], with a road layer added that makes vehicles wait for one another; it prices the rules in cargo delay over a year. The encounter simulator runs two to four vehicles, a landing and a crew member through continuous kinematics on a small piece of road, through the same safety check, and counts violations. The discrete model is a small abstraction of the same situations, searched exhaustively. This section describes the base and its roads, the rules, the safety program, the two coordination modes, and the two smaller models. Provenance follows the convention of [41]: Measured values come from a cited measurement or document, Spec from a published specification, Derived from a computation on such values, and Assumed values are our choices, each varied in Section 4.2 or named in Section 7.

3.1 Base, terrain and roads

The base is the one of [41, 42, 44], on the Connecting Ridge between Shackleton and de Gerlache craters, on NASA Goddard's 5 m terrain tile from the Lunar Orbiter Laser Altimeter (Measured). Its sites are the habitat and control centre (BASE), a cargo depot, a power plant, a construction site, an oxygen pilot plant, a science site and a permanently shadowed crater; landers use two pads 1 km from the base. Routes between sites are the driving routes of [41], sampled every 100 m along their length: 2,421 samples on 242 km of distinct road, with 17 junction samples where routes meet. Demand, landings and the fleet are those of the companion paper at its middle load [44]: six vehicle classes of 2028–2032 programmes, from 80 kg crew rovers to a 2,000 kg cargo rover, owned by three companies, busy about 30 % of the cargo vehicles' time (load factor 2.806, Assumed, calibrated in [44]). Larger fleets in Section 4.2 multiply every vehicle and the demand by the same factor.

Which parts of these roads are one lane wide is a question the terrain can only partly answer. We measured the drivable corridor at every sample as the width, across the route direction, over which the slope on the 5 m grid stays below a limit, and called a sample single-lane when that corridor is narrower than two vehicle widths plus two lateral margins: 2 × 2.3 m + 2 × 2.7 m = 10 m (Spec for the width of the reference rover [16]; Assumed for the margin). At the rovers' 20° slope limit no sample is single-lane: the median corridor is 101 m wide and the steepest slope on any route is 17.2°. At a 15° limit 0.4 km of road is single-lane, at 10° 36.5 km (15.1 % of samples), and at 8° 96.5 km. The 5 m grid cannot see boulders, crater rims, shadows or a graded one-lane road, all of which narrow a real corridor, and NASA's own corridor modelling found sections 20–40 m wide [2]. We therefore treat the single-lane share as an assumed scenario factor: a share s marks the s × 2,421 samples with the narrowest corridor at the 10° criterion as single-lane, so the places that go first are the real pinch points of the route network, with s = 0.15 by default (Assumed; the 10° value) and s from 0 to 40 % in Section 4.2. The ranked places, with coordinates, are in the repository. Consecutive single-lane samples along any route form a physical segment; passing bays split segments every 1 km (Assumed; 0.5–2 km in Section 4.2). Chargers and the pads' aprons are reserved slots, two per pad (Assumed).

3.2 Rules of the road

Table 2 states the ten rules, their default parameters and the basis of each. They are written so that a vehicle can check every one of them from what it knows locally: its own state, the reservations it holds, the reservations and intents its neighbours have broadcast, and the published constraints. Four principles run through them. A vehicle never assumes a reservation it has not had confirmed, so a lost message can only add delay [7]. A vehicle enters a one-lane segment only with the whole segment reserved for its traversal and its exit guaranteed, so no vehicle ever waits inside a segment and a circular wait cannot form [19, 23]. Priority follows a fixed ladder in which crew on foot and landings come first and the identity of the owner never counts, with ties broken by the order in which the base computer queued the requests and then by vehicle number, so no conflict is left open [8]. And the rules are the same for every owner, which is what lets a safety program on one vehicle reason about what the others will do.

3.3 The safety program

Each vehicle runs a planner and a safety program. The planner may be anything: the hand-built driving logic of [41], a learned policy, or, in the tests of Section 3.5, a generator of random and adversarial proposals. It proposes the next action as a road segment, a speed and a decision to go or wait. The safety program holds the vehicle's reservations, the broadcasts it has heard and the published constraints, checks the proposal against every rule of Table 2 that applies at its level, and either passes it or substitutes the safe fallback: slow to the safe speed, hold at the current safe point, or stop. It is the post-posed shield of [28] in the switching form of [11, 12]: it never plans, it only refuses. One function implements the check, and the logistics model, the encounter simulator and the discrete model all call it, so what is checked is what runs.

3.4 The road layer in the logistics model

The logistics model moves cargo through the base for a simulated year with the pooled dispatcher of [40], which sees every vehicle and every item. The road layer sits between dispatch and driving. When the dispatcher commits a job, the layer walks the vehicle's planned legs over the route samples and applies, in time order, the rules that act at the resolution of a 100 m sample and a job: the one-lane reservations of R3 with the ladder of R4 and R8, the apron slots of R10, the crew slow zone of R6 around the habitat during a scheduled EVA, and the landing closure of R5, which is the plume window of [41] unchanged. A reservation covers the whole segment for the vehicle's traversal window and ends at a bay or junction, so the exit-bay rule holds by construction, no vehicle waits inside a segment, and because the reservation table is filled one job at a time, a circular wait cannot arise. A conflict is a reservation in the opposite direction that overlaps in time; the later request waits at the bay until the segment is free, and the wait extends every later leg of the job. Following vehicles in the same direction are not separated at this resolution: R1 and R2 act within a segment, below the model's grain, and belong to the encounter simulator. Four conditions of this layer bound what its delay figures mean. The dispatcher is an oracle: it sees every vehicle and item, and its assignments arrive at once and are never lost. The windows of every later leg of a job are computed at commit and kept to the second, so the model has no uncertainty in travel time. No vehicle follows the lost-link ladder of Section 3.11; none ever turns back or drives at caution speed. And on the contact plan of the month used in Section 5.12 vehicles have a radio path to the base for little more than half of their driving time.

Two coordination modes run the same rules. In the ATC mode there is one shared reservation table, kept by the base computer and reached over the robots' radio mesh of [44]; a reservation is granted in milliseconds when the robot is linked. By default the reservation travels with the job assignment, which reached the robot, so every job files at commit; in the strict variant of R7 a robot files only from its start site or the first route sample the base's mast can see, and a job that cannot file goes to the second mode's procedure. In the uncoordinated mode each owner keeps its own table and sees only its own vehicles' reservations. A conflict between vehicles of different owners is detected when they meet: both stop at the conflict time and wait until the owners' operators on Earth settle it by hand, which takes thand = 2 h (Assumed) and can only begin while the base sees Earth, with the Earth-link windows of [42]. The other vehicle's stop lengthens its busy time; deliveries it had already recorded are not moved, which understates the uncoordinated mode's cost and is listed in Section 7. A third setting, none, removes the road layer and the landing closure altogether and is the baseline against which the rules are priced.

3.5 The encounter simulator

The encounter simulator runs two to four vehicles on a small local road geometry with continuous two-dimensional kinematics at 10 Hz, with the class speeds of Table 2 and guaranteed braking b = min(bservice, μ g cos θ) − g sin θ, the smaller of the 0.5 m/s2 service brake and the friction limit μ g cos θ, less the downhill term g sin θ, with bservice Assumed and friction μ swept over 0.3, 0.45 and 0.6 (Assumed; the regolith braking coefficient is not published, Section 7). Each vehicle's planner is one of three: the nominal driving logic, a planner that proposes a random action at every step, and an adversarial planner that proposes the action closest to a violation. Every proposal passes through the safety program of Section 3.3. Twelve encounter types (Table 3) are generated with randomised parameters, one encounter per seed; the closest approach between steps is computed exactly from the kinematics, not sampled; rare types are generated at boosted rates and reweighted, with the weights reported. Types 1 and 7 run 100,000 encounters each and the other ten 10,000 (change 6 of [43], a cost of compute on the shared machine that weakens those bounds tenfold); type-1 draws whose slope and friction cannot hold the vehicle (25 % of them) are not driven and are counted apart. They are the 20° draws: the assumed 0.5 m/s2 service brake is smaller than g sin 20° = 0.55 m/s2, so it cannot hold the slope for which the reference vehicle is specified [16], and the assumed brake is at odds with the sourced slope. Slopes are downhill only and only in type 1 (and in the fuzzer's draws of it): types 2 to 11 run on the flat, and none of the twelve types is driven uphill; uphill following was simulated separately, after an internal review (Appendix A.4). A hard violation is one of: contact; a bumper gap to the vehicle ahead on the same road and in the same direction below the static margin d0 = 5 m; any part of a vehicle inside a closed landing zone; a moving vehicle closer to crew than its stopping distance, that is Rstop less its 10 m margin, or touching crew; two opposing vehicles inside one one-lane segment, a vehicle inside a reserved resource without a grant, or more vehicles in a slot resource than it has slots. A gap below R1's dmin while the follower is not braking fully is not a hard violation and is counted apart (Section 5.6). Each type's bound is the 95 % upper limit on the probability of a hard violation under that type's parameter distribution: with no violation 1 − 0.051/neff and, beside it, the Bonferroni limit 1 − (0.05/12)1/neff over the twelve types; with violations the Clopper–Pearson upper limit on the unweighted count at the same levels, with the limit on the weighted violation share at the effective n reported beside it. Types 6, 7 and 9 are importance-sampled (the inside vehicle's exit distance, the crew's emergence time and the dead-stop time) with weights p/q and an effective n of (Σw)2/Σw2; type 12 fuzzes draws of types 1, 2, 4 and 7 and so inherits type 1's undriven slopes and type 7's weights. A near miss is a same-lane gap below twice dmin, a time to collision below 10 s, a crew clearance below twice the stopping radius, or a zone exit margin below 300 s; the minimum margin is the smallest clearance to another vehicle or crew member. Every encounter receives exactly one of six outcomes by precedence (not driven, violation, blocked by a fault, deadlock, finished, incomplete); deadlock is judged only where no random fuzzer runs, and an emergency-brake event (no candidate satisfies every rule and the shield applies full braking) is a rate, not an outcome. The first main run was stopped when two simulator defects showed as violations, the vehicle body swinging off the path at corners and type-6 crossings built without a junction tile; both were fixed, the stored encounters regenerated and all twelve types rerun at the same n (change 8 of [43]); the stopped run's counts are reported in Section 5.6 and its files archived. During the rerun the type-6 cell ran three times: its first pass showed a third defect, start states inside a vehicle's own stopping distance of a crossing tile, and a same-lane test in the gap metric wide enough to count two vehicles leaving a shallow crossing on different roads; its second pass exposed a stall classifier that counted a vehicle correctly held at a closed zone as waiting for a grant. The builder and the classifier were fixed, the metric's same-lane test narrowed to the same road and direction (the shield's own leader test is unchanged), and types 3, 5 and 6 rerun at the same n so that every count is under the one final metric and classifier (change 9 of [43]). The reruns used the same seeds, so the main run's bounds of types 4, 5 and 6 are computed on the encounters on which the defects were found and fixed. After an internal review those three types were therefore run once more on seeds never used before, at the same n, and both sets are reported (Section 5.6). Twenty stored encounters must reproduce exactly on every build.

3.6 The discrete model and its checker

The discrete model is a road graph of 12 nodes with typed edges (two-lane, one-lane) and nodes (junction, bay, parking, pad boundary), including one one-lane segment of four cells with a bay at each end and one junction; two or three vehicles, each with a cell, a heading, a mode (drive, wait, yield, stopped) and its held reservations; one landing with phases open, notice, closed and reopen; one crew member moving on a few cells; and a link state per vehicle. Time is one cell transit of the slowest class. Transitions interleave vehicle moves, crew moves, landing phases and link drops, all nondeterministic, and the planner is fully nondeterministic, so whatever the search proves holds for any planner. Each vehicle move goes through the same safety check as the simulators. The checker is an explicit-state search in the style of [50, 32]: states packed into integers, vehicles of the same class interchanged by sorting, a hard cap of three million states. It checks the safety invariants on every reachable state (no two vehicles in the same or adjacent cells on a segment, no vehicle in a closed zone), checks R6 on each move (no vehicle moves into a crew cell or a cell next to one; crew may walk up to a stopped vehicle, which is why R6 is not a state invariant), and checks deadlock freedom (every reachable non-goal state has an enabled move that is not “all wait”), bounded liveness (from every reachable state a state where all vehicles have reached their goals is reachable under fair scheduling), and livelock (strongly connected components with no exit). A checker that never fails proves nothing, so we remove one rule clause at a time, R3's exit reservation, R5's entry check and R7's hold, and require a counterexample each time. R6 and R10, which the model also holds, were not removed.

3.7 Mars

The Mars variant changes five things and nothing else. Gravity is g = 3.71 m/s2 in the braking and slope terms of R1 and R2. The Earth round trip, 6, 20 and 44 minutes, is added to the two hours of staff time for every conflict referred to Earth, in place of 2.6 s. The Earth windows, which on the Moon are the computed terrain-masked windows of [42], are on Mars assumed to be the first half of every sol (Assumed, Section 7), or the whole sol outside the conjunction and the storms when one relay in Mars-synchronous (areostationary) orbit is added as a variant. A solar-conjunction blackout of about 14 days comes on Mars once in about 26 months, and the simulated year holds one. And two five-day dust storms are modelled as loss of the Earth link only (Assumed), with the robots' radio mesh unchanged. The road geometry is the Connecting Ridge network with Mars parameters, as the controlled case; a second geometry on a Mars site was planned and not run, because the terrain reader is written for the lunar polar tiles and a Jezero base would need its own reader, sites and route network (Section 7).

3.9 Position uncertainty in the margin

Sections 3.1 to 3.8 take every vehicle to know where it is. Without a navigation satellite service it does not, and the rules must hold on what it believes [80]. Dead reckoning from wheel odometry, an inertial unit, a star tracker and visual odometry drifts by 2 % of the distance driven on firm level ground (Measured [60]; a requirement in [62, 63]), 3 % in shadow (Assumed) and 10 % on loose soil or on slopes above 10° (Measured on Earth [61]). Two-way radio ranging to four surveyed beacons at the base and to as many as four other vehicles in line of sight is good to 0.3 m (Measured on Earth [64, 65]) within 100 m and to 3 m beyond that (both ranges Assumed). A terrain fix against the orbital map, taken at a stop of 120 s (Assumed), is good to 1.7 m in sunlight (a requirement of [62]) and to 3 m in the dark, where 30 % of attempts are taken to fail [63]; the lunar navigation service would add a fix of 10 m (95 %) but offers none before 2029 [67, 68]. A filter on each vehicle fuses the four, carries the bias of the current drive leg as a state, takes ranges to beacons as Kalman updates and ranges to other vehicles by covariance intersection, and gives the vehicle a 95 % bound on its own horizontal error, b = 2.45 σmax.

The margins follow from that bound. R1's static margin d0 = 5 m was set aside for position uncertainty; with a bound it becomes d0 + k bAB, where bAB is the 95 % bound of the pair's relative position and k = 1 (Assumed). A one-lane reservation is widened in time: a vehicle with bound b that crosses the segment at speed v files its window earlier by k b/v at the entry and later by k b/v at the exit. Proposition 2 (Appendix A) shows that windows widened in this way stay disjoint in true time whenever both vehicles' errors are inside their bounds and each vehicle passes the ends of the segment at the speed v. An overlap in this part of the paper is counted in true time, which only the simulation knows: each logged window is shifted by the vehicle's along-track error divided by its speed, at the entry and at the exit, and two windows of different vehicles on one segment that conflict under R3 and overlap after the shift are one overlap. Two things limit what that count can show. The shift by error over speed is the premise of Proposition 2, so the count with the margin measures how often the filed bound was exceeded and cannot test whether the margin keeps real vehicles apart. And a waiting vehicle's window is filed to start where the conflicting one ends, so without a margin a pair overlaps whenever the two shifts have the unfavourable order, about half the time, whatever the size of the errors. We therefore report the count as a count and not as a measure of safety. The positions are sampled every 60 s. The separation budget against which position error is judged is d0.

3.10 Clocks and the reservation guard

A reservation is stamped by the clock of the vehicle that holds it. Each vehicle's clock error against the base's time server is followed from its last two-way time transfer, which leaves a residual of 100 ns (Assumed) and corrects the offset but not the frequency. Two oscillator classes are compared: a temperature-compensated crystal whose frequency error is drawn uniformly from 0 to 8 × 10−7, the range the crystal of the LuGRE receiver moved through on the lunar surface (Measured [69]), and a chip-scale atomic clock at 3 × 10−10 with ageing of 9 × 10−10 a month (Spec [70]; the part is not space-rated). A transfer succeeds only while the vehicle has a radio path to the base mast, directly or through other vehicles. Each reservation carries a guard g at both ends, 0.1 s by default (Assumed), and a conflict caused by clocks alone is a pair of conflicting reservations whose clock errors close their stamped gap by more than 2g (Proposition 6). Ranging between vehicles is two-way, so the clock offset cancels and only the difference of the two clock rates over the 1 ms reply delay remains [71].

3.11 Lost link and message custody

R7 says what a vehicle out of contact does. The protocol adds a ladder of states, filed in advance as the lost-link procedures of aviation are [73, 74] and close to the rule of factory vehicles that a disconnected robot finishes its order up to the last released node [75]. A vehicle that has not heard the base for 5 s files no new reservation and keeps those it holds; after 30 s, or with no neighbour in reach, it finishes its reserved segment at reduced speed and stops at the next safe point, never inside a one-lane segment, a landing zone or a crew zone; then it holds, returns along its own path and, at last, waits in a safe state (times Assumed [80]). A silent holder's reservation is not released. The base and the neighbours keep its last reserved volume blocked until it is heard again; when the reservation's timeout and a grace period have passed without word, the segment is marked unconfirmed. Another vehicle may then enter it only at caution speed, the speed from which it can stop within its own perception range (Proposition 3), and reports what it finds. The reason is that a vehicle may be silent because it has failed inside the segment, and a rule that frees the segment would then grant it to an oncoming vehicle. This is the rule of the companion papers too [44, 45]; it replaces the release at the timeout that the first draft of this paper stated, was adopted after an internal review, and has not been run in any model here. Drop and retry sends a message end to end, tries again every 180 s and gives up after 5 tries or at the message's lifetime (Assumed). Store and forward passes custody hop by hop: the holder sends again after 10 s while the link lasts and keeps the message while there is no path. A reservation grant lives 60 s and a job award for its lease of 1 h (Assumed [80]); because the retry interval is longer than a grant's lifetime, drop and retry sends each grant once. In the replay a vehicle that has heard nothing for 600 s retraces its path at half speed to the last point where it had a link. The replay was run with the rule as first written, in which the base released a silent holder's reservation at its timeout; what it counts, the reservations whose holder was unheard 60 s after the window ended, are under the rule as stated above the segments marked unconfirmed.

3.12 Integrity of position reports

R9 lets neighbours act on the position a vehicle reports, and a wrong report, from a fault or a false claim, defeats that. The setting is one observer and one reporter: vehicle A hears B's position report once a second and measures the range to B by two-way ranging, and from the last two minutes must say whether B's reports are wrong. A's own position estimate in this experiment is simpler than the filter of Section 3.9: its error grows by 2 % of the distance driven and collapses to 1.7 m at a terrain fix, taken at every stop and about every 100 m of driving, with no filter and no terrain; the range is good to 0.3 m within 100 m (the short-range class) and to 3 m beyond (the long-range class). Five fault types are put into B's reports (sizes Assumed): a jump of 5 to 50 m, a slow drift of 0.01 to 0.5 m/s, a frozen report, a frame error of 300 to 1,000 m and report noise inflated 3 to 10 times. Fault-free data keep the honest disturbances: stops and starts, navigation error that grows and then collapses at a fix, dropouts beyond radio range, the change of ranging class at 100 m and bursts of blocked-path bias. True motion comes from 17 simulated months of the logistics model, one per seed, rebuilt at 1 Hz. The data are split by month and by vehicle pair, so that no month and no pair is in two of the training, validation and test sets; every vehicle is in all three, because a pair is assigned by the difference of its two vehicle numbers: months 1 to 6 with 28 ordered pairs for training (3,600 fault streams), months 7 to 9 with 16 pairs for validation (1,020 fault streams and 600 fault-free hours) and months 10 to 13 with 12 pairs for the test (2,000 fault streams, 400 per type, and 575 fault-free hours). An out-of-distribution set, months 14 to 17 on the test pairs, has ranging noise multiplied by 2 and dead reckoning drifting 5 % while A's filter still assumes the nominal values, with jumps of 2 to 5 m and drifts of 0.005 to 0.01 m/s, below the training range. It is one shift, chosen by us, and not a sample of the ways a sensor model can be wrong.

The classical test is the squared range innovation divided by its predicted variance, compared with a chi-square threshold (Proposition 5), per step and as a mean over 5 s. The other baselines are a two-sided CUSUM on the innovation and a logistic regression on 28 hand-made statistics of the last 16 and 128 s. The learned detector is a causal convolutional network: six dilated one-dimensional convolutions of 24 channels over the last 127 s of ten inputs that A can know, 9,529 parameters in all, 38 kB as 32-bit floats. It is small for three reasons. It has to run beside the safety program on a flight computer: one window takes 1.27 ms on one thread of a desktop processor, and a radiation-tolerant processor is slower by a factor we did not measure. A network of this size can be shipped as a table of weights and replayed exactly. And the search rule, fixed before the first training run, kept the smallest configuration within 0.005 of the best validation recall: the wider network tried, 16,545 parameters, gained 0.0049. In the protocol the per-step test is followed by a persistence rule, three flagged tests of the last five, and shared quantities that many vehicles average use a trimmed mean that discards the F largest and F smallest values a vehicle hears (Propositions 5 and 7).

4. Experiment design

4.1 Questions and hypotheses

The pre-registration [43] states three hypotheses for this paper. H7 (safety). Zero violations in each of the twelve encounter types at 105 encounters per type, with the bound 1 − 0.051/n reported per type, and a counterexample from the checker for each rule removed. Change 6 of [43], made after the development runs of the simulator and before any main E44 row, cut ten of the twelve types to 104 encounters for compute and kept types 1 and 7 at 105 (Section 3.5); the bound per type is unchanged and is weaker by a factor of ten at the smaller sample. H8 (cost of safety). At today's fleet the rules add less than 5 % to the delay per delivery; at four to eight times the fleet the cost rises. We treat the first clause as confirmatory and report the second; the label is this paper's, since the confirmatory family of [43] holds only hypotheses of the companion paper [44]. H9 (Mars, exploratory). The same rules keep zero violations under Mars gravity and braking; the delay cost of the rules is similar to the Moon's; and coordination that depends on Earth loses more on Mars than on the Moon because of the round trips and the conjunction. H7 and H8 were written before any run; H9 was added with the Mars part on 27 October 2026, before any run, when the three papers were regrouped (change 1 of [43]).

A second pre-registration, written on 11 November 2026 before any of the later results existed [59], states six hypotheses that this paper reports. Its numbering restarts at H9, so two of its labels repeat labels of [43]; we write those two with a prime, here and in the companion papers, and keep H9 for the Mars hypothesis. H9′ (positioning). With dead reckoning alone the 95th percentile of position error exceeds the separation budget within one 8-hour shift of driving; with ranging it stays inside the budget within radio range of the base beacons; with absolute fixes it stays inside the budget away from the base. H10′ (margin). Delay per delivery with the widened margin and the layered scheme is within 1.5 times the delay with perfect position at today's fleet, and the gap grows at four and eight times; with dead reckoning alone and the widened margin the delay is larger, by an interval that excludes zero. H11 (time). For the cheaper oscillator a resync interval exists at which clock error causes no reservation conflict while it still adds more than the ranging radio's own noise to ranging. H14 (lost link). At a message loss of 0.25 and with a 14-day Earth blackout, store and forward with the lost-link rule loses fewer jobs than drop and retry and leaves no reservation stuck past its timeout. H15 (integrity). At one false alarm per pair-day, (a) on slow drifts the network's median detection delay is shorter than the per-step chi-square test's; (b) on jumps the two are within one second; (c) against the CUSUM the comparison has no predicted direction; (d) out of distribution the network's recall may fall below the CUSUM's, reported whichever way it comes out. H16 (agreement). With f faulty rovers the trimmed-mean rule keeps the agreed value inside the range of the honest rovers' values whenever the mesh is (2f+1)-robust, in every run; when it is not, at least one run leaves the range. H9′, H10′, H14 and H15 (a) are confirmatory and the others exploratory. The remaining hypotheses of [59], on the shared map and the message set, belong to the companion papers [44, 45].

4.3 Seeds and common random numbers

Every cell of E43 uses the same seeds, so a seed's demand, landings and faults are identical across modes and the difference between two modes on one seed is the effect of the road layer alone. The engine's phase-5 check D10 (Section 3.8) confirms the pairing on the companion paper's regimes: the demand stream, landings and manifests, was identical across 880 regime pairs on the same seed and start year, while the per-job intervention and fault redraws, shared in draw order, diverge once the allocations differ; E43's modes draw from the same streams, so a pair shares its year and diverges in those draws after the first conflict. Pairs are formed on the seed; because the none and ATC runs do not depend on the start year, an uncoordinated run of 2038 pairs with the none and ATC runs of the same seed. Encounters use one seed per encounter, so each is an independent trial. No run is excluded after the fact; a run that crashes is fixed and repeated with the same seed, and the crash is reported.

4.4 Outcomes

The primary outcome of E43 is the mean delay per delivered item, in hours from the item's readiness to its delivery, the measure of [40]. Secondary outcomes are the 90th percentile of that delay, the mass-weighted delay, the censored mean wait of urgent items of [44], items delivered, operator hours, and the road layer's own counts: hours waited at bays, conflicts, conflicts referred to Earth and the vehicle-hours spent waiting for Earth, hours other vehicles stood because of a stopped vehicle, deadlocks, and bay wait per owner. The primary outcome of E44 is the count of violations per type, with near misses and the minimum separation margin; of E45, the reachable state count and the properties proved. For E50 the outcomes are the position error while driving (median and 95th percentile, near the base and away from it), the share of driving steps in which the vehicle's own 95 % bound exceeds the budget, the true-time overlaps of conflicting reservations with and without the margin, and the delay per delivery against perfect position. For E51 they are the range error that clock error adds and the reservation conflicts it causes alone; for E54, jobs lost, reservation grants lost and reservations left blocked. For E56 and E55 the primary outcomes are event-level recall and the median detection delay per fault type at a fixed false-alarm rate of one per pair-day on fault-free data; accuracy over all seconds is not reported, because faults are rare. For the agreement part of E55 they are the share of runs in which every honest value stays inside the first honest range and the share that converge.

4.5 Statistics

Differences between modes are paired means over seeds with a percentile bootstrap 95 % confidence interval of 20,000 resamples [55]; the ratio of a difference to the baseline's mean carries its own bootstrap interval. H8's first clause is supported if the upper end of the two-sided 95 % bootstrap interval of the ratio (ATC minus none, over the mean of none) at one times the fleet lies below 0.05. That is this paper's reading of the registered “add less than 5 %” [43]; the one-sided 95 % bound gives the same verdict, which the build script checks. It is a margin test in the spirit of equivalence testing [56]: as in Schuirmann's two one-sided tests, the claim is that the cost lies within a stated margin, not that it is zero, so the end of the interval nearest the margin decides. The density ladder, the uncoordinated mode and the sweeps are reported with intervals and not tested. Deadlocks are counted as a check of the implementation: the road layer cannot deadlock by construction (Section 3.4), so a non-zero count would be a defect in the code, not a property of the rules, and the count says nothing about finite bays, which only the encounter simulator and the checker have. For E44 the per-type bound with zero violations is 1 − 0.051/n [33], and the joint statement over the twelve types uses α = 0.05/12 per type, which raises each bound from about 3/n to about 5.5/n; with any violation the Clopper–Pearson interval replaces it [57]. All intervals are descriptive of the simulated population of seeds, not of a lunar fleet.

The experiments of [59] use the same bootstrap. H9′ is judged per clause by the side of the budget on which the 95th percentile falls, and H10′'s first clause by two one-sided tests with a margin of 50 % of the perfect-position delay, read from the 90 % interval of the paired difference [56]. In E56 the intervals are over events (1,000 resamples) and, for the network, the numbers are means over five training seeds; H15 (b) is two one-sided tests with a margin of 1 s. [59] names a family of confirmatory hypotheses, H9′, H10′, H13, H14 and H15 (a), for a Holm correction at α = 0.05. H9′ and H13 are comparisons with a threshold and have no sampling test; they are reported by the share of seeds or draws on the predicted side, and H13 belongs to the companion papers. The correction is applied over the three that have a test: H10′ by paired two one-sided tests at today's fleet, joined with its other two clauses by intersection and union, so that its p value is the largest of the three; H14 by an exact sign test on the lost jobs per seed; and H15 (a) by the bootstrap over events, whose p value is an upper bound when no resample reaches zero. These p values are not registered: [59] gives its decision rules by interval, and the three tests were constructed after the results so that the correction could be applied. The sign test is one-sided (two-sided p = 0.0039), and the registered rule for H14, a paired interval that excludes zero, gives the same verdict. Over the registered family of five, counting the two hypotheses that have no test, the adjusted values would be at most 0.005 for H15 (a) and 0.008 for H14, and no verdict changes.

4.6 Choices made after looking at results

Three choices were made after looking at data, all before the runs they affect, and all are recorded in [43] and in the design file of E43. First, the single-lane share became an assumed scenario factor after the corridor analysis of Section 3.1 showed no one-lane road at the rovers' slope limit; the default and the sweep were fixed before the first E43 run. Second, the horizon of the eight-times cells: it was planned at 60 days on ten seeds, cut to 30 days when the first 60-day run took 310 s, over the per-run limit of Section 4.7, abandoned when a 30-day run went past 470 s, and restored to 60 days once the defect below was fixed; no 30-day row was ever used, and every eight-times row in this paper is a 60-day row. Third, a defect in the engine's event handling, found while timing those cells: the dispatcher's consolidation hold re-posted a wake-up for every held lane on every call, so the event queue grew geometrically with fleet size (the cause of the 470 s run above) and, because one dispatch call does not reach a fixed point, the duplicates also changed assignments. The fix, pre-registered as change 5 of [43] before any rerun row, posts one wake-up per lane and lot and repeats dispatch at the same instant until nothing changes. Every cell of E43 was then rerun; the 610 earlier rows are archived and reproducible with the fix off. Against the 610 archived rows with the same cell and seed, the primary outcome moved by −0.13 h on average and by 3.8 h in absolute value, with a largest single-run change of 43 h (in the uncoordinated mode, whose hand-resolution waits depend on the order of events); 69 % of the rows moved by more than 0.1 h. The mean run time fell from 14 to 8 s and the longest run from 394 to 32 s. Table 4 and Table 5 hold only rerun rows; the before/after table per cell is results/E43_fixpoint_before_after.csv.

For the later experiments, [59] records changes dated after first results, and each stays visible in the text. The seed counts of E50 were set after one timing cell, on run time alone, and its separation budget was fixed as d0. The fleet at which H10′'s third clause is tested was fixed after the results at today's fleet and five of the ten seeds at four times had been seen. E50's delay was found to leave out the time a vehicle stands for a terrain fix, about 37 h a month for today's fleet; a follow-up run (E50b) puts the stops into the logistics model, and E50's numbers stay as run. And H16 is recorded as not supported as written, with the reason given in Section 5.11. After an internal review of the first draft, [59] records five more points: the second clause of H9′ is reported under each reading of “near the base” with the verdict “depends on the reading”; the second clause of H14 is not testable as written, and the lost-link rule was changed (Section 3.11); two hypotheses of the companion papers are calculations and not tests; the sign test for H14 was added after the results; and the primes on H9′ and H10′. Four analysis choices were made with or after the E50 results and are not in the registered text. “Near the base” was taken as line of sight to two or more of the four beacons. The bound a vehicle files with was taken as its hourly maximum. The position filter was changed after the first E50 cell so that a parked vehicle ranges once and not at every epoch, because repeated ranges had made its bound too small. And [59] says that a parameter without an opened source is assumed and swept, while only the nominal values were run, apart from the guard and one out-of-distribution set (Section 7): that is a deviation from the registration. Two further things were done after results and are not in [59]: the variants of E51 named in Section 4.2, and a correction to the atomic-clock model, whose ageing had restarted at every resync instead of accumulating over the month. The corrected rows are the ones reported; the crystal rows and the verdict of H11 did not change.

4.7 Compute

Runs are single-process on one laptop shared with other work, in chunks of at most nine minutes with a per-run limit of five minutes. A year at one times the fleet took 5 s per run, at two times 12 s; the 90-day runs at four times took 8 s; the 60-day runs at eight times took 14 s. E43 took about 1.5 h of processor time. E44's final run took 9.4 h of processor time in resumable batches of 250 to 4,000 encounters, type 11 the longest at 2.7 h, with a process peak of 136 MB. E45's four layouts and three mutations took 50 s in all, the largest layout 37 s and 29 MB.

5.3 Larger fleets

Fleet and demand were multiplied together, so a larger fleet is also a busier road network. At two times the fleet the rules added 0.29 h [0.21, 0.39 h] to a baseline delay of 8.0 h, 3.7 % (upper bound 4.9 %), with 847 conflicts and 288 h of bay wait a year. At four times, over 90 days, the rules added 0.40 h [0.26, 0.50 h] to a baseline of 5.0 h, 7.9 % (upper bound 10.2 %), with 999 conflicts and 384 h of bay wait in the period. At eight times, over 60 days, the rules added 1.56 h [1.17, 1.92 h], 41.7 % (upper bound 53.9 %). The baseline delay itself fell as the fleet grew. 5.4 Owners who keep their own tables

In the uncoordinated mode, with the same rules but each owner seeing only its own reservations and every cross-owner conflict settled from Earth, the mean delay per delivery at today's fleet was 79.1 h in the 2028 start year and 98.5 h in 2038, against 15.5 h without the road layer: differences of +63.5 h [58.4, 68.7 h] and +82.9 h [76.0, 89.7 h], that is 5.1 and 6.3 times the baseline. Vehicles met 53 cross-owner conflicts a year in 2028, and the fleet's vehicles stood for 1,969 h a year in all waiting for a decision from Earth, plus 1,961 h in which the other vehicle of each conflict stood. That is 37.2 h of waiting per conflict against two hours of staff time, so the cost is set by the gaps between Earth windows; the staff time is assumed and was not swept, and the mode has no local fallback. In 2038 vehicles met 45 conflicts and stood for 2,527 h waiting for Earth: the poorer year cost more, in delay and in waiting, at today's fleet. Against the ATC mode on the same seeds the differences were +63.5 h [58.4, 68.9 h] and +82.9 h [76.0, 89.7 h]. At two times the fleet the uncoordinated delay was 8.1 times the baseline in 2028 and 10.3 in 2038, with 5,020 h of waiting for Earth in 2028; at four times it was 12.6 and 12.8 times the baseline, and at eight times 20.2 and 18.1 times.

5.5 Single-lane share, bay spacing and the lost-contact rule

With no single-lane road at all, the ATC mode's delay differed from the baseline by −0.03 h and the uncoordinated mode's by +0.1 h, the latter with 1.2 cross-owner conflicts a year. At a 5 % share the ATC mode differed from the baseline by +0.03 h and the uncoordinated mode by +63.7 h (5.1 times); at 40 %, with 96.8 km of one-lane road, the ATC mode differed by +0.28 h [−0.03, 0.58 h], +1.8 %, with 43 h of bay wait, and the uncoordinated mode by +79.7 h (6.1 times). Bays every 0.5 km instead of 1 km changed the ATC mode's delay by +0.06 h [−0.16, 0.27 h] and raised the segment traversals from 1,843 to 4,834; bays every 2 km changed it by 0.00 h, identically by construction, because at the default share no one-lane segment is longer than 1 km and the 2 km spacing places no bay. Under the strict reading of R7, in which a robot files a reservation only from a place the base's mast can see, the ATC mode's delay rose by 49.1 h [43.8, 54.8 h] over the default ATC mode on all 30 seeds, with 725 jobs a year that could not file, 37 conflicts referred to Earth, and vehicles standing 1,551 h a year in all waiting for it.

5.7 The exhaustive check

The four layouts have 47,085 reachable states and 272,899 transitions in all; the largest, a pad visit with two oncoming vehicles and radio links, has 36,912 states. In every layout the search found no state that breaks a safety invariant, no deadlock state, no state from which the goal is unreachable and no livelock component; the longest shortest path to the goal is 41 steps of 8.5 s on 10 m cells. Each mutation produced its counterexample. Without R3's exit-slot check, 2 reachable states of the three-vehicle line are deadlocks and 39 cannot reach the goal (13 livelock components); the shortest deadlock trace is 12 steps. Without R5's entry check, 384 reachable states have a vehicle inside the landing zone while it is closed, the shortest reached in 14 steps. Without R7's hold, 198 reachable states break an invariant (138 in the segment without the reservation, 96 opposite directions in the segment; a state may break both), the shortest in 5 steps. The traces are stored beside the results. H7's second clause as registered asks for a counterexample for each rule removed [43]. It holds for the three rule clauses that were removed, R3's exit slot, R5's entry check and R7's hold; R6 and R10 are also in the discrete model and were not mutated, so the clause is met in part. Development of the checker found four counterexamples before the final run, three in the checker itself (R6 checked as a state invariant rather than on the vehicle's motion, which flagged crew walking up to a stopped vehicle; no crew distance computed on the landing spur, 80 violating states in the first layout; bay slots counted per lane, which hid the R3 mutation) and one in the reading of a rule: with the bays fixed, the three-vehicle line deadlocked when a vehicle granted the segment with a free exit slot lost that slot to a vehicle that drove into the bay afterwards. R3's “reserved for it” therefore binds every other vehicle: a bay counts the slots reserved by segment holders as taken, in the checker and in the encounter simulator alike.

5.12 The lost link and reservation grants (H14)

Over the 30 seeds the month held 6,028 reservation grants and 6,028 job awards. At a loss of 0.25 per hop, drop and retry lost 1,543 grants, 26 %; with custody 2 were lost, 0.03 %. A lost grant is a vehicle stopped before a one-lane entry, because an unconfirmed reservation is never assumed. At losses of 0.10 and 0.40 drop and retry lost 9 % and 40 %. These shares are the per-hop loss itself and are a calculation on two assumed inputs, not a measurement: the retry interval (180 s) is longer than a grant's lifetime (60 s), so each grant was sent once, with a mean delay of 0.01 s. Three tries inside the lifetime would by the same arithmetic lose 0.253, 1.6 %; that baseline was not run, so the replay does not show that custody is needed, only that a grant sent once is lost as often as a hop fails. Lost jobs, awards that expired before they arrived, were 0.33 a seed [0.17, 0.53] with drop and retry and none with custody and the lost-link rule, a paired difference of −0.33 [−0.53, −0.17]. H14 [59] has two clauses. The first, fewer lost jobs by an interval that excludes zero, is supported. The second, no reservation left stuck past its timeout, is not testable as written and is withdrawn as a result [59]: the zero followed from the rule's definition, and the rule as first written released a silent vehicle's reservation, which is unsafe when the vehicle is silent because it has failed inside the segment. Under the rule as it now stands (Section 3.11) the reservation is not released and the segment is marked unconfirmed. What the replay gives is how many those are: 2,566 of the 2,628 reservations of the month had a holder with no radio path to the base 60 s after the window ended, 7,349 h of unconfirmed segment in all, although in the replay every one of those vehicles had left its segment on schedule. An unconfirmed segment is the usual case, then, and the delay that entering it at caution speed costs has not been priced. Nor has the rest of the ladder. Along the month's contact plan vehicles had a radio path to the base mast in 56.6 % of their driving time; the replay holds 82 lost-link returns a seed against 199 jobs, and credits 336 h a seed as spent waiting, linked, at the return point; but it applies the return to the contact plan only, and the recorded job still completes. No logistics run of this paper contains the ladder (Section 7). The base's knowledge of a vehicle was 45 s old on average with the rule (worst vehicle 149 s) and 88 min without it (worst 31 h), in a replay in which a returning vehicle is given a direct link. By an exact sign test, custody with the lost-link rule lost fewer jobs in 9 seeds, more in none and the same number in 21 (one-sided p 0.002; Holm-adjusted 0.004), so the first clause stands in the confirmatory family; the test was added after the results (Section 4.5).

6. Discussion

6.1 What the results say about the question

The question had three parts: whether a small shared rule set keeps robots of several owners safe against any planner, what it costs, and whether it holds on Mars. On cost the answer is clear under the conditions of the model. At today's planned fleet the rules cost 0.1 % of delay per delivery, with an upper bound of 1.4 %, an amount the runs cannot tell from zero, and the whole fleet lost 24 h a year at passing bays. The conditions are those of Section 3.4: an oracle dispatcher, reservations filed with the job and kept to the second, no uncertainty in travel time, and no vehicle on the lost-link procedure. That is because the base's roads are long and its vehicles few: at today's fleet the whole fleet meets an oncoming vehicle on a one-lane segment about 1.4 times a week (73 conflicts a year), and a wait at a bay is minutes against deliveries measured in hours. The cost grows with the fleet, to 7.9 % at four times the fleet and demand, which is the congestion the warehouse literature predicts [6, 17], and the price of safety at eight times is 41.7 % of delay per delivery. The baseline delay itself fell as the fleet grew, because the pooled dispatcher had more vehicles to choose from for each item, so the rules' cost is a share of a smaller number at the larger fleets. The rules as implemented in the logistics model never deadlocked, in 650 runs at up to eight times the fleet; that is the exit-bay construction of R3 doing what the theory says it does [19, 23]. That zero is a property of the logistics model rather than of the rule: its bays are unbounded and its reservation table is filled one job at a time (Section 3.4), so no circular wait can form there, and the count is a check of the code. With finite bays the exit-bay rule is not deadlock-free on a ring of single-lane segments, as the encounter simulator's ring type shows (Section 5.6), and whether this base has such a ring the 5 m route samples cannot settle: merged to road nodes within 40 m they show one candidate ring of 419 m at the default share, one at a 5 % share and 14 at 40 %, but the merge can itself join neighbouring roads, and before merging the 5 loops at the default share are one road sampled twice by different legs. A surveyed centreline map would settle it; the samples do not.

The second number depends on two assumptions that we state with it: two hours of staff time per conflict, which was not varied, and no local fallback at all. The same rules, with each owner keeping its own reservation table and every cross-owner conflict settled by hand from Earth, cost 5.1 times the baseline delay at today's fleet, with its vehicles standing 1,969 h a year in all waiting for a decision that could only be made while the base saw Earth, 37.2 h per conflict. The gaps between Earth windows, not the staff time, make up that wait. The rules did not change between the two modes; the place of the decision did. A conflict on a one-lane road is settled in milliseconds by a shared table on the Moon and in hours by two control rooms on Earth, and for about eleven days in every month it is not settled at all [42]. The strict reading of the lost-contact rule tells the same story from the other side: a robot that may not file a reservation until the base's mast sees it behaves like an uncoordinated one for the first leg of every job, and under that reading the ATC mode's delay per delivery differed from the default's by 49.1 h. The rule that matters most for cost is therefore not any of the ten in Table 2 but the one about who may grant a reservation.

On safety, the exhaustive check says that the rules are consistent on every layout the checker can hold, 47,085 states of up to three vehicles on one segment with its bays, a pad and a crew member, and that each of the three clauses we removed is load-bearing: without it the search finds an unsafe or a stuck state within a few steps. It says nothing about larger layouts, which is where the simulator takes over. The failure of type 9 is where the derivation says it should be. R1's gap is computed for a leader that brakes at no more than bmax, and a vehicle that stops dead, a fault rather than a manoeuvre, lies outside that model [14], so a same-direction follower already inside a one-lane segment can come inside the 5 m static margin and, in 7 of 10,000 encounters, touch. A dead-stop margin, the follower's own stopping distance at its current speed, would close the case at a cost in segment capacity; we did not run it, and the count stands as a limit of braking-based separation. The twelve types could not have found the other case the derivation names, a conforming leader that brakes on an uphill grade and so exceeds bmax, because every one of their encounters is on the flat or downhill. A separate simulation of uphill following found it (Appendix A.4): under R1 as it was, no contact, but followers inside the static margin from 14° on the Moon and 6.5° at Mars gravity when the follower's actuation lags by a second. A slope term in R1 removed every such case when following and left a remainder of at most 0.14 m in the Mars one-lane convoy, which comes from the lag model. We therefore state R1 with the slope term as the proposed rule (Table 2); the twelve types were run without it, on ground where it is inactive. The ring deadlocks of type 5 (0.7 % of the judged encounters under R3 as written, 19.0 % under the keep-one-slot-free variant) are the property noted before the main run [43]: the exit-bay rule is not deadlock-free on a ring of one-lane segments with finite bays, the variant that was meant to help makes it worse, and the logistics model's zero count comes from its unbounded bays; the pooled count stood when the stall classifier was corrected (change 9 of [43]). The landing closure is the second type with a hard violation, found only when it was run on fresh seeds: in 1 of 10,000 encounters a vehicle held at the zone boundary kept its grant for a one-lane resource beyond the zone, and a vehicle inside the zone waited for that resource until after the closure. R5 and R3 are each obeyed and together hold a vehicle where it must not be. The request rule of change 9 of [43] removed the violation in that one encounter, which is all it was run on; whether it holds on the other fresh encounters is not known. The main run, on the seeds used to fix the simulator, had not shown the case, which is what the fresh seeds were for. The four deadlocks of type 6 are a gap in the rules: a crossing that lies on a landing zone's boundary can be held by a vehicle stopped at the zone line, and the vehicle leaving the closing zone through it is blocked. R3 keeps a vehicle out of a segment it cannot leave into a bay, but no rule keeps it out of a junction whose far side is a hold point. The repair is a one-line extension of R3's exit check to junctions; we did not run it, and the count stands as found. On Mars, the safety program behaved as on the Moon, the dead-stop exception included, but that is weaker evidence than it looks: the only ground the lunar brakes hold at Mars gravity is nearly flat, and on the flat the braking term is the same on both bodies, so the Mars encounters that were driven repeat the lunar ones; the rules are untested on the slopes where Mars gravity acts. The same holds for the rules' cost, measured only by the paired encounter delays. The Earth-dependent mode lost about one-sixth of what it loses on the Moon, 11.1 h against 63.5 h, the opposite of H9's third clause. The reason is in the Earth windows, not the round trip: the three round trips gave the same cost within their intervals, and the relay lowered the mean at each, while a Mars base, as we modelled it, sees Earth for half of every sol (Assumed, Section 7) and the polar base's computed windows [42] leave it blind for days, so a referred conflict waited 8.2 h for a decision on Mars and 37.2 h on the Moon. The reversal therefore holds for any Mars site whose horizon returns Earth every sol, and would not for one that does not. The Mars fleet referred more conflicts (94 against 53) for the same demand and almost the same deliveries (2,932 against 2,928 items); we have not traced the cause. The one Mars-specific finding is about hardware. The guaranteed braking of Section 3.5 is the smaller of the service brake and the friction limit, less the downhill component: on the Moon the friction term binds at μ = 0.3 and the 0.5 m/s2 service brake above it, so the holdable downhill is 16.7–18.0°; on Mars the service brake binds at every friction value, so braking does not grow with gravity while the slope term does, and the holdable downhill falls to 7.7°. Of the following draws, 75 % were on steeper ground, against 25 % on the Moon. R2 gives a zero admissible speed on such a slope, so the safety program refuses the slope; it has no rule that says a vehicle is under-braked for the body it is on, and vehicles for Mars need a service brake sized for Mars gravity.

The later experiments asked what the rules need from navigation, from clocks and from the honesty of reports. On position the answer divides at the edge of the beacons' coverage. Where two or more beacons are in line of sight, 23 % of the driving, two-way ranging keeps a vehicle within the 5 m that R1 already sets aside. That is a reading we chose after the results. Under the registered wording, within radio range of the base beacons, the error is 50.1 m and the second clause of H9′ fails in every seed; with one or more beacons in sight it is 12.5 m and within 1 km of the mast 24.7 m. Ranging to four beacons at the base, then, holds the margin only where two of them are seen at once. Elsewhere terrain fixes leave a 95th-percentile error three times the margin (Table 9); the weak places are the dark legs, where fix opportunities are 500 m apart and 30 % of fixes fail, and the loose slopes, where dead reckoning drifts 10 %. What a margin sized by each vehicle's own bound buys in safety we cannot yet say. Proposition 2 guarantees disjoint windows while each error stays inside its bound and the vehicle passes the ends of the segment at speed, and E50 counted how often the filed bound was exceeded (Table 10). The count of overlaps without a margin is fixed by the way back-to-back windows are filed and measures no hazard: no overlap, with or without the margin, brought two vehicles within the static margin, and the first vehicle was about 440 m ahead when the second entered. The margin removes overlapping windows from the reservation accounts; it does not move vehicles further apart. What the margin costs is clearer. With the layered estimate it is 39 s at each end and the widened windows cost 0.007 h per delivery at today's fleet; with dead reckoning alone it is 25 min at today's fleet, where its cost of 0.12 h was not resolved, and 27 min at four times the fleet, where it cost 2.1 h per delivery. The layered estimate, though, depends on stops for terrain fixes, 37 h a month, and with those in the logistics model the delay per delivery rose by 0.63 h, 3.5 %, a lower bound because the model inserted only part of the stop time (Section 5.9). The cost of positioning without satellites is the stops, not the margins. Two of the three clauses of H10′ were not supported, and the experiment resolved neither: at four times the fleet ten seeds do not settle the layered margin's difference, and at today's fleet the dead-reckoning margin cost more in 24 of 30 seeds while its mean could not be told from zero. The pre-registration said that a failure of H10′ would mean that position error sets traffic capacity [59]; these runs neither show that nor exclude it.

On time, clock quality matters for one-way ranging long before it matters on the roads, and the protocol ranges two-way. The guard of 0.1 s is less than a hundredth of the position margin. With it a crystal resynced within six hours caused no conflict in 303 pairs, but that is an observation and not the guarantee: Proposition 6 needs every clock within the guard, clocks out of radio reach were up to 0.84 s off, and the worst pair used 62 % of the two guards between its windows. A design that wants the guarantee must size the guard to the largest clock error it tolerates; in this sweep a guard of 1 s covered the crystal's largest error up to a six-hour resync. The lost-link experiment shows two things and prices neither in delay. A lost grant is a stopped vehicle, because R3 never assumes an unconfirmed reservation. The 26 % of grants that drop and retry lost at a loss of 0.25 per hop is that loss itself: its retry interval is longer than a grant's lifetime, both assumed, so each grant was sent once, and three tries inside the lifetime would by arithmetic lose 1.6 %. Custody removes the loss; the replay does not show that it is needed. And a silent vehicle is the usual case: along the month's contact plan vehicles had a radio path to the base in 57 % of their driving time, and 2,566 of 2,628 reservations ended with their holder unheard. Under the rule as first written the base released those segments, which is unsafe when the holder is silent because it has failed inside one; under the rule as it now stands they are marked unconfirmed and entered only at caution speed. No logistics run contains the lost-link ladder. The fleets of E43 and E50 never turn back, the replay holds 82 returns a month against 199 jobs, and the nearest thing that was priced, the strict reading of R7, cost 49.1 h per delivery. The delay cost of the ladder and of caution-speed entry is therefore unknown and probably large.

On integrity, the network did what H15 (a) asked and more on jumps and noise, at the same nominal false-alarm rate on held-out data. Out of distribution, when the true sensor errors were larger than the filter claimed, no detector kept its false-alarm rate, so their recalls there cannot be compared and no choice of detector follows from them. The three innovation tests gave 199 to 863 false alarms per pair-day and the network 14. A detector that alarms in most minutes meets most faults by coincidence: in the long-range class, where the CUSUM alarmed in 71 % of fault-free minutes, it found 98 % of the small jumps; in the short-range class, where it gave 28 false alarms per pair-day, it found 20 %, against the network's 21 % at 3.3. The classical tests lost more of their calibration than the network (the per-step test's rate rose 700 times, the network's 26), and with thresholds reset to the nominal rate every detector found 1 % to 3 % of the small jumps and slow drifts. R9 turns each false alarm into an inflated volume and a delay, and no run prices false alarms at these rates, so failing towards alarms is not shown to be the safe direction. On this evidence none of the detectors is qualified for the safety path. What the chi-square test has is a threshold that follows from a stated assumption (Proposition 5); what the experiment shows is that the assumption, the claimed variance, is the thing to monitor. No detector helps with slow drifts, of which every one missed 37 % or more: a single range sees only the error along the line of sight, and a second witness at another bearing is needed. No experiment here has two witnesses cross-check one report. E55 adds two observations. The protocol's rule as written gave 0.10 false alarms per pair-day on the test set without tuning, because of its persistence clause: the same threshold alone alarmed in 53 minutes of a day. That rate rests on a handful of alarm-minutes and has no interval, and with the noisier sensors it was 69. And agreement among rovers by trimmed averaging does not work on this terrain: the rule cannot be pushed outside the honest range while F covers the faulty rovers, and more links do not make up for an F that is too small (Section 5.11), but the honest rovers converged in at most 10 % of runs, 16 % with the base mast as a node, because few of them hear three others. On this mesh a shared estimate, such as the position of a beacon or a hazard, cannot be left to averaging among rovers. Computing it at the base, with the trimmed mean kept as a fallback that cannot be pushed outside the honest range, is the design these results point to; it was not run.

6.2 Comparison with earlier studies

Our cost of safety is smaller than the warehouse and intersection literature reports, for a reason that is in the numbers rather than in the method. Hönig and colleagues' execution layer cost nothing in unnecessary wait and beat synchronous execution by 2.5 times in station use [5]; Dresner and Stone's reservations added under 0.1 s to travel time at densities that saturated every traffic light [7]; lifelong warehouse planners lose throughput only past a density peak of hundreds of agents on a few thousand cells [6, 17]. Our base has eight vehicles on 242 km of road, and the density ladder of Section 5.3 shows the same shape, with the cost becoming visible between two and four times today's fleet. The uncoordinated mode's cost has a closer analogue in drone traffic management, where NASA's tests found operational impact from downed servers and misimplemented specifications rather than from latency [9]; on the Moon the equivalent of a downed server is the horizon, and it is down for eleven days at a time. Our deadlock result agrees with the theory of well-formed layouts [19, 20] and with Dresner and Stone's observation that coarse reservations deadlock [7]: our reservations are whole segments, which is coarse, but they end at bays, which is what the theory requires. Our per-type bounds are the zero-numerator bounds of Hanley and Lippman-Hand [33]: with no violation in n encounters the 95 % upper limit is close to 3/n, 3.0 × 10−5 at 100,000 and 3.0 × 10−4 at 10,000, larger at the smaller effective n of the weighted types, and they hold for the generated distribution of each type, not for the base's actual traffic. Zhao and colleagues reach rarer events by importance sampling on the lane-change parameters [35]; we used the same device on three types (the exit distance, the crew's emergence time and the dead-stop time), which cost effective sample size (Section 5.6) and found the one violating case, type 9, where the boosted region was.

6.3 Implications

For the agreements that the Artemis Accords leave to the parties [1], the implication is that the rules themselves are cheap and the sharing of a reservation table is where the value lies. A base that adopts rules like Table 2 but lets each company keep its own table, with conflicts escalated to Earth, keeps the safety of the rules and pays 5.1 times the delay; one that shares the table on the Moon pays 0.1 %. The shared table does not require a shared planner, a shared dispatcher or a shared owner, only that every vehicle run the same small program and accept a grant from the base computer, which is the federated structure of drone traffic management [8, 25] with the server moved to the Moon. For NASA's policy question of when central coordination becomes necessary [2], the simulation says: as soon as there is a one-lane road and two owners. For designers, the number that drives cost is the share of one-lane road, which the 5 m terrain cannot give; a survey of the real corridors at rover scale, or a graded road, would settle what we had to assume.

For the protocol that would carry these rules between owners [80], the later experiments give four requirements. A position report must carry the vehicle's own error bound, because the reservation margin is computed from it (Proposition 2) and the integrity test is normalised by it (Proposition 5); when the claimed bound was smaller than the true error, every detector lost its false-alarm rate, which is what the out-of-distribution set shows. Every reservation needs a guard and every time stamp a named time scale. A reservation grant needs custody or a retry inside its lifetime, because the rules turn a lost grant into a stopped vehicle. And the lost-link procedure must be filed in advance and known to all, as it is for aircraft [73, 74]; even then the base cannot tell a silent vehicle that has left its segment from one that has failed inside it, so the segment is marked unconfirmed and not released. On speed, Proposition 3 gives the caution speed for entering such a segment, and it shows what a driver on Earth costs: with a driver on Earth in the loop the safe speed at 10 to 20 m of sight is about half to three-fifths of the on-board value, before any allowance for the driver reading the scene.

6.4 Limitations

The findings hold for one base, one route network and one demand model, with the single-lane share assumed; the road layer acts at a 100 m grain and leaves separation within a segment to the encounter simulator; the uncoordinated mode's accounting understates its cost; the encounter bounds hold for the generated distribution and the proof for the discrete model; no sensor is modelled in the encounters; Mars is the Moon's roads with Mars parameters, with its encounters repeating the lunar ones on the flat; the cost of the rules is measured with an oracle dispatcher, reservations kept to the second and no lost-link procedure on the road; the twelve encounter types hold no uphill ground, where the gap rule needed a slope term that those runs do not carry; and the experiments on position, clocks, the lost link and wrong reports are replays of one month with simulated sensors and simulated faults on the motion of the same simulated base, in two dimensions, with several parameters assumed and some analyses not yet run. Section 7 states each in full.

6.5 Future work

Five pieces follow directly. A survey of corridor widths at rover scale, from higher-resolution imagery or a graded road plan, would replace the assumed single-lane share. The safety program should be run on the flight-class computer the companion paper sizes [45], against recorded traffic from this model. The lunar uncoordinated mode should be given the relay the Mars part had and the plan-ahead option of [44], to see how much of its cost they recover. The encounter simulator should be extended to a lander in flight and to the dust a landing raises, which the rules here treat only as a closed zone. And a Mars base on its own terrain, which needs a reader for the HiRISE tiles, a site and a route network, would test the rules on the slopes where Mars gravity acts.

The later experiments leave their own list. The analyses that the internal review asked for have all been run. Beyond those: the terrain-fix stops on repositioning drives, which the follow-up run left out; the lost-link ladder and caution-speed entry inside the logistics model, whose delay cost is unknown; a drop-and-retry baseline whose retry fits inside a grant's lifetime; a sweep of the hand-resolution time and one local fallback for the uncoordinated mode; type 9 with the full-stopping-distance gap; detectors compared at matched realised false-alarm rates, and the delay that R9's false alarms cost at 0.1, 1 and 70 per pair-day; and how often each vehicle's error is inside its own bound. Positioning away from the base needs more than the fixes we modelled: surveyed markers at road junctions and denser fix opportunities on the dark legs are the two candidates. The encounters should be rerun with position error and the widened gap of R1, and with the full-stopping-distance gap that Proposition 4 points to. Two witnesses at different bearings should cross-check one report, which is where the slow drifts that every single-range detector missed can be found. And every detector here should be run on recorded ranging and navigation data from a field test before any of them is trusted.

7. Limitations

The single-lane share is assumed. At the rovers' slope limit the 5 m terrain shows no corridor narrower than two vehicles, so every one-lane segment in E43 is a choice: a share of the route samples, the narrowest at a 10° criterion first. The sweep from 0 to 40 % brackets the plausible range, and the ATC mode's cost stayed under 3.7 % across it, but the absolute waits depend on where the real pinch points are.

One base, one network, one demand. The Connecting Ridge base of [41, 42], its routes and the demand model of [40] are the only ones simulated. The fleet multiples change the number of vehicles and items, not the road network, so the density ladder is a ladder of traffic on fixed roads.

The road layer has a 100 m grain. In the logistics model vehicles reserve whole segments at job commit and following vehicles are not separated; R1, R2 and R9 act only in the encounter simulator. A vehicle that would be delayed within a segment by a slower one ahead is not delayed in E43, which understates the ATC mode's cost by an amount the encounter simulator bounds but does not price.

The uncoordinated mode's cost is understated. When a cross-owner conflict stops two vehicles, the other vehicle's stop lengthens its busy time but deliveries it had already recorded are not moved, and the hand-resolution time of two hours is assumed. Both choices favour the uncoordinated mode.

The cost of the rules is measured under favourable conditions. Every E43 run uses the oracle dispatcher, whose assignments arrive at once and are never lost. A job's reservation windows are computed at commit for every later leg and kept exactly, and the model has no uncertainty in travel time, which for windows filed hours ahead is a larger term than the position error that E50 adds. No logistics run contains the lost-link ladder of Section 3.11: the vehicles of E43 and E50 are out of radio contact for a large part of their driving and never turn back, stop or slow for it, and the strict reading of R7, the nearest thing priced, cost 49.1 h per delivery. The 0.1 % is the cost of the reservations alone.

The twelve encounter types are flat or downhill, and the fresh-seed rerun covers three of them. Slopes are drawn only in the following type and only downhill; types 2 to 11 run on the flat. On an uphill grade a conforming leader brakes harder than the gap rule assumed, and a separate simulation of uphill following showed followers inside the static margin, without contact, under the rule as it was (Appendix A.4). The slope term that removes this when following is the rule we now propose, but the twelve types, the discrete model and the logistics runs were all run without it; the uphill runs cover following and one convoy geometry only, and a remainder of a few centimetres in the Mars convoy is unexplained beyond the lag model. The main run's bounds of types 4, 5 and 6 are computed on the seeds on which simulator defects were found and fixed; the rerun of those three types on fresh seeds found one hard violation in type 6 that the main run had not shown (Section 5.6), and the other nine types, and the Mars encounters, were not rerun on fresh seeds. The request rule that removed that violation was run on the one encounter only. The ring type pools the exit rule as written with a variant.

The rules are checked, not validated. The exhaustive check covers a small discrete model, the encounter bounds hold for the twelve generated types and their parameter ranges, and neither covers real sensors, navigation error inside an encounter (Section 5.9 treats it at the level of reservations only), or a vehicle that breaks the rules on purpose. Owners who misreport are the subject of [45]; Section 5.11 treats a single wrong report as a fault.

The safety program is one implementation. What is checked is our code; a vehicle maker's implementation of the same rules would need the same checks against its own code.

The shield accepts one unsafe case no run reaches. The phase-5 check S2 injected 1,000 proposals to brake on a slope steeper than friction can hold and the shield refused 990; the 10 it accepted were vehicles at near-standstill, because the shield's kinematics take a stopped vehicle to stay stopped. R2 never admits a vehicle to such a slope, so no run of E43, E44 or E47 reaches the case, and the shield was not changed after the runs; a flight implementation should flag it.

A hand-stopped vehicle's plotted position runs early. When Earth resolves a conflict in the uncoordinated mode, the engine shifts the reservation and the busy time of the vehicle that goes first but not its leg plan, so that vehicle's plotted position runs ahead of its reservation by the hand delay until the job ends. Delay, deliveries and waits are computed from busy times and reservations, which are shifted, so no reported number depends on it; the plotted positions feed only the mesh radio, which the road runs of this paper do not use. The defect was found by the trace export after the runs and was not corrected in the engine; the exported trace follows the reservation.

Horizons and seeds differ by fleet size. The four- and eight-times cells run 90 and 60 days on ten seeds, not a year on thirty, so their intervals are wider and their delays are not directly comparable with the year-long cells' absolute values; the paired differences within a cell size are the result. The horizons were fixed in the design of E43 before the dispatch fix of Section 4.6 and kept so that the rerun matched the archived rows; after that fix they are a choice of the design, not a limit of compute.

Mars is the Moon's roads with Mars parameters. The Mars part runs the same base, roads, routes and demand with Mars gravity, a Mars Earth link and the lunar vehicles, and its encounters repeat the lunar ones on the flat (Section 6.1); a Mars site with its own terrain, roads and vehicles was planned and not run, because the terrain reader is written for the lunar polar tiles; the share of each sol with Earth in view, the conjunction date and the dust storms are assumed, and the simulated year holds a conjunction, which comes once in about 26 months, a conservative choice; and at 2,000 encounters per type the Mars bounds are five times the Moon's for the same type, 2.7 × 10−3 for the unweighted types with the Bonferroni correction and up to 6.0 × 10−3 for the weighted types (Table 7).

The later experiments are simulated twice over. In E50, E51, E54, E55 and E56 the sensors, the clocks, the radio and the faults are models laid over the motion of the logistics model, itself a simulation. The one use of real data is the check of Section 5.11 on spacecraft housekeeping telemetry, which is not position data; no ranging radio, navigation solution or flight clock was used. The five fault shapes of E56 are textbook shapes, and the network was trained and tested on one generator, with different months and vehicle pairs but the same physics; a real fault may look like none of them, and the out-of-distribution set shows how quickly a detector calibrated on one sensor model fails on another.

One site, two dimensions, no terrain in the integrity data. All of it is the Connecting Ridge base with eight vehicles, two of which do most of the driving. Positions are horizontal. In E56 radio line of sight is the smooth horizon: terrain blocking, multipath and elevation are not modelled, though E50, E51 and E54 take line of sight from the terrain. A single range sees an error only along the line between the two vehicles, and no experiment has several witnesses test one report.

Parameters of the position and clock models are assumed. The reach of the short-range ranging radio, the error of long-range ranging, the spacing of terrain-fix opportunities and the stop a fix takes, the drift in shadow, the failure rate of fixes in the dark, the residual of a time transfer, the margin multiplier k = 1 and the guard are marked Assumed in the research notes [80]. Only their nominal values were run, which departs from [59], where such parameters were to be swept. The guard was swept in E51 and the sensor errors were changed once, in the out-of-distribution set of E56, which is one shift of our choosing; nothing else was swept. The crystal's frequency error is drawn from zero to its maximum on one side only, which halves the spread of clock differences against a draw of both signs.

E50's delay leaves out the fix stops, and its bounds come from another run. A vehicle that stops for a terrain fix loses that time, about 37 h a month for today's fleet, and E50 does not count it. The follow-up run that does (Section 5.9) is exploratory, covers today's fleet only and inserts only part of the stop time, so its figure is a lower bound. A quarter of E50's fixes were taken inside one-lane segments, where a vehicle may not stop, so the errors of Table 9 inside segments are lower than a fleet that obeys R3 would have. The bound each vehicle files with is taken from the perfect-position run of the same seed, as an hourly maximum, not from the run in which it is used; an overlap that remains with the margin may therefore come from that mismatch and not from an error outside a correctly computed bound, and how often the error is inside the vehicle's own bound is not in the results files. Positions and errors are sampled every 60 s. Every vehicle starts the month with zero error. All of these experiments use one month, July 2028. The verification rows for these models check formulas and that the options switch off; none checks the position filter, the clock model, the contact plan or the overlap counter against an independent reference.

Three experiments are replays of recorded months. E51 stamps the logged reservations with each vehicle's clock after the run, and E54 carries the month's messages over its contact plan. Neither changes the logistics run, so a vehicle stopped by a lost grant, or a vehicle that drives back to find its link, does not alter later dispatch, and a returning vehicle is given a direct link to the base, which is optimistic when its last link went through another vehicle. E55's agreement runs draw positions from the recorded trajectories and exchange one scalar.

Few fault-free days, a partial multiplicity correction, one untested margin. The false-alarm rates near one per pair-day rest on fault-free data that stand for 24 pair-days, so the realised test rates differ between detectors and have wide intervals; recalls are compared at the validation thresholds and not at exactly equal test rates. The Holm correction of [59] covers the three confirmatory hypotheses that have a sampling test (Section 4.5). H9′ is a comparison with a threshold and has none, and the clause of H10′ on larger fleets rests on 10 seeds at four times the fleet, which do not resolve it, and 5 at eight times. The p values were constructed after the results. The widened gap of R1 is implemented and unit-tested but was not exercised in the encounter simulator, whose vehicles know their positions exactly. Every analysis that the internal review of this paper asked for has been run.

9. Conclusion

We asked whether shared traffic rules keep robots of different owners safe at a lunar base, what they cost, and whether they survive Mars gravity and imperfect position, time and reports. One safety program enforcing ten rules, tested in simulation, allowed no violation in ten of twelve encounter types, all flat or downhill; a vehicle that stops dead in a one-lane segment defeats the gap rule, one landing closure in 10,000 left a vehicle inside the zone, and a ring of one-lane segments can deadlock. Uphill, a braking leader took followers inside the static margin, without contact, until a slope term was added to the gap rule. At today's fleet the rules cost 0.1 % of delay per delivery when reservations are filed with the job; settling every cross-owner conflict from Earth cost 5.1 times the baseline delay. At Mars gravity only flat encounters could be driven; with Earth assumed visible half of each sol, referral cost one-sixth as much. Without satellites, ranging held position error inside the 5 m margin only where two beacons were in sight, not over the registered radio range; the stops for terrain fixes, not the widened reservations, set the cost, 0.63 h per delivery as a lower bound; and no detector of wrong position reports kept its false-alarm rate with noisier sensors.

For practice: share the reservation table on the Moon, and treat a silent vehicle's segment as unconfirmed, not free. The results hold for one simulated base and a checked, not validated, model.

Appendix A. Guarantees: seven propositions

Each proposition states its assumption and is proved in a few lines. The derivations follow the research notes [80]; we recomputed every number, and each number quoted below is computed again by the build script from the rule parameters in the code and, where an experiment bears on the proposition, checked against its results file. A proposition says what holds under its assumption. How often the assumption holds is what the experiments measure.

A.1 Dead-reckoning error growth

Proposition 1. Let the along-track error of dead reckoning after a distance d be e(d) = εd + w(d), where ε is a constant scale error with zero mean and standard deviation σε (wheel slip that the odometer does not model) and w is a random walk, independent of ε, with variance s2d. Then

σe(d)2 = σε2d2 + s2d,

σe grows without bound, and it reaches a margin m at the distance

d* = [ −s2 + √(s4 + 4σε2m2) ] / (2σε2)  ≤  m/σε,

with equality when s = 0. At speed v the margin is reached after the time d*/v.

Proof. The two terms are independent, so their variances add. The sum increases with d, and σe(d*) = m is the quadratic σε2d2 + s2d − m2 = 0, whose positive root is d*. Since s2d* ≥ 0, σε2d*2 ≤ m2, which is the bound. ∎

With m = d0 = 5 m and the drift of Section 3.9, σε = 2 % gives d* ≤ 250 m and 10 % gives 50 m; the two-sided 95 % level, 1.96 σ, is reached after at most 128 m and 26 m. A crew rover at 15 km/h covers 250 m in 60 s and a cargo rover at 4 km/h in 225 s. Across the track a bounded heading error β adds βd, 0.17 % of the distance at the star tracker's 0.1°, and a gyro bias b alone adds v b t2/2, 2.6 % of the distance after an hour at 3°/h [60], so the heading reference decides whether the odometer's figure is kept. E50 illustrates the growth and does not test the formula: with dead reckoning alone the 95th percentile of the error was 85 m after one hour of driving and 308 m after eight (30 seeds).

A.2 The margin rule

Proposition 2. Vehicles A and B hold conflicting reservations of one segment, with nominal windows [sA, tA] and [sB, tB]. Vehicle i crosses at speed vi > 0, and its along-track position error at the entry and at the exit is at most βi in size, so that it is truly at each end of the segment within βi/vi of the time it believes. Let each vehicle file its window widened by μi = kβi/vi at both ends, with k ≥ 1, and let the table grant only windows that do not overlap as filed. Then the true windows do not overlap.

Proof. Let A be the earlier of the two, so that tA + μA ≤ sB − μB. A truly leaves no later than tA + βA/vA ≤ tA + μA, and B truly enters no earlier than sB − βB/vB ≥ sB − μB. So A has left before B enters. ∎

The premise is about speed as well as position. A vehicle is at each end of the segment within β/v of the time it believes only if it covers the stretch of length β there at the speed v. A vehicle that starts from rest at a bay, as every waiting vehicle does, needs √(2β/a) to cover β at its acceleration a: 6.5 s for β = 4.2 m and a = 0.2 m/s2, against a margin β/v of 1.0 s at 15 km/h. And a waiting vehicle whose position is wrong by β may stand up to β inside the mouth of the segment for as long as it waits, which no margin in time covers; a stop line set back from the entry by β does. Neither is in the rule as it was run: the logistics model takes v as the mean speed of the whole leg and has no stop line. Errors of opposite sign on the two vehicles are covered by the proof.

The proposition covers errors inside the bound and promises nothing outside it. A 95 % bound is by its definition exceeded about one time in twenty, and E50 still counted overlaps with the margin (Table 10). E50 does not test the proposition: it shifts each window by error over speed, which is the premise. Clock error adds in the same way: with clock errors of at most DA and DB the two margins must together cover DA + DB + βA/vA + βB/vB [80]; Proposition 6 treats the clock terms alone.

A.3 Maximum safe speed

Proposition 3. A vehicle sees clear ground for a distance dp, holds its speed through a reaction delay tr and then brakes at a constant a > 0. It stops within dp − d0, for a margin 0 ≤ d0 < dp, if and only if its speed is at most

vmax = −a tr + √( a2tr2 + 2a(dp − d0) ).

Proof. The distance covered is v tr + v2/(2a), which increases with v ≥ 0. Setting it equal to dp − d0 gives v2 + 2a trv − 2a(dp − d0) = 0, whose positive root is vmax. ∎

On a downhill slope θ with friction μ the braking is at most g(μ cos θ − sin θ), and no stop is possible when μ ≤ tan θ. On the Moon g = 1.62 m/s2, so friction allows a = μg = 0.73 m/s2 on the flat at μ = 0.45; R2 uses the smaller of that and the 0.5 m/s2 service brake, and d0 = 0 inside the sight distance. With 0.5 m/s2 and 20 m of clear sight, a vehicle that reacts on board after 1 s may drive at 14.4 km/h; one whose driver is on Earth and reacts after 6 s, the middle of the 4 to 8 s delays NASA tested [78], at 8.6 km/h. With 10 m of sight the two caps are 9.7 km/h and 4.9 km/h, and with the friction limit alone (a = 0.73 m/s2) at 20 m they are 17.0 km/h and 9.3 km/h. NASA's simulator study measured a mean of 2.03 km/h at a 6 s delay [78], below these caps, which is consistent with them and validates nothing. At long delays the term a tr dominates and friction hardly matters; at short delays it does. R2 computes the distance as the proposition does, at constant speed through the reaction time; R1's gap lets the follower accelerate at aacc through it, which is the more cautious. The same formula gives the caution speed of the lost-link rule (Section 3.11). A vehicle that enters a segment marked unconfirmed may find a stopped vehicle in it, so it drives no faster than vmax for its own perception range dp, with the static margin d0 = 5 m kept: 6.4 km/h for dp = 10 m, 12.3 km/h for 20 m, and the 15 km/h class limit from 27 m on, with the on-board reaction time and the service brake on the flat. No model in this paper has run that speed.

A.4 Reservations and the gap

Proposition 4 (for the flat and for downhill grades; on uphill grades assumption (vii) fails for a conforming vehicle, as shown below). Assume that (i) one authority grants the reservations of a segment at any time; (ii) it grants a window on a one-lane segment only if no confirmed window of a vehicle travelling in the opposite direction overlaps it as filed (R3); (iii) a vehicle is inside a one-lane segment only during a window confirmed to it, has left by the window's end, and without a confirmation can stop before the entry (R3, R2); (iv) position and clock errors are within the margins of Propositions 2 and 6; and (v) every vehicle complies. Then (a) no two vehicles travelling in opposite directions are in one one-lane segment at the same time. If also (vi) a following vehicle keeps the gap of R1, (vii) the vehicle ahead never decelerates harder than bmax, and (viii) the follower knows the gap and the leader's speed without error, or with an error inside d0, then (b) the follower does not touch it.

Proof. (a) By (iii) and (v) each vehicle is in the segment only inside its own window as it believes it. By (ii), with (i) so that the comparison covers every pair, the filed windows of two opposing vehicles do not overlap, and by (iv) with Propositions 2 and 6 their true windows do not overlap either. (b) R1's gap is dmin = Dr − vf2/(2bmax) + d0, where Dr = vrρ + aaccρ2/2 + (vr + ρaacc)2/(2bmin) is the follower's worst-case stopping distance and vf the leader's speed. Under (vii) the leader travels at least vf2/(2bmax) before it stands, so once both have stopped the gap is at least dmin + vf2/(2bmax) − Dr = d0 > 0, and by the argument of [14] it is not smaller before that. ∎

The assumption that fails in the dead-stop case of Section 5.6 is (vii). A leader that a fault stops at once travels no distance, and the final gap is d0 − vf2/(2bmax) in the worst case, a follower at exactly the minimum gap that accelerates through its response time. With bmax = 0.8 m/s2 and d0 = 5 m the final gap is negative for a leader faster than 2.83 m/s (10.2 km/h); at 15 km/h the shortfall is 5.9 m. A follower that keeps its own full stopping distance, 23.3 m + d0 at 15 km/h in place of 12.5 m + d0, needs no assumption about the leader; we did not run that rule. E44 found the case: in 58 of 10,000 encounters with a nonconforming vehicle the follower came inside the 5 m static margin, and in 7 it touched, all with a leader stopped dead by a fault (Section 5.6).

Assumption (vii) also fails for a vehicle that breaks no rule. Guaranteed braking is b = min(bservice, μg cos θ) − g sin θ with θ positive downhill, so a leader that brakes fully on an uphill grade decelerates at bservice + g sin|θ|, which exceeds bmax = 0.8 m/s2 on grades steeper than 10.7° on the Moon and 4.6° on Mars (where the service brake binds; at μ = 0.3 on the Moon friction binds and the grade is 11.5°). The steepest slope on the base's routes is 17.2°. By the formulas above, at 15 km/h a follower at exactly dmin behind a leader that brakes fully on that grade ends 3.0 m from it on the Moon: inside the 5 m static margin, which is a hard violation by this paper's own definition, with no fault and no rule broken. At Mars gravity on the same grade the final gap is 0.2 m. That is the worst case of the formulas, a follower that accelerates through its whole response time.

Uphill encounters were then simulated: following on a two-lane road and a convoy inside one one-lane segment, at lunar and Mars gravity, on grades of 5°, 10°, 15° and 17.2°, 2,000 encounters a cell, with every follower held at the rule's limit, once with commands that act at once, as in the main run, and once with an actuation lag of 1 s, R1's response time. Under R1 as it was, no vehicle touched another in any cell, but followers came inside the 5 m static margin, which is a hard violation. On the Moon this happened only when following with the lag, from 14° (14 of 2,000 encounters at 15°, 335 at 17.2°, smallest gap 4.15 m), and never in the one-lane convoy. At Mars gravity with the lag it happened from 6.5° when following and 8.5° in the convoy, down to a gap of 0.72 m, and at 15° and above even without the lag. With a slope term in R1, the leader's braking taken as the larger of bmax and its guaranteed braking on the grade, no follower came inside the margin when following, on either body, at any grade, with or without the lag. In the Mars convoy with the lag 71 of 2,000 encounters at 15.0° and 62 of 2,000 encounters at 17.2° still ended inside it, by at most 0.14 m (smallest gap 4.86 m). A trace puts that remainder on the lag model, in which a vehicle that commands braking still applies one 0.1 s step more of its earlier commands than R1's response time allows; it was not patched. Part (b) as stated, with a fixed bmax, is therefore established for the flat and for downhill grades only, which is where the twelve encounter types were driven. With the slope term, (vii) reads “no harder than the larger of bmax and its guaranteed braking on the grade”, the proof goes through unchanged, and the simulation agrees with it when following. R1 with the slope term is the rule we propose (Table 2); in the code it is an option, and the twelve types were run without it, on the flat and downhill, where it changes nothing. Two more things are assumed without being listed. Part (b) takes the follower to know the true gap and the leader's speed; under position error the widened gap d0 + k bAB of Section 3.9 is meant to cover that, and the encounters did not exercise it. And part (a) is close to the definition of the rule: (iii) assumes that a vehicle has left by the end of its window, which a vehicle disabled inside the segment has not. That case is outside (a) as well as (b). What covers it is the lost-link rule of Section 3.11: the segment stays reserved, is marked unconfirmed, and is entered only at the caution speed of Proposition 3. Liveness is a separate matter: exclusive segments alone allow the ring deadlock of Section 5.6.

A.5 The chi-square innovation test

Proposition 5. Vehicle A measures z = h(pA, pB) + w, with w normal with covariance R, and predicts the measurement from its own position estimate, with covariance PA, and from the position B reports, with the covariance PB that B claims. Assume that the report is honest and that all errors are Gaussian, independent and of the stated covariances. Then the innovation ν, the measurement minus its prediction, is normal with mean zero and covariance S = R + HAPAHAT + HBPBHBT; the statistic d2 = νTS−1ν follows the chi-square law with as many degrees of freedom as there are scalar measurements; and the test d2 > γ, with γ the (1 − α) quantile of that law, raises a false alarm with probability α. For a single range, γ = z2 with z the (1 − α/2) quantile of the standard normal law, and a report wrong by b along the line of sight is flagged with probability

Φ(δ − z) + Φ(−δ − z),   δ = b/σv,   σv2 = S,

which is at least p once δ ≥ z + zp, with zp the p quantile. If successive tests are independent, the rule “k of the last n flagged” has false-alarm probability Σi≥k C(n, i) αi(1 − α)n−i.

Proof. To first order in the errors, ν is a sum of independent zero-mean Gaussian terms with the covariances listed, so S−1/2ν is standard normal in as many dimensions as there are measurements and its squared length is chi-square. For one range, d2 > γ is |ν|/σv > z. An error b along the line of sight shifts ν/σv to a normal variable with mean δ and unit variance, which gives the probability stated, and Φ(δ − z) ≥ p when δ − z ≥ zp. The last statement is the binomial tail. ∎

For one range and α = 10−3, γ = 10.83 (z = 3.29); for a range and a bearing, γ = −2 ln α = 13.82. A report wrong by b along the line of sight is flagged with probability 0.9 once b reaches 4.57 σv and with 0.99 at 5.62 σv. With navigation errors of 2 m on both vehicles and a ranging error of 0.3 m, σv = 2.84 m: the threshold is 9.4 m and the error found nine times in ten is 13.0 m; R9's fixed tolerance of 11 m lies between them. If three of the last five tests must be flagged, and the tests are independent, a false alarm has probability 1.0 × 10−8 per window and an error flagged with 0.9 per test is declared with 0.991. E56 bears on the assumption twice. At one report a second, one false alarm a day is α = 1/86,400 and γ = 19.2; the threshold found on validation data, with none of the Gaussian argument, was 19.1. That is one point at which the two tails cross and not a test of the law: at the lower thresholds the same test alarmed less often than Gaussian innovations would (Section 5.11). And when the true errors were larger than the variance the filter claimed, the same threshold gave 479 false alarms per pair-day (Section 5.11): the false-alarm rate is only as good as S. The independence in the three-of-five clause does not hold on those streams either: E55 measured 0.10 false alarms per pair-day for the rule, against 0.0009 for independent tests. A single range sees only the part of an error that lies along the line between the two vehicles.

A.6 The clock guard

Proposition 6. Two conflicting reservations are stamped [sA, tA] and [sB, tB], with sB − tA = G ≥ 0, by clocks that read true time plus xA and plus xB. The true windows are disjoint if and only if xB − xA ≤ G. In particular, if every reservation carries a guard g at both ends, so that G ≥ 2g between conflicting windows, and every clock is within g of the base's time scale, no pair conflicts. Put the other way: windows separated by g cannot conflict when the two clocks differ by no more than g, which holds when each is within g/2 of the base scale.

Proof. A's window truly ends at tA − xA and B's truly begins at sB − xB, so the true gap is G − (xB − xA). In the first case xB − xA ≤ |xA| + |xB| ≤ 2g ≤ G, and the second case is the same with g in place of 2g. ∎

A clock with frequency error y, resynced every τ to a residual r, has |x| ≤ |r| + |y|τ, so it stays within g while τ ≤ (g − |r|)/|y|: for g = 0.1 s and the crystal's 95th-percentile frequency error that is 36.5 h, and the drawn clocks of E51 held it for 36.7 h. In all 51 cells of E51 in which the largest clock error was within the guard, on the two time scales, no conflict occurred, as the proposition requires: 42 atomic-clock cells and 9 crystal cells with the 1 s guard. No crystal cell with the 0.1 s guard is among them, because vehicles out of radio reach miss their resyncs, so the zero conflicts that Section 5.10 reports for that guard lie outside this proposition: at the 6 h resync the largest error was 0.84 s and the worst pair closed 0.123 s of the 0.2 s between its windows. The premise is sufficient, not necessary: at a 10 s resync the crystal's largest error was 0.33 s, on vehicles out of radio reach, yet the clocks closed no gap between two conflicting reservations by more than 18 µs, because those vehicles held no reservation next to an opposing one at the time. That zero is an observation on 30 seeds; the guarantee is the proposition's.

A.7 Trimmed-mean agreement

Proposition 7 (safety proved here and tested in E55; agreement stated from the reference). Each vehicle holds a value, and the values a vehicle hears at a step are the senders' values at that step, without delay. At each step a vehicle that is not faulty sorts the values it hears, discards the F largest that exceed its own and the F smallest that are below its own (all of them where there are fewer than F), and replaces its value by an average, with positive weights, of its own value and the rest. (a) Safety. If no such vehicle has more than F faulty neighbours, the largest value M(t) and the smallest value m(t) among the vehicles that are not faulty never move outwards, on any graph and whatever the faulty vehicles send. (b) Agreement. Call a graph (r, s)-robust if, for every pair of disjoint non-empty sets of nodes, at least one of the two sets consists entirely of nodes with r or more neighbours outside it, or the two sets together hold at least s such nodes. With at most F faulty vehicles in the network, the others converge to one value if and only if the graph is (F+1, F+1)-robust; with at most F in every neighbourhood, (2F+1)-robustness is sufficient; and every vehicle then hears at least 2F+1 others, three for F = 1 [72].

Proof of (a). Take a vehicle that is not faulty, with value x, and a value y that survives its trimming and comes from a faulty neighbour. If y > x, then F values at least as large as y were discarded. At most F − 1 of them come from faulty neighbours, because y comes from one and there are at most F, so one comes from a neighbour that is not faulty, and y ≤ M(t). If y < x the same argument gives y ≥ m(t). Values from neighbours that are not faulty lie between m(t) and M(t) by definition, and so does x. The new value is an average of numbers between m(t) and M(t) and lies there too. ∎

Part (b) is the theorem of LeBlanc and colleagues, who prove it for a continuous-time form of the rule [72]. The discrete-time statement is in a journal paper that we could not open, and we rely on (b) for nothing beyond what is said here. E55 tested part (a). In all 1,500 runs with F at least the number of faulty rovers, every honest value stayed inside the first honest range at every step, whether or not the mesh was robust; with two faulty rovers and F = 1, where the premise can fail, the range held in only 48 % to 92 % of runs, by cell. Part (b) is stated from [72] and E55 does not test its sufficiency: only 12 of the 800 meshes small enough for an exact check were (2F+1)-robust, and agreement was reached in at most 10 % of runs in any cell in which F covered the faulty rovers (Section 5.11).
