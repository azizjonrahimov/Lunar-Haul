3. Model

Three models share one implementation of the rules. The logistics model is the discrete-event simulation of our earlier papers [40, 41, 42], with a road layer added that makes vehicles wait for one another; it prices the rules in cargo delay over a year. The encounter simulator runs two to four vehicles, a landing and a crew member through continuous kinematics on a small piece of road, through the same safety check, and counts violations. The discrete model is a small abstraction of the same situations, searched exhaustively. This section describes the base and its roads, the rules, the safety program, the two coordination modes, and the two smaller models. Provenance follows the convention of [41]: Measured values come from a cited measurement or document, Spec from a published specification, Derived from a computation on such values, and Assumed values are our choices, each varied in Section 4.2 or named in Section 7.

3.1 Base, terrain and roads

The base is the one of [41, 42, 44], on the Connecting Ridge between Shackleton and de Gerlache craters, on NASA Goddard's 5 m terrain tile from the Lunar Orbiter Laser Altimeter (Measured). Its sites are the habitat and control centre (BASE), a cargo depot, a power plant, a construction site, an oxygen pilot plant, a science site and a permanently shadowed crater; landers use two pads 1 km from the base. Routes between sites are the driving routes of [41], sampled every 100 m along their length: {{P2-N-SAMPLES}} samples on {{P2-ROUTE-KM}} of distinct road, with {{P2-N-JUNCTIONS}} junction samples where routes meet. Demand, landings and the fleet are those of the companion paper at its middle load [44]: six vehicle classes of 2028–2032 programmes, from 80 kg crew rovers to a 2,000 kg cargo rover, owned by three companies, busy about 30 % of the cargo vehicles' time (load factor {{P2-K30}}, Assumed, calibrated in [44]). Larger fleets in Section 4.2 multiply every vehicle and the demand by the same factor.

Which parts of these roads are one lane wide is a question the terrain can only partly answer. We measured the drivable corridor at every sample as the width, across the route direction, over which the slope on the 5 m grid stays below a limit, and called a sample single-lane when that corridor is narrower than two vehicle widths plus two lateral margins: 2 × 2.3 m + 2 × 2.7 m = 10 m (Spec for the width of the reference rover [16]; Assumed for the margin). At the rovers' 20° slope limit no sample is single-lane: the median corridor is {{P2-MEDIAN-WIDTH-20}} wide and the steepest slope on any route is {{P2-SLOPE-MAX}}. At a 15° limit {{P2-SINGLE-KM-15}} of road is single-lane, at 10° {{P2-SINGLE-KM-10}} ({{P2-SINGLE-SHARE-10}} of samples), and at 8° {{P2-SINGLE-KM-8}}. The 5 m grid cannot see boulders, crater rims, shadows or a graded one-lane road, all of which narrow a real corridor, and NASA's own corridor modelling found sections 20–40 m wide [2]. We therefore treat the single-lane share as an assumed scenario factor: a share s marks the s × {{P2-N-SAMPLES}} samples with the narrowest corridor at the 10° criterion as single-lane, so the places that go first are the real pinch points of the route network, with s = 0.15 by default (Assumed; the 10° value) and s from 0 to {{P2-SHARE-MAX-PCT}} in Section 4.2. The ranked places, with coordinates, are in the repository. Consecutive single-lane samples along any route form a physical segment; passing bays split segments every 1 km (Assumed; 0.5–2 km in Section 4.2). Chargers and the pads' aprons are reserved slots, two per pad (Assumed).

3.2 Rules of the road

Table 2 states the ten rules, their default parameters and the basis of each. They are written so that a vehicle can check every one of them from what it knows locally: its own state, the reservations it holds, the reservations and intents its neighbours have broadcast, and the published constraints. Four principles run through them. A vehicle never assumes a reservation it has not had confirmed, so a lost message can only add delay [7]. A vehicle enters a one-lane segment only with the whole segment reserved for its traversal and its exit guaranteed, so no vehicle ever waits inside a segment and a circular wait cannot form [19, 23]. Priority follows a fixed ladder in which crew on foot and landings come first and the identity of the owner never counts, with ties broken by the earlier filed request and then by vehicle number, so no conflict is left open [8]. And the rules are the same for every owner, which is what lets a safety program on one vehicle reason about what the others will do.

3.3 The safety program

Each vehicle runs a planner and a safety program. The planner may be anything: the hand-built driving logic of [41], a learned policy, or, in the tests of Section 3.5, a generator of random and adversarial proposals. It proposes the next action as a road segment, a speed and a decision to go or wait. The safety program holds the vehicle's reservations, the broadcasts it has heard and the published constraints, checks the proposal against every rule of Table 2 that applies at its level, and either passes it or substitutes the safe fallback: slow to the safe speed, hold at the current safe point, or stop. It is the post-posed shield of [28] in the switching form of [11, 12]: it never plans, it only refuses. One function implements the check, and the logistics model, the encounter simulator and the discrete model all call it, so what is checked is what runs.

3.4 The road layer in the logistics model

The logistics model moves cargo through the base for a simulated year with the pooled dispatcher of [40], which sees every vehicle and every item. The road layer sits between dispatch and driving. When the dispatcher commits a job, the layer walks the vehicle's planned legs over the route samples and applies, in time order, the rules that act at the resolution of a 100 m sample and a job: the one-lane reservations of R3 with the ladder of R4 and R8, the apron slots of R10, the crew slow zone of R6 around the habitat during a scheduled EVA, and the landing closure of R5, which is the plume window of [41] unchanged. A reservation covers the whole segment for the vehicle's traversal window and ends at a bay or junction, so the exit-bay rule holds by construction, no vehicle waits inside a segment, and because the reservation table is filled one job at a time, a circular wait cannot arise. A conflict is a reservation in the opposite direction that overlaps in time; the later request waits at the bay until the segment is free, and the wait extends every later leg of the job. Following vehicles in the same direction are not separated at this resolution: R1 and R2 act within a segment, below the model's grain, and belong to the encounter simulator.

Two coordination modes run the same rules. In the ATC mode there is one shared reservation table, kept by the base computer and reached over the robots' radio mesh of [44]; a reservation is granted in milliseconds when the robot is linked. By default the reservation travels with the job assignment, which reached the robot, so every job files at commit; in the strict variant of R7 a robot files only from its start site or the first route sample the base's mast can see, and a job that cannot file goes to the second mode's procedure. In the uncoordinated mode each owner keeps its own table and sees only its own vehicles' reservations. A conflict between vehicles of different owners is detected when they meet: both stop at the conflict time and wait until the owners' operators on Earth settle it by hand, which takes thand = 2 h (Assumed) and can only begin while the base sees Earth, with the Earth-link windows of [42]. The other vehicle's stop lengthens its busy time; deliveries it had already recorded are not moved, which understates the uncoordinated mode's cost and is listed in Section 7. A third setting, none, removes the road layer and the landing closure altogether and is the baseline against which the rules are priced.

3.5 The encounter simulator

The encounter simulator runs two to four vehicles on a small local road geometry with continuous two-dimensional kinematics at 10 Hz, with the class speeds of Table 2 and braking bmin = 0.5 m/s2 on the flat, reduced by g sin θ downhill, and friction μ swept over 0.3, 0.45 and 0.6 (Assumed; the regolith braking coefficient is not published, Section 7). Each vehicle's planner is one of three: the nominal driving logic, a planner that proposes a random action at every step, and an adversarial planner that proposes the action closest to a violation. Every proposal passes through the safety program of Section 3.3. Twelve encounter types (Table 3) are generated with randomised parameters, one encounter per seed; the closest approach between steps is computed exactly from the kinematics, not sampled; rare types are generated at boosted rates and reweighted, with the weights reported. {{P2-E44-DESIGN-SENTENCE}}

3.6 The discrete model and its checker

The discrete model is a road graph of {{P2-E45-N-NODES}} nodes with typed edges (two-lane, one-lane) and nodes (junction, bay, parking, pad boundary), including one one-lane segment of four cells with a bay at each end and one junction; two or three vehicles, each with a cell, a heading, a mode (drive, wait, yield, stopped) and its held reservations; one landing with phases open, notice, closed and reopen; one crew member moving on a few cells; and a link state per vehicle. Time is one cell transit of the slowest class. Transitions interleave vehicle moves, crew moves, landing phases and link drops, all nondeterministic, and the planner is fully nondeterministic, so whatever the search proves holds for any planner. Each vehicle move goes through the same safety check as the simulators. The checker is an explicit-state search in the style of [50, 32]: states packed into integers, vehicles of the same class interchanged by sorting, a hard cap of three million states. It checks the safety invariants on every reachable state (no two vehicles in the same or adjacent cells on a segment, no vehicle in a closed zone, none in a crew cell or next to it), deadlock freedom (every reachable non-goal state has an enabled move that is not “all wait”), bounded liveness (from every reachable state a state where all vehicles have reached their goals is reachable under fair scheduling), and livelock (strongly connected components with no exit). A checker that never fails proves nothing, so we remove one rule at a time, R3's exit reservation, R5's entry check and R7's hold, and require a counterexample each time.

3.7 Mars

The Mars variant changes four things and nothing else: gravity g = 3.71 m/s2 in the braking and slope terms of R1 and R2; the Earth round trip, 6–44 minutes with 6, 20 and 44 swept, in place of 2.6 s, for every decision that reaches Earth; a solar-conjunction blackout of about 14 days a year with no Earth contact, with no relay in the base case and one relay as a variant; and dust storms as bursts of message loss (Assumed). The road geometry is the Connecting Ridge network with Mars parameters, as the controlled case; {{P2-E47-GEOMETRY-SENTENCE}}

3.8 Checks

The engine's existing checks are those of [40, 41, 42, 44]. The road layer adds its own: a single vehicle on the network sees zero added delay from the road layer; a run with the single-lane share at zero and no landings reproduces the baseline's deliveries; reservations never overlap in the shared table; a run is bit-identical when repeated with the same seed; and every row of every run file carries the cell's settings, so a result cannot be matched to the wrong cell. {{P2-CHECKS-SENTENCE}}

4. Experiment design

4.1 Questions and hypotheses

The pre-registration [43] states three hypotheses for this paper. H7 (safety). Zero violations in each of the twelve encounter types, with the bound 1 − 0.051/n reported per type, and a counterexample from the checker for each rule removed. H8 (cost of safety). At today's fleet the rules add less than 5 % to the delay per delivery; at four to eight times the fleet the cost rises. Only the first clause is confirmatory; the second is reported. H9 (Mars, exploratory). The same rules keep zero violations under Mars gravity and braking; the delay cost of the rules is similar to the Moon's; and coordination that depends on Earth loses more on Mars than on the Moon because of the round trips and the conjunction. H7 and H8 were written before any run; H9 was added with the Mars part on 27 October 2026, before any run, when the three papers were regrouped (change 1 of [43]).

4.3 Seeds and common random numbers

Every cell of E43 uses the same seeds, so a seed's demand, landings and faults are identical across modes and the difference between two modes on one seed is the effect of the road layer alone. Pairs are formed on the seed; because the none and ATC runs do not depend on the start year, an uncoordinated run of 2038 pairs with the none and ATC runs of the same seed. Encounters use one seed per encounter, so each is an independent trial. No run is excluded after the fact; a run that crashes is fixed and repeated with the same seed, and the crash is reported.

4.4 Outcomes

The primary outcome of E43 is the mean delay per delivered item, in hours from the item's readiness to its delivery, the measure of [40]. Secondary outcomes are the 90th percentile of that delay, the mass-weighted delay, the censored mean wait of urgent items of [44], items delivered, operator hours, and the road layer's own counts: hours waited at bays, conflicts, conflicts referred to Earth and the vehicle-hours spent waiting for Earth, hours other vehicles stood because of a stopped vehicle, deadlocks, and bay wait per owner. The primary outcome of E44 is the count of violations per type, with near misses and the minimum separation margin; of E45, the reachable state count and the properties proved.

4.5 Statistics

Differences between modes are paired means over seeds with a percentile bootstrap 95 % confidence interval of 20,000 resamples [55]; the ratio of a difference to the baseline's mean carries its own bootstrap interval. H8's first clause is a one-sided rule: supported if the 95 % upper bound of the ratio (ATC minus none, over the mean of none) at one times the fleet is below 0.05. The density ladder, the uncoordinated mode and the sweeps are reported with intervals and not tested. Deadlocks are counted and must be zero for the exit-bay construction to be reported as holding. For E44 the per-type bound with zero violations is 1 − 0.051/n [33], and the joint statement over the twelve types uses α = 0.05/12 per type, which raises each bound from about 3/n to about 5.5/n; with any violation the Clopper–Pearson interval replaces it [57]. All intervals are descriptive of the simulated population of seeds, not of a lunar fleet.
