The Vehicles, the Network and the Market: What Lunar ATC Puts Inside Each Vehicle and How Its Marketplace Works

Azizjon Rahimov
Roatify Research · Preprint, October 2026; revised November 2026 · Code, data and pre-registration: github.com/azizjonrahimov/Lunar-Haul · Live site: azizjonrahimov.github.io/Lunar-Haul

Abstract. The rovers, hoppers and landers planned for a lunar south-pole base in 2028–2032 have different owners, carry far less than the base needs, and share no navigation or time standard. We ask what the robot-run job market of our companion papers needs in each vehicle, whether that fits, and how its money works among distrustful owners. We collected the vehicles' capabilities, described the radio, navigation, time, map and security layers, timed our implementation, priced four payment rules over {{P3-E46-N-RUNS}} simulated years, and ran pre-registered experiments. {{P3-E48-ABSTRACT-CLAUSE}} {{P3-E46-ABSTRACT-CLAUSE}} {{P3-H5-ABSTRACT-CLAUSE}} {{P3-P6-ABSTRACT-CLAUSE}} Designers can budget a vehicle's share of such a network; operators can price a payment rule. Nothing is measured on the Moon.

Keywords: lunar surface operations, multi-owner robot fleets, mechanism design, surface radio network, lunar positioning and time, flight software sizing

1. Introduction

The vehicles planned to drive, hop and land at a lunar south-pole base between 2028 and 2032 have names, and none of them has flown. NASA ordered two crew rovers in May 2026, Astrolab's CLV-1 and Lunar Outpost's Pegasus, and stated in October 2026 what they will do: carry up to 80 kg of samples and tools, drive 10 km on a charge and 6 km a day when driven from Earth [1]. Cargo-class rovers that could move a tonne or two exist only as company designs [2, 3]. Four hopping drones are planned for 2028 [1], of about 250 kg each according to press reports [7], a nuclear-powered rover was added for late 2028 in November 2026 [107], a small cargo rover of 250 kg is a catalogue product [3], and the landers of the period put between 150 kg and 3 t on the surface at a time [11, 12, 13, 14]; the upper figure is from a secondary reference page [11]. NASA's own planning puts the base's recurring need at 2,500–10,000 kg a year and single elements at 500–15,000 kg, and says that demand and mobility capacity are mismatched by 1,000 to 15,000 kg per asset [5, 6]. The fleet of the period is small, its vehicles are owned by different companies, and no two of them are built to talk to each other.

The radio that would connect them is standardised only in part, and nothing built to that standard has worked on the Moon. The LunaNet Interoperability Specification fixes the links between orbit and the surface and between the Moon and Earth, routes IP and bundle-protocol traffic from surface users, and marks surface Wi-Fi and cellular links as not part of LunaNet 1.0, with their physical layer “bilaterally defined” [15]. The space agencies' surface-wireless standard of May 2024 has since fixed Wi-Fi 6 and LTE Release 12 for that link [97]; what two vehicles of different owners say to each other over it is in no space standard. The one cellular network flown to the Moon ran for about 25 minutes on a lander lying on its side and never completed its call [17]. NASA's surface-relay concept reaches about 10 km from a 10 m mast where terrain allows, and its viewshed study found a single 4 m tower covering 10.6–23.7 % of a 20 × 20 km polar area [16]. Earth itself is hidden from the pole for about 14 days of every month [16, 19]. Any coordination among these vehicles has to run on the vehicles, over short, terrain-blocked links, with Earth as an occasional witness rather than a controller.

Our two companion papers argued that this is enough. The first put a job market on the robots, run over a mesh computed from 5 m terrain with the base's real Earth windows, and measured how much of the gain from sharing vehicles across owners it keeps under message loss and Earth blackouts [60]. The second gave the robots rules of the road and a small safety program that overrides any planner, checked the rules exhaustively on a discrete model and in simulated encounters, and priced them in cargo delay [61]. Both papers took three things for granted: that each vehicle can carry the software and the radio the design needs, that a base computer and its spare can hold the shared reservation table and the ledger, and that owners will accept a market in which their robots bid against one another for work. This paper is about those three assumptions. We call the design Lunar ATC, by analogy with air-traffic control, though its decisions are made by the vehicles and a base computer on the Moon rather than by controllers.

Each assumption has a literature that stops short of our case. Multi-robot autonomy for flight exists in one system, CADRE, three rovers of one owner that elect a leader over a mesh and run a central planner on it, with a lightweight distributed database [26, 27, 28]; its mesh radios share at least 1 Mbit/s of bandwidth [28], and their band and range are not public [10]. Field robotics in denied communication, the DARPA Subterranean Challenge, settled on mesh radios, droppable relays, data mules and time budgets out of contact, and the winning team fielded single-robot autonomy only [54, 55]. Runtime-assurance filters have measured computation costs, with implicit filters 11–13 times costlier than explicit switching on a docking problem [45], but no one has sized a safety program for a lunar vehicle's computer. Mechanism design says that efficiency, truthful bidding, individual rationality and a balanced budget cannot all be had at once [29, 30, 31], and freight carriers who collaborate share the gain by Shapley value and name a fair rule and a trusted coordinator as their main obstacles [35, 36]; ride-hailing data put the cost of fragmentation among operators at 2.5–67 % of fleet per extra operator, falling with demand density [39]. Multi-vendor robot fleets negotiate traffic in practice in Open-RMF, which reports high processor load during negotiation and has no stable wire protocol [41]. Robotics task allocation otherwise assumes cooperative robots; the closest work on self-interested robots is about paths, not jobs [34]. We found no study that sizes the on-vehicle software and radio of a multi-owner robot network against the vehicles that will actually fly, and none that measures a robot market's payments, fairness and manipulability in a logistics model.

Our question is therefore in three parts. What do the vehicles of 2028–2032 carry, and what can they not carry at all? What does Lunar ATC put inside each vehicle, how much processor time, memory, radio bandwidth and power does it need as implemented, and what happens when the base computer fails? And under which payment rules is every owner better off in the shared market than alone, how much can an owner gain by misreporting its costs, and what do audits from Earth recover?

We answered the first part from published sources, with every figure flagged by provenance and the contradictions between sources recorded. We answered the second by instrumenting our own implementation: one function decides every move and reservation, one auction runs the market, one message layer carries bids, grants, status and ledger entries over the mesh, and one base computer with a spare holds the shared table and the ledger. We measured these on the laptop that ran the companion experiments, at one, four and eight times today's fleet, and scaled the results to a radiation-tolerant processor by a stated factor; we swept the base computer's failure rate and measured how long the fleet is without a leader at an assumed re-election delay. We answered the third with a market experiment, E46, in which three owners with equal and with 50/35/15 shares of the cargo run the pooled market under four payment rules over 30 seeds, and in which one owner scales its bids by 0.8 to 1.25 while the others bid honestly, with Earth charging the expected penalty of an audit of 0, 5 or 20 % of the jobs afterwards. The hypotheses, H5 on incentives and H10 on sizing, were written down before any run [59].

A revision of November 2026 adds a fourth part, asked by the same reasoning one layer down. A market and rules of the road assume that every vehicle knows where it is, what time it is and what the ground looks like, in terms that another owner's vehicle shares, and that a message can be believed. None of that is given on the Moon. No navigation satellite is in lunar orbit, no lunar time scale is realised, the public maps do not resolve what stops a rover, and no space standard says what two rovers of different owners say to each other; the two terrestrial standards for robots of mixed vendors that our message set follows are named in Section 3.8. We set out the layers Lunar ATC needs under its market, position, time, a shared map in one frame and a communication stack with authentication, each with its numbers and where they come from. Where a quantity can be derived we derive it, with its assumption (Appendix A); where it cannot, we test it in an experiment whose hypothesis was written down first [67]. Some of those hypotheses turned out to be arithmetic on assumed inputs, which could not have come out otherwise; an internal referee round found them, and they are reported here as design calculations and not as tests. Section 8 lists every claim of the paper as proved, tested, calculated from assumed inputs, assumed or not yet tested.

Our contributions are:

  • A provenance-flagged account of the lunar vehicles of 2028–2032 and the surface radio network, with the capacity plateau the fleet implies and the contradictions between sources recorded rather than resolved.
  • A bill of materials for Lunar ATC inside one vehicle: the safety program, the planner, the radio unit and the local copies of the reservation table and ledger, with the processor time of the safety check and of one robot's bid computation timed on our implementation and scaled to flight hardware by a stated factor, the memory estimated and measured on a laptop, the radio traffic computed from event counts and serialised sizes, and failover simulated.
  • Market rules for owners who do not trust one another, with the money flow from bid to settlement on Earth, and a measurement of individual rationality, manipulation by bid scaling and audits under four payment rules.
  • The layers under the market, with their provenance: what a vehicle carries to know where it is and what time it is, one named frame and one base-held map, and a communication stack that reuses existing standards up to the application layer, where an authenticated interaction protocol is implemented and serialised; each layer has a pre-registered experiment, reported clause by clause as supported, not supported, dependent on a reading, or a design calculation.
  • Eight derivations with their assumptions (Appendix A), each checked by the build against the results files, and a table that sorts every claim of the paper into proved, tested, calculated from assumed inputs, assumed and not yet tested (Section 8).

Several things are outside this paper. Which vehicle takes which job, and how the robots' auction behaves under message loss and Earth blackouts, is the subject of [60]; the rules of the road and the checks that the safety program keeps them are the subject of [61]; here both are treated as given and only their cost in computation, memory and bandwidth is sized. The vehicles' internal layouts are published for few of them, and this paper describes the parts Lunar ATC adds, not the vehicles' own avionics. What position error costs the rules of the road, how a vehicle that reports a false position is detected, and how vehicles agree on a shared value are the companion paper's subjects [61]; this paper gives the position error, the clocks, the map and the authentication they rest on. Owners who misreport their position or availability, hog chargers or pick easy jobs are not simulated; the one manipulation tested is bid scaling. The market has three owners; Section 7 says what that leaves open.

The paper has two halves, and a reader may take either alone. The first is the design and the layers under it: the vehicles (Section 3.2), the radio network (3.3), what Lunar ATC puts inside a vehicle (3.4), position, time, the shared map and the communication stack (3.5 to 3.8), their experiments (E48 and E50 to E55 in Section 4.2), their results (Sections 5.1 to 5.4 and 5.8 to 5.13) and the derivations of Appendix A. The second is the market: the payment rules and the money (Section 3.9), the experiment E46 and hypothesis H5 (Sections 4.1 and 4.2), and its results (Sections 5.5 to 5.7). Section 6 discusses both, Section 7 states the limitations, and Table 18 in Section 8 is the shortest way into the paper: it lists every claim with what stands behind it. What was not run is said where its result would go, and again in Sections 6.6 and 7.
