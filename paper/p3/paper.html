The Vehicles, the Network and the Market: What Lunar ATC Puts Inside Each Vehicle and How Its Marketplace Works

Azizjon Rahimov
Roatify Research · Preprint, October 2026; revised November 2026 · Code, data and pre-registration: github.com/azizjonrahimov/Lunar-Haul · Live site: azizjonrahimov.github.io/Lunar-Haul

Abstract. The rovers, hoppers and landers planned for a lunar south-pole base in 2028–2032 have different owners, carry far less than the base needs, and share no navigation or time standard. We ask what the robot-run job market of our companion papers needs in each vehicle, whether that fits, and how its money works among distrustful owners. We collected the vehicles' capabilities, described the radio, navigation, time, map and security layers, timed our implementation, priced four payment rules over 1,080 simulated years, and ran pre-registered experiments. Scaled by 54.8 to flight hardware, the safety check takes 41.8 % of one core; one robot's bid takes 0.29 s (99th percentile) at today's fleet and 1.19 s at eight times it, above the one-second decision. Twenty robots' authenticated traffic, 7.86 kbit/s at the sources, would need 10.06 kbit/s of a base-scheduled channel if every robot were within one hop of the base; a driving robot has a path to it 57 % of the time. Every owner beat working alone under every rule, but owner C fell below the no-payment market under every paid rule; the bid rules ran a deficit. First-price shading gained at most 7.7 M USD a year, interval including zero (H5's first clause not supported; the other two supported). Ranging holds the 95th percentile of position error at 4.2 m where two or more base beacons are in line of sight, 23 % of driving steps, and fails under the registered wording, all driving (50.1 m); terrain fixes leave 15.2 m elsewhere. Authentication adds 52 % to the bit rate; senders declare frame, datum and time scale, and reports carry a 95 % bound, not a covariance. Designers can budget a vehicle's share of such a network; operators can price a payment rule. Nothing is measured on the Moon.

Keywords: lunar surface operations, multi-owner robot fleets, mechanism design, surface radio network, lunar positioning and time, flight software sizing

1. Introduction

The vehicles planned to drive, hop and land at a lunar south-pole base between 2028 and 2032 have names, and none of them has flown. NASA ordered two crew rovers in May 2026, Astrolab's CLV-1 and Lunar Outpost's Pegasus, and stated in October 2026 what they will do: carry up to 80 kg of samples and tools, drive 10 km on a charge and 6 km a day when driven from Earth [1]. Cargo-class rovers that could move a tonne or two exist only as company designs [2, 3]. Four hopping drones are planned for 2028 [1], of about 250 kg each according to press reports [7], a nuclear-powered rover was added for late 2028 in November 2026 [107], a small cargo rover of 250 kg is a catalogue product [3], and the landers of the period put between 150 kg and 3 t on the surface at a time [11, 12, 13, 14]; the upper figure is from a secondary reference page [11]. NASA's own planning puts the base's recurring need at 2,500–10,000 kg a year and single elements at 500–15,000 kg, and says that demand and mobility capacity are mismatched by 1,000 to 15,000 kg per asset [5, 6]. The fleet of the period is small, its vehicles are owned by different companies, and no two of them are built to talk to each other.

The radio that would connect them is standardised only in part, and nothing built to that standard has worked on the Moon. The LunaNet Interoperability Specification fixes the links between orbit and the surface and between the Moon and Earth, routes IP and bundle-protocol traffic from surface users, and marks surface Wi-Fi and cellular links as not part of LunaNet 1.0, with their physical layer “bilaterally defined” [15]. The space agencies' surface-wireless standard of May 2024 has since fixed Wi-Fi 6 and LTE Release 12 for that link [97]; what two vehicles of different owners say to each other over it is in no space standard. The one cellular network flown to the Moon ran for about 25 minutes on a lander lying on its side and never completed its call [17]. NASA's surface-relay concept reaches about 10 km from a 10 m mast where terrain allows, and its viewshed study found a single 4 m tower covering 10.6–23.7 % of a 20 × 20 km polar area [16]. Earth itself is hidden from the pole for about 14 days of every month [16, 19]. Any coordination among these vehicles has to run on the vehicles, over short, terrain-blocked links, with Earth as an occasional witness rather than a controller.

Our two companion papers argued that this is enough. The first put a job market on the robots, run over a mesh computed from 5 m terrain with the base's real Earth windows, and measured how much of the gain from sharing vehicles across owners it keeps under message loss and Earth blackouts [60]. The second gave the robots rules of the road and a small safety program that overrides any planner, checked the rules exhaustively on a discrete model and in simulated encounters, and priced them in cargo delay [61]. Both papers took three things for granted: that each vehicle can carry the software and the radio the design needs, that a base computer and its spare can hold the shared reservation table and the ledger, and that owners will accept a market in which their robots bid against one another for work. This paper is about those three assumptions. We call the design Lunar ATC, by analogy with air-traffic control, though its decisions are made by the vehicles and a base computer on the Moon rather than by controllers.

Each assumption has a literature that stops short of our case. Multi-robot autonomy for flight exists in one system, CADRE, three rovers of one owner that elect a leader over a mesh and run a central planner on it, with a lightweight distributed database [26, 27, 28]; its mesh radios share at least 1 Mbit/s of bandwidth [28], and their band and range are not public [10]. Field robotics in denied communication, the DARPA Subterranean Challenge, settled on mesh radios, droppable relays, data mules and time budgets out of contact, and the winning team fielded single-robot autonomy only [54, 55]. Runtime-assurance filters have measured computation costs, with implicit filters 11–13 times costlier than explicit switching on a docking problem [45], but no one has sized a safety program for a lunar vehicle's computer. Mechanism design says that efficiency, truthful bidding, individual rationality and a balanced budget cannot all be had at once [29, 30, 31], and freight carriers who collaborate share the gain by Shapley value and name a fair rule and a trusted coordinator as their main obstacles [35, 36]; ride-hailing data put the cost of fragmentation among operators at 2.5–67 % of fleet per extra operator, falling with demand density [39]. Multi-vendor robot fleets negotiate traffic in practice in Open-RMF, which reports high processor load during negotiation and has no stable wire protocol [41]. Robotics task allocation otherwise assumes cooperative robots; the closest work on self-interested robots is about paths, not jobs [34]. We found no study that sizes the on-vehicle software and radio of a multi-owner robot network against the vehicles that will actually fly, and none that measures a robot market's payments, fairness and manipulability in a logistics model.

Our question is therefore in three parts. What do the vehicles of 2028–2032 carry, and what can they not carry at all? What does Lunar ATC put inside each vehicle, how much processor time, memory, radio bandwidth and power does it need as implemented, and what happens when the base computer fails? And under which payment rules is every owner better off in the shared market than alone, how much can an owner gain by misreporting its costs, and what do audits from Earth recover?

We answered the first part from published sources, with every figure flagged by provenance and the contradictions between sources recorded. We answered the second by instrumenting our own implementation: one function decides every move and reservation, one auction runs the market, one message layer carries bids, grants, status and ledger entries over the mesh, and one base computer with a spare holds the shared table and the ledger. We measured these on the laptop that ran the companion experiments, at one, four and eight times today's fleet, and scaled the results to a radiation-tolerant processor by a stated factor; we swept the base computer's failure rate and measured how long the fleet is without a leader at an assumed re-election delay. We answered the third with a market experiment, E46, in which three owners with equal and with 50/35/15 shares of the cargo run the pooled market under four payment rules over 30 seeds, and in which one owner scales its bids by 0.8 to 1.25 while the others bid honestly, with Earth charging the expected penalty of an audit of 0, 5 or 20 % of the jobs afterwards. The hypotheses, H5 on incentives and H10 on sizing, were written down before any run [59].

A revision of November 2026 adds a fourth part, asked by the same reasoning one layer down. A market and rules of the road assume that every vehicle knows where it is, what time it is and what the ground looks like, in terms that another owner's vehicle shares, and that a message can be believed. None of that is given on the Moon. No navigation satellite is in lunar orbit, no lunar time scale is realised, the public maps do not resolve what stops a rover, and no space standard says what two rovers of different owners say to each other; the two terrestrial standards for robots of mixed vendors that our message set follows are named in Section 3.8. We set out the layers Lunar ATC needs under its market, position, time, a shared map in one frame and a communication stack with authentication, each with its numbers and where they come from. Where a quantity can be derived we derive it, with its assumption (Appendix A); where it cannot, we test it in an experiment whose hypothesis was written down first [67]. Some of those hypotheses turned out to be arithmetic on assumed inputs, which could not have come out otherwise; an internal referee round found them, and they are reported here as design calculations and not as tests. Section 8 lists every claim of the paper as proved, tested, calculated from assumed inputs, assumed or not yet tested.

The results are these. As implemented, the safety check fits the reference flight processor: it takes 41.8 % of one core, from one function timed on a laptop and scaled by an assumed factor. The on-robot data are estimated at 0.4 MB and measured on the laptop at 0.13 MB at most. The bidding does not fit at every fleet size: one robot's bid computation in an auction round, timed on a laptop in one month of one seed and scaled by the same factor, takes 0.29 s at the 99th percentile at today's fleet, within the one-second decision, and 1.09 s and 1.19 s at four and eight times the fleet, above it. The authenticated radio traffic of 20 robots, 7.86 kbit/s at the sources, would need 10.06 kbit/s of a channel that the base schedules if every robot were within one hop of the base, within the 16 kbit/s target, and 21.87 kbit/s on slotted random access and 19.64 kbit/s if every report were relayed two hops, both above it. The one-hop condition is taken from positions that are parked in 97 % of vehicle-steps; a driving vehicle has a radio path to the base 56.6 % of the time, so the condition holds for robots standing at sites and not for a fleet on the move (Sections 5.1 to 5.3). In the market, every rule made every owner at least as well off as alone in both splits, though owner C was worse off than under no payments under every paid rule; the operator ran a deficit under first price, a deficit under the second price and a balanced budget under the posted price (Sections 5.5 to 5.7). H5's first clause, that first-price bids reward shading, is not supported; its second, that the second price reduces the gain, is supported; its third, that the posted price with Shapley sharing leaves no owner worse off than alone, is supported (Section 5.7). Of the layers under the market, with the hypotheses of [67]: dead reckoning alone passes the 5 m separation budget within the first driving hour (H9′, first clause, supported). Whether ranging holds the budget near the base (second clause) depends on the reading of the registered words: the 95th percentile is 4.22 m where two or more of the four beacons are in line of sight, 23.1 % of driving steps, 24.7 m within a kilometre of the base, and 50.1 m under the registered wording taken literally, where it fails in every seed. Terrain fixes leave 15.2 m elsewhere, and 7.54 m even with a navigation service as assumed for 2030 (third clause, not supported); with the fix stops inside the engine the delay per delivery rises by 0.63 h, 3.5 %, a lower bound from an exploratory run. A crystal oscillator resynchronised whenever the base is heard caused no reservation conflict with a guard of 0.1 s in 303 reservation pairs (H11's first half, observed); that its error exceeds the ranging radio's noise follows from the assumed transfer residual and is a design calculation. Two registered hypotheses are design calculations and are reported as such: by the assumed change rate the changed tiles of a day are 1.06 % of a full resend (H12), and 20 rovers load the busiest link with 7.86 kbit/s at 1 Hz, their sizes times their rates (H13). Authentication adds 52 % to the bit rate of protocol version 0.1. Its position report named neither frame, datum nor time scale; version 0.2 has each sender declare them under its key and puts a 95 % bound in every report, at 8.37 kbit/s for the same 20 rovers, and carries no covariance. Store-and-forward with a lost-link rule lost no job where drop-and-retry lost 0.33 a seed (H14's first clause, supported; 10 awards of 6,028, at a retry budget we chose); its second clause is not testable as written, and the rule now marks a silent holder's segment unconfirmed instead of releasing its reservation. A ledger bundle that lives 14 days does not outlast an Earth outage of 24.8 days, so 11 of the month's 31 daily bundles expire with store-and-forward and none with a lifetime of 25.8 days, a calculation from the calendar.

Our contributions are:

  • A provenance-flagged account of the lunar vehicles of 2028–2032 and the surface radio network, with the capacity plateau the fleet implies and the contradictions between sources recorded rather than resolved.
  • A bill of materials for Lunar ATC inside one vehicle: the safety program, the planner, the radio unit and the local copies of the reservation table and ledger, with the processor time of the safety check and of one robot's bid computation timed on our implementation and scaled to flight hardware by a stated factor, the memory estimated and measured on a laptop, the radio traffic computed from event counts and serialised sizes, and failover simulated.
  • Market rules for owners who do not trust one another, with the money flow from bid to settlement on Earth, and a measurement of individual rationality, manipulation by bid scaling and audits under four payment rules.
  • The layers under the market, with their provenance: what a vehicle carries to know where it is and what time it is, one named frame and one base-held map, and a communication stack that reuses existing standards up to the application layer, where an authenticated interaction protocol is implemented and serialised; each layer has a pre-registered experiment, reported clause by clause as supported, not supported, dependent on a reading, or a design calculation.
  • Eight derivations with their assumptions (Appendix A), each checked by the build against the results files, and a table that sorts every claim of the paper into proved, tested, calculated from assumed inputs, assumed and not yet tested (Section 8).

Several things are outside this paper. Which vehicle takes which job, and how the robots' auction behaves under message loss and Earth blackouts, is the subject of [60]; the rules of the road and the checks that the safety program keeps them are the subject of [61]; here both are treated as given and only their cost in computation, memory and bandwidth is sized. The vehicles' internal layouts are published for few of them, and this paper describes the parts Lunar ATC adds, not the vehicles' own avionics. What position error costs the rules of the road, how a vehicle that reports a false position is detected, and how vehicles agree on a shared value are the companion paper's subjects [61]; this paper gives the position error, the clocks, the map and the authentication they rest on. Owners who misreport their position or availability, hog chargers or pick easy jobs are not simulated; the one manipulation tested is bid scaling. The market has three owners; Section 7 says what that leaves open.

The paper has two halves, and a reader may take either alone. The first is the design and the layers under it: the vehicles (Section 3.2), the radio network (3.3), what Lunar ATC puts inside a vehicle (3.4), position, time, the shared map and the communication stack (3.5 to 3.8), their experiments (E48 and E50 to E55 in Section 4.2), their results (Sections 5.1 to 5.4 and 5.8 to 5.13) and the derivations of Appendix A. The second is the market: the payment rules and the money (Section 3.9), the experiment E46 and hypothesis H5 (Sections 4.1 and 4.2), and its results (Sections 5.5 to 5.7). Section 6 discusses both, Section 7 states the limitations, and Table 18 in Section 8 is the shortest way into the paper: it lists every claim with what stands behind it. What was not run is said where its result would go, and again in Sections 6.6 and 7.

2. Related work

2.1 The vehicles and what they carry

NASA's 2024 mobility white paper states the gap in plain numbers: the base's elements weigh 500–15,000 kg and move up to 5,000 m, “current demand and mobility capacity are mismatched on the order of 1,000 to 15,000 kg per asset”, wheel and soil interactions do not scale linearly with vehicle size, and the cooperation of several robots on one load “may be an enabling feature” and a reason to standardise robot interfaces [5]. The same series puts the recurring logistics need at 2,500–10,000 kg a year with occasional deliveries of 12,000–15,000 kg [6]. The original Lunar Terrain Vehicle requirement asked for at least 800 kg of uncrewed cargo [5]; what NASA bought in May 2026 was two crew-sortie rovers of about 907 kg (CLV-1) and a lighter, arm-less Pegasus, and what it stated in October 2026 was 80 kg of samples and tools, 10 km per charge and 6 km a day when driven from Earth [1, 4]. The cargo-class parents of those rovers, FLEX with 2,000 kg on two top-deck interfaces and Eagle with a cargo bed and an arm, are company claims without a contract [2, 3]. Below them sit a 250 kg rover with a 200 kg payload [3], hoppers of about 250 kg that fly about 50 km in 14 days [7] and a 1 m hopper that carries 8–10 kg over 25 km [8]. The closest two-rover cargo carry we found shares 475 kg between two 800 kg rovers with a formation error of 9.2 cm mean and 33.4 cm maximum, over 13.8 kB/s of inter-rover traffic on 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi [25]. No battery size is published for any Lunar Terrain Vehicle; the reference points are VIPER's 5,420 Wh [9] and the Apollo rover's two 121 Ah silver-zinc batteries [64]. Our vehicle table (Section 3.2) is built from these sources; its lesson for this paper is that the fleet's capacity is a plateau set by two uncontracted cargo rovers, and that everything heavier moves only if two vehicles, usually of two owners, lift it together [60]. The policy frame for several owners on one ridge is thin. The Artemis Accords' safety zones ask for notification and coordination, not exclusion [52]; NASA's policy analysis of landing and surface operations finds transit corridors near the pole as narrow as 20–40 m, says that once three or more actors are involved the difficulty of, for example, overlapping safety zones compounds, and quotes a major partner that some level of centralised coordination among actors “may become necessary” [51]; and the one proposal for lunar surface traffic regulation raises priority of passage, passing and signalling without giving rules [53].

2.2 Surface radio

LunaNet standardises proximity links from orbit, direct-with-Earth links, the network layer (IPv4, IPv6 and Bundle Protocol version 7, which is mandatory for delay-tolerant traffic) and the time and navigation signal; it marks “IP over Wi-Fi” and “IP over 3GPP” on the surface as not part of LunaNet 1.0, lists the lunar cellular bands as under investigation, and leaves sub-IP details of surface links “out of scope … bilaterally defined” [15]. Its S-band proximity rates run from 0.5 ksps at the low end to 5 Msps [15]. The delay-tolerant networking that carries the Bundle Protocol became an operational service on NASA's near-space and deep-space networks in January 2026, with no demonstration on the lunar surface yet [22]. NASA's Lunar Surface Relay concept carries cellular sector antennas on a 10 m boom for coverage “out to 10 km, depending on local terrain”, Wi-Fi at about 300 m, two rubidium clocks for surface timing, and a stationary version whose communications draw 131 W; its viewshed study of the Malapert massif found a 4 m tower covering 10.6–23.7 % of a 20 × 20 km area and a 20 m tower 12.1–29.2 %, with only 7 of 400 candidate sites meeting both of its constraints, and warned that moving a tower by about 1 km “could have severe impacts” on coverage [16]. Lander-to-payload Wi-Fi at 2.4 GHz is specified for the Griffin and Peregrine landers in their user's guide of 2021 [18]; Peregrine did not reach the Moon and Griffin has not launched [68]. The space agencies' surface-wireless standard of 2024 fixes Wi-Fi 6 and LTE Release 12 for surface links [97] (Section 3.8). Nokia's cellular network on IM-2 targeted a few hundred metres to 2–5 km with antennas 3–5 m high and ran for about 25 minutes without completing a call, so no measured lunar cellular range or rate exists [17]. CADRE's three rovers carry mesh radios with “a minimum of 1Mbps of aggregate bandwidth” [28] and ultra-wideband ranging, but their band and range are not published [10]. Direct-to-Earth coverage at candidate south-pole sites is 17–43 % of a year, 80–86 % with one relay [19], and the pole does not see Earth for about 14 days a month [16]. The physics behind these numbers is geometry: on a smooth Moon of radius 1,737.4 km the horizon of an antenna at height h is √(2Rh), 2.95 km at 2.5 m and 5.89 km at 10 m, with no atmospheric refraction [63]; rover antennas cannot clear the first Fresnel zone at kilometre ranges (5.6 m radius at 2.4 GHz over 1 km), so links sit near grazing, and a blocked path loses about 6 dB at grazing and 20 dB at a Fresnel parameter of 2.4 under the standard knife-edge model [20]; path loss over regolith “can be much more severe than the free space propagation” and the south-pole regolith's permittivity is unmeasured [21]. Lunar clocks run 56.02 µs a day fast relative to Earth, which a shared time base absorbs [23]. In short, the robot mesh of Lunar ATC has no flight precedent at its scale, and the design has to accept links that fail in steps as a vehicle drives behind a ridge.

2.3 Flight software on robots that coordinate

CADRE is the one multi-robot autonomy system we found prepared for lunar flight: three rovers and a base station on the lander, a centralised planner on an elected leader with distributed execution, a lightweight distributed database, and a mesh radio; the leader is chosen by a spanning-tree election that finds a unique appointer every 10 s, which appoints the agent farthest from its thermal and power limits; the team is without a planner for at most 10 s after it loses its leader; the rovers are awake for 25 minutes in every 30, shut down by hardware to cool; on a leader change, in-progress tasks stop and the new leader replans [26, 27, 28, 106]. It is one owner and science exploration, not logistics, but its leader election and its database are the pattern our base-computer failover follows. The DARPA Subterranean Challenge shows how teams coped with denied communication: mesh radios with four to six deployed nodes, effective ranges of 49–1,058 m, maximum delays of 1–13 s, robots acting as relays and data mules, and a warning that requiring ordered delivery of mission-critical topics built large queues after reconnection [54]; the winning team ran out-of-contact exploration on a time budget and deployed single-robot autonomy only [55]. Open-RMF lets fleets of unrelated vendors publish intended trajectories to a shared schedule and negotiate conflicts peer to peer, with the explicit goal that no vendor changes its fleet manager; it has no stable wire protocol yet, and users report high processor load during negotiation [41]. The computation of a safety filter is measured in the runtime-assurance literature: on a spacecraft-docking problem, explicit optimisation cost 3.3 times explicit switching, and implicit filters that simulate the backup trajectory online 11–13 times [45]; the tutorial of Hobbs and colleagues lists computation time among the costs a safety layer must report [46]. Our safety program is explicit switching in the Simplex form [47, 48], chosen for that reason, and Section 5.1 reports its cost per call. Drone traffic management gives the closest measured latency we found of a federated coordination service among operators: 189 ms on average and 95 % of exchanges within 531 ms over all message types, but a 95th percentile of 3,908 ms for negotiation messages, the type nearest an auction and the only one that missed the test's minimum, with operational impact traced to downed servers and misimplemented specifications rather than latency [66]. Two terrestrial standards let robots of mixed vendors share a floor: VDA 5050, whose topics are an order, instant actions, a state, a visualisation, a connection and a factsheet [117], and the MassRobotics interoperability standard, with an identity report and a status report [118]; the message set of Section 3.8 follows them. Decentralised auctions over lossy links have known degradation curves [42, 43, 44]; their message counts are reported per round, not per robot-hour, and never against a link budget.

2.4 Markets among owners who do not trust one another

When robots belong to different companies, an allocator is also a market, and the theory is unkind. For a single buyer and seller with private values, no incentive-compatible, individually rational mechanism is efficient without an outside subsidy [29]. The Vickrey–Clarke–Groves mechanism is truthful and efficient [30], its payment rule is in general not budget-balanced (a standard result that our research note credits to Green and Laffont, 1977, and that we did not re-read), and with few bidders it is open to collusion by losing bidders and to shill identities [31]. Trade-reduction double auctions buy truthfulness and budget safety by dropping the least valuable trade, which guarantees at least 1 − 1/q of the gains with q efficient trades, cheap in thick markets and expensive in a market of three owners [32]. When the mechanism can observe what an agent actually did, truthful mechanisms exist where unverifiable ones fail [33] (cited as the standard result; the paper was not re-read for this study); a rover's odometry, energy log and the shared trace make its costs checkable after the fact, which is the basis of the audits in Section 3.9. The closest mechanism-design study of self-interested robots that we found covers paths, not jobs, and finds that VCG needs an optimal solver, that approximate solvers lose strategyproofness unless maximal in range, and that three strategyproof, individually rational mechanisms work on dozens to hundreds of agents [34]. Freight carriers who collaborate save about 20–30 % in joint route planning, name “a reliable party to lead” and “a fair allocation of benefits” as the highest impediments [35], mostly share the gain by Shapley value [36, 37], and run request exchanges in five phases, selection, bundling, bidding, winner determination and profit sharing, under the constraint that no carrier's profit falls [38]; a survey of 120 such studies finds carrier alliances the most studied form and the managerial and technical issues of putting one into practice the least [65]. Ride-hailing data give the price of not coordinating: m operators raise the fleet by (m − 1)·D, with D from 2.52 % in Manhattan to 67.14 % in Curitiba per extra operator, and with 50/35/15 shares in Singapore the smallest operator paid a 48.05 % fleet penalty against 8.48 % for the largest [39]; the theory predicts that unequal shares can make the excess grow linearly [40]. Our market experiment uses the same three-owner, 50/35/15 split, measures what each owner earns against working alone, reports the operator's budget rather than assuming it balanced, and tests one manipulation, bid scaling, with and without audits.

2.5 Our earlier work and the gap

Our first paper built a discrete-event simulation of cargo movement at a south-pole base and a pooled dispatch marketplace in which vehicles bid their marginal cost in hours [56]. The second added landing precision, pad standoff, a plume keep-out window and rover autonomy on NASA's 5 m terrain of the Connecting Ridge [57]. The third computed the base's Earth-link windows from ephemerides and terrain and priced their gaps [58]. The two companion papers of this series moved dispatch onto the robots over a terrain-limited mesh with learned bids [60] and gave the vehicles rules of the road enforced by a safety program checked three ways [61]. None of them said what a vehicle must carry to take part, or what owners are paid.

The pieces exist separately: a flight leader election and database for one owner, measured mesh behaviour underground, a negotiation protocol for mixed vendors on Earth, measured filter costs for one spacecraft, and mechanism design for carriers and cars. 3. The vehicles, the network and the design

This section has the paper's two halves in order. Sections 3.1 to 3.8 are the design and the layers under it: the vehicles of 2028–2032 as published, the radio network as it stands and as we model it, what Lunar ATC puts inside each vehicle, how a vehicle knows where it is and what time it is, the map the vehicles share, and the communication stack with its security. Section 3.9 is the market: how jobs are bid for, how they are paid and how the money is settled. Provenance follows the convention of [57, 60]: Measured values come from a cited measurement or document, Spec from a published specification or a company claim, Derived (or Modelled) from a computation on such values, and Assumed values are our choices, each swept in Section 4 or named in Section 7. Results of this study are never flagged; they come from the result files listed under Availability. Where the sources we reached hold no value, the text says so rather than supplying one.

3.1 The base and the shared engine

Everything in this paper runs on the engine of the companion papers [60, 61], which extends the discrete-event simulation of [56, 57, 58]. The base is on the Connecting Ridge between Shackleton and de Gerlache craters, on NASA Goddard's 5 m terrain tile (Measured). Its sites are the habitat and control centre (BASE), a cargo depot, a power plant, a construction site, an oxygen pilot plant, a science site and a permanently shadowed crater, with two landing pads 1 km from the base. Demand follows the landings and bulk flows of [56], scaled so that the two cargo-class vehicles are busy about 30 % of the time (load factor 2.806, Assumed, calibrated in [60]); landed cargo belongs to the owner who ordered it; a tenth of landed items are team items that need two vehicles (Assumed). The pooled marketplace of [56] offers every job to every vehicle in auction rounds, each vehicle bids the marginal cost in hours of adding the job to its plan, weighted by the item's urgency, and the best bid wins; [60] runs those rounds on the robots over the radio mesh of Section 3.3 and adds a learned correction to the bids; [61] adds the rules of the road and the safety program. With every bid multiplier at 1 the market run reproduces the pooled auction of [60] exactly, and the E48 timers leave the event trace bit-identical (Section 3.10).

The capacity plateau. The largest single payload in the fleet is 2,000 kg, the largest pair that can couple lifts 2,800 kg together (the two-rover carry of [25] is the closest measured case we found, at about 0.3 of the pair's combined mass), and the fleet's eight vehicles carry 3,380 kg in all if every one is loaded at once. Against that, NASA puts single base elements at 500–15,000 kg and occasional deliveries at 12,000–15,000 kg [5, 6], and the landers of the period put 150 kg (Blue Ghost [13]), 455 kg (Griffin as tested [12]), 1.5 t (Argonaut, from 2030–2031 [14]) and 3 t (Blue Moon MK1 [11]) on the surface at a time. Nothing heavier than 2,800 kg moves at all in this fleet, whatever the dispatcher does; the companion paper found that most of the value of sharing vehicles across owners is cargo that owners alone never deliver [60]. The plateau is a fact about the fleet, not about Lunar ATC, and we state it here because the sizing of Section 3.4 is for these vehicles and not for the cargo rovers NASA has not yet bought. Larger fleets in Section 4.2 multiply every vehicle class and the demand by the same factor.

3.3 The radio network

Earth. Earth is in view of the base when any part of its disc is above the terrain horizon of a 2.5 m antenna at BASE, the criterion of [58]; the windows are those of [58] for 2028–2031 and the same pipeline's windows to 2046 after that (Modelled). A message to Earth takes 2.6 s round trip plus 2.4 s of ground processing (Measured for the light time, Assumed for the processing, as in [56]). Earth in this paper never dispatches; it publishes prices and rules, receives the ledger, settles accounts and audits (Section 3.9).

LunaNet. The surface links of Lunar ATC are not LunaNet links. LunaNet 1.0 standardises the links between orbit and the surface and the network layer above any surface radio, and leaves the surface wireless link to bilateral agreement [15]; the surface-wireless standard of the space agencies has since fixed its radio (Section 3.8). Lunar ATC therefore assumes only what LunaNet provides to a surface user: IP routing, the Bundle Protocol for anything that can wait, and a shared time base from the navigation signal or the relay's clocks [15, 16] (Assumed that every vehicle holds it; a free-running oscillator would drift by about 86 ms a day at 1 ppm, Assumed; Section 3.6 gives the one crystal measured on the lunar surface and what each use of time needs). The robot-to-robot link below IP is ours, and Section 3.4 states what it carries. Its physical layer is left open by this paper; the link budget of Section 4.2 (E48) is computed at one frequency, 2.4 GHz, the Wi-Fi-class radio specified for the Griffin lander [18] (Provisional), with transmit powers of 1.0 W for the crew and cargo rovers, 0.25 W for the small rover, 0.5 W for the hopper and 5 W for the base mast (Assumed, no source). A 400 MHz UHF fallback would lose less at knife-edge diffraction over a low ridge [20], and a 390–405 MHz lunar band is in draft [24], but it is not budgeted here.

The mesh. Two robots can talk directly when their antennas see each other over the terrain and are within range. The mesh is the data product of [60]: line of sight for every pair of 2,421 points (the sites, the pads and samples every 100 m along every route) on the 5 m terrain with the Moon's curvature and no refraction, for antennas 2.5 m high on rovers and 1.0 m on small robots and hoppers (Assumed) and a 10 m mast at BASE that carries the base computer (Assumed, after the surface-relay concept of [16]). The range is 5 km and the clearance margin 0 m (Assumed), so a ray that grazes the ground counts as a link. Line of sight is scarce on this ridge: two rover antennas within 500 m see each other in 50.5 % of point pairs and at 2–5 km in 9.3 %; for two 1 m antennas at 2–5 km the share is 6.3 %. A stricter test is in the same file and is not used by the mesh: with 0.6 of the first Fresnel zone clear at the worst point, the share for two rover antennas at 2–5 km falls to 5.4 % at 2.4 GHz and to 1.8 % at 400 MHz, where the zone is wider (Assumed threshold). The mast sees 23.3 % of the route points directly, none of them beyond 2 km from the base. Rovers nevertheless form one connected network from 2 km range upward; at 5 km 23.3 % of the points reach the mast in one hop, 58.1 % in two, and every point in at most four (median two); small robots leave four points with no neighbour at all within 2 km and need up to five hops. A message hop takes 10 ms (Assumed, terrestrial practice; no lunar measurement exists) and is lost with probability p, independently or in bursts of mean length 10 messages (Assumed), with p = 0.10 in the runs of [60] and in E48. E46 runs with p = 0 by design, a perfect radio (Section 4.2). A robot that nobody hears drives to a rally point that sees the mast (Assumed; a lost-link procedure every robot carries).

Hops: the terrain and the fleet. The median of two hops is over all 2,421 route points, each counted once. The fleet does not stand on them evenly: in the one-month trace of Section 4.2 the robots with a path to the base computer were 1.024 hops from it on average, because the work is near the base, and 5.6 % of the robot-epochs had no path at all and are in no average. Both numbers are true; the first describes the terrain and the second where this fleet stands. The second is a mean over positions at decision epochs, and in the recorded month a vehicle is parked in 97 % of its steps. While it drives it has a radio path to the base in 56.6 % of its steps, against 93.2 % over all time (E54, Section 5.12). What follows is that the one-hop condition of the link claim (Section 5.2) holds for robots standing at sites within reach of the mast, and not for a vehicle on the road nor for a fleet spread over the routes. E53 measures the other case directly (Section 5.11): with 20 rovers spread uniformly over the route samples the rovers that reach the base need 1.8 hops on average, the busiest link carries 58 % of the load it carries when the fleet works near the base, and only 62 % of the rovers reach the base at all.

Why the numbers are what they are. On a smooth Moon the horizon of a 2.5 m antenna is 2.95 km, of a 1.0 m antenna 1.86 km and of a 10 m mast 5.89 km (Proposition 1), so two rovers can never see each other beyond 5.89 km, two small robots beyond 3.73 km, and a rover cannot see the mast beyond 8.84 km [63]; the mast's direct view above reaches less than half of that because the ridge is not smooth; the relay viewsheds of [16] show the same kind of loss at another site, and the two were not matched (Section 6.2). One difference between the mesh and the link budget has to be stated. The mesh keeps pairs of 1.0 m antennas up to 5 km apart wherever the terrain shows line of sight, although the smooth-Moon horizon of two such antennas is 3.73 km. Both are right: the ridge is not a sphere, and two antennas on two rises see each other across a valley farther than a sphere allows, while two on a plain do not. The horizon check is therefore off on the terrain mesh and on in Table 7; switched on in the mesh it would remove 6.9 % of the links between 1.0 m antennas and none between rover antennas. Under the two-ray law every link of the mesh closes 16 kbit/s at margins of 3 and 10 dB for every class; at 15 dB 94.2 % of the small rover's links and 99.1 % of the hopper's do, and in the month's trace of Section 4.2 no link in use is lost either way (Assumed on paths the terrain shows as visible: no path loss has been measured on the Moon). A rover antenna cannot clear the first Fresnel zone at kilometre ranges (5.6 m at 2.4 GHz over 1 km, 13.7 m at 400 MHz), so a link in line of sight still sits near grazing; beyond a two-ray breakpoint of about 128 m at 2.4 GHz for 2 m antennas, loss grows about 40 log d instead of 20 log d; free-space loss alone is 100 dB at 1 km and 120 dB at 10 km at 2.4 GHz, and 84.5 and 104.5 dB at 400 MHz (all Derived in the research note from standard formulae [20, 63]). The lunar regolith's permittivity at the pole is unmeasured [21]. The link budget of E48 therefore reports a data rate at the worst hop under the flat-ground two-ray law, with the rate under free-space loss beside it as an upper bound (Section 4.2), and not a measured range.

3.4 What Lunar ATC puts inside each vehicle

Lunar ATC is four pieces of software and the radio that carries their messages. Table 3 lists them per vehicle class, with what E48 times, counts or estimates of each (Section 4.2). The design is ours; the parts of the vehicles themselves, their avionics, batteries and motor controllers, are not described here beyond what Section 3.2 needs, because few of their layouts are published.

The safety program. One function, safety_check(state, proposal), is the only place where the rules of the road of [61] are decided. It answers two kinds of proposal. A reservation proposal asks whether this vehicle may hold a resource, a one-lane road segment, a junction tile or a pad apron, for a time window, and is decided on the reservation entries the vehicle can see; a reservation that has not been confirmed is never assumed [49], so a lost message can only add delay. A move proposal is the planner's acceleration for the next step with the vehicle's kinematic situation, and is accepted only if, after the step, every rule still holds with the vehicle braking at its guaranteed deceleration; otherwise the first safe candidate, no acceleration and then full braking, is substituted. It is explicit switching in the Simplex form [47], a post-posed shield [48]: it never plans, it only refuses. The same function is called by the logistics model at job commit, by the encounter simulator at 10 Hz for every vehicle, and by the model checker for every move [61], so what E48 times is what was checked. Its state is the vehicle's own reservations, the broadcasts it has heard and the published constraints: landing closures, crew zones, the priority ladder.

The planner. The planner may be anything; the safety program does not trust it. In this study it is the nominal driving logic of [57] for moves and, for bids, the marginal-cost bid of the marketplace [56] with the learned correction of [60]: a linear model of 13 weights, which was the model that improved on hand-built bids, or a multilayer network of 1,537 parameters, which did not. Both are read from the policy files; both are small enough that the question for sizing is the auction's bookkeeping, not the arithmetic of a bid.

The radio unit. The unit carries two kinds of traffic, and they travel differently. State reports, position, status and intent, are broadcast one hop, in the time slots the base schedules, to whoever is in view; they are not relayed. Events, bids and winners' rebroadcasts, awards, reservation requests and grants, team-lift proposals, answers and commits, alerts and ledger entries, are relayed hop by hop through other robots or the mast until they reach their addressee, with the loss and latency of Section 3.3. The first version of this paper said that the unit floods all of this hop by hop. That cannot stand beside the finding of Section 5.2: the 16 kbit/s target holds only when the reports travel one hop on a scheduled channel, and only the events, which are a small share of the bytes, can afford relaying. A robot in a part of the network that holds the mast knows every waiting item; a robot elsewhere knows only the items at sites where one of its part's robots stands [60]. The report rates are stated once, here, and every traffic figure of this paper uses them: the position report goes at 1 Hz, which is the conformance broadcast of [61], and the status and intent reports at 0.1 Hz, the protocol's default (Assumed). Section 5.2 gives the source load if all three went at 1 Hz. E48 does not serialise these messages; it counts the traffic from each run's events. Every road traversal produces one reservation request, one grant and one release, every auction round its bid, award and claim, every landing one announcement, and every robot one status message a second (Assumed rate, after rule R9 of [61]); each type has an assumed byte layout, ids, times and positions as 32-bit values under an 8 B header (Assumed; no serialiser is flown). The winners' rebroadcasts, the team-lift proposals, answers and commits, and the ledger entries are not in that traffic; the ledger is sized separately from the year's jobs. The airtime of Section 4.2 uses a second, authenticated layout: around its content every message carries a packet header of 6 B, a protocol header of 8 B, a security header of 6 B and a message authentication code of 8 B, 28 B in all (36 B with a code of 16 B); grants and awards carry a signature of 64 B in place of the code; the 1 Hz status message becomes a position report at 1 Hz with status and intent reports at 0.1 Hz, and a landing announcement is sent four times (Design, the interaction-protocol draft in the repository's research notes; not flown, and E48 does not serialise it either). The design targets we set before measuring were 100 B/s per robot, a 16 kbit/s-class link for 20 robots, and under 1 MB a day of market messages (Assumed; hypothesis H10 of [59]). The link target is compared with the airtime the traffic needs on a shared channel, not with the load at the sources alone (Section 4.2; change 11 of [59]). For comparison, the closest measured inter-rover coordination load we found, a two-rover convoy controller, used 13.8 kB/s [25].

Local copies. Each vehicle keeps a copy of the reservation entries it has heard and of the ledger entries it has produced or relayed, so that it can check a proposal while out of contact (rule R7 of [61]: a vehicle out of contact keeps the reservations it holds and takes no new ones) and can give Earth its own account of every job. E48 gives two figures for them. One is an estimate of the on-robot data: the bytes of a 24-hour window of the reservation table and of a year's ledger copy at the assumed layouts, the two policy files as stored, and an allowance for working data. The other is a measurement on the laptop of what the interpreter allocates for the same parts while they run. Neither counts the program, its interpreter or its stack (Section 5.1).

The base computer and its spare. The base computer on the 10 m mast holds the shared reservation table, the manifests of waiting items, the market's rounds when the leader regime runs them, and the ledger that Earth settles from. It has a spare board; in the model the computer fails as a process with a mean time between failures of 2,000 h and a repair time of 24 h (both Assumed, swept in E48). While it is down the robots elect a leader among themselves, the robot that reaches the most others over the mesh, after a re-election delay of 0.5 h (Assumed; the value was taken from CADRE's half-hour cycle of waking and cooling, which is not its election time: CADRE selects an appointer every 10 s and is without a planner for at most 10 s after it loses its leader [106], so 0.5 h is pessimistic for an election of that kind; E48 does not measure this delay, it copies the assumed value and measures the share of time without a leader at it); robots outside the leader's part of the network act on their last instructions and, when idle, drive to the rally point. The leader runs the market for the robots it reaches; the reservation table it holds is the union of what those robots have heard. When the base computer returns after repair, or two parts of the network merge, the base computer's state is rebuilt from the robots: what merges is the assignment table, by the rule that an earlier commit keeps its reservation and a job is never assigned twice [61]. E48 checks after every rebuild that no job is assigned twice. The ledger itself is not simulated; its size is estimated from the year's jobs (Section 4.2). The auction regime of [60] needs no leader: nothing waits for one, and the base computer's failure only removes the mast from the mesh.

3.5 Position without GPS

What exists. No satellite navigation service covers the Moon. NASA, ESA and JAXA are building one interoperable service, the LunaNet Lunar Augmented Navigation Service, whose satellites all send the same signal near 2.49 GHz [77, 114]. As of 11 November 2026 none of its satellites is in lunar orbit. NASA's 2024 plan put a first navigation signal in September 2026, two by mid-2028 and four by September 2029 [78]; the first satellite is to ride with the IM-3 lander according to a secondary source [119], and that lander's launch has moved to the first quarter of 2027 [81]. ESA states initial operations of its Moonlight service for the end of 2028 and full operations for 2030 [79], and JAXA's slides of February 2026 list a demonstration in its fiscal year 2029 [80] (the slide's text did not let us tie its later years to their milestones). The providers' own simulation of a five-satellite configuration at 87° S gives a horizontal error of 17.2 m (95 %) when the receiver takes its height from a terrain model and 121.6 m when it does not [77] (Simulation). Earth's own navigation signals were received on the lunar surface in March 2025, with point fixes wrong by 0.1 to 100 km [82] (Measured): they help with time, not with driving. A base that opens in 2028 therefore cannot depend on a navigation service, and the availability we assume for later years (Table 4) is our reading of the providers' plans, not a commitment by them.

The layered scheme. Lunar ATC asks every vehicle for position in layers, each bounding the error of the one below (Design, [68]). Layer 0 is one named frame and one named time scale (Sections 3.6 and 3.7). Layer 1 is dead reckoning, which every vehicle carries: wheel odometry, an inertial unit, a star tracker for heading and cameras that track the ground. Field tests on Earth for the Mars rovers gave 1 to 2.5 % of the distance driven with visual odometry [69] (Measured on Earth), and the lunar crater-matching work takes “typically under 2 %” as a design assumption [70, 71]; on loose or sloped ground wheel odometry alone is 10 % or worse [72] (Measured on Earth). Layer 2 is two-way ranging between vehicles and to surveyed beacons, which needs no shared clock (Proposition 4) and has been measured at 0.1 to 0.7 m over tens of metres on Earth [75], never on the Moon. Layer 3 is a fix against the orbital map: the vehicle matches craters or terrain it sees to the map it carries. This is the one layer with flight proof, on Mars, where Perseverance has matched panoramas to orbital images on board since February 2026 [73] (Measured); the lunar versions are designed for under 5 m in sunlight and were within 5 m in most dark test runs [70, 71] (Spec; simulation and field tests). Layer 4 is the navigation service when it exists. A receiver has to be able to widen its margins instead of trusting a number, so a position must come with its frame, its datum, its time scale, an uncertainty and the layers that contributed. The protocol does this in the following way, and not by putting every item in every report (Section 3.8 says exactly what is on the wire): the report itself carries a one-sigma value, a 95 % horizontal bound and a code for the source; the frame, the datum and the time scale are declared by each sender and bound to its key, and a report from a sender without a declaration is refused. A covariance is not carried. The first version of the protocol, with which the experiments were run, had neither the bound nor the declaration. The model of E50 gives each layer one number (Table 4). Dead reckoning drifts by 2 % of the distance driven on firm level ground, 10 % on loose soil or slopes above 10° and 3 % in shadow, with a star tracker holding the heading to 0.1°. A two-way range is good to 0.3 m within 100 m and to 3 m over the base radio out to 5 km, to four beacons surveyed to 0.15 m and to at most four other vehicles in line of sight. A terrain fix is good to 1.7 m in sunlight and 3 m in the dark, where 30 % of attempts are taken to fail (Assumed); an opportunity comes every 100 m in sunlight and every 500 m in shadow, costs a stop of 120 s, and is taken when the vehicle's own 95 % bound exceeds 2.5 m; a vehicle that arrives at a site takes one there. The navigation service is given the providers' target of 10 m (95 %) with the height taken from the map [79], where their own simulation gives 17.2 m [77], for a share of each day of 0 in 2028, 0.4 in 2029, 0.6 in 2030 and 0.8 from 2031 (both Assumed). The budget all of this is held against is 5 m, the distance rule R1 of [61] keeps free for position uncertainty. Proposition 8 gives the bound that beacon geometry puts on a single epoch of ranges with every beacon in view; the layer as E50 simulates it is a sequential filter with a dead-reckoning prior and is not bound by it.

3.6 Time

One named scale. Every sender of Lunar ATC messages declares the time scale of its time stamps, and the base keeps one lunar scale that the vehicles follow (Design, [68]). A message carries one time stamp, in tenths of a second; the scale it is in is not a field of the message but part of the sender's declaration, which a receiver must hold before it accepts a position from that sender (Section 3.8). The first version of the protocol had no such declaration. The scale has to be named because lunar and terrestrial seconds differ: a clock on the lunar surface runs fast against the same clock on Earth's geoid by 56.02 µs a day. Three independent derivations give 56.0199 [23], 56.025 [83] and 56.0256 µs a day [84]. The White House memorandum of 2 April 2024, which asked NASA for a strategy for lunar time by 31 December 2026, quotes 58.7 µs a day [85]; that figure matches the intermediate value of [23] that leaves out the Moon's own gravitational potential, 2.71 µs a day. Proposition 3 derives the offset term by term and gives 56.02 µs a day, a rate of 6.4844 × 10−10, which is the constant the clock model of E51 uses; left uncorrected it is 16.8 km of light time a day, and without the Moon's own potential it would be 58.7 µs a day. No standard realises a lunar scale yet: LunaNet requires every provider to relate its time to a LunaNet Reference Time whose defining document is not released [15], and on 11 November 2026 we found no public statement that NASA's strategy had been delivered. The scale's identifier is therefore a declared value that can change, not a constant of the design.

Two oscillator classes. Between synchronisations a vehicle keeps time on its own oscillator. The model has two classes. A temperature-compensated crystal has a constant frequency error drawn per vehicle, uniformly between 0 and 8 × 10−7 and so of one sign. That range is the excursion the crystal of one receiver, LuGRE's, showed on the lunar surface as it warmed from 25 to 45 °C [82] (read from a figure's axis); using one crystal's excursion as the spread of constant offsets across vehicles is our modelling choice (Assumed). A chip-scale atomic clock has a frequency error with a standard deviation of 3 × 10−10, its temperature term, and ages by 9 × 10−10 a month [86] (Spec; the data sheet says the model is not rated for space). A vehicle is synchronised by a two-way time transfer whenever it has a radio path to the base mast, which leaves a residual of 100 ns (Assumed) and corrects the offset, not the frequency (Assumed; Section 5.9 reports a variant that steers frequency). A reservation is stamped in the vehicle's own clock and keeps a guard of 0.1 s (Assumed). The crystal's excursion is the one number in this section measured on the Moon, on one receiver; it moved by several parts in 107 within a day with temperature alone [82], and a rover that drives in and out of shadow will see larger swings than a lander did.

What each use needs. The uses of time differ by seven orders of magnitude (Proposition 4; Table 12 lists the budgets). One-way ranging against a beacon's clock turns 1 ns of clock error into 0.30 m, so it needs nanoseconds. Two-way ranging needs no shared clock at all: the offset cancels and only the rate error over the reply delay remains. Radio time slots need 1.5 µs in cellular practice [87] (Spec), or some tens of microseconds for longer slots with a small guard (Derived). A position report stamped with a time is wrong by the speed times the clock error, which at the speeds of these vehicles allows milliseconds to tens of milliseconds for a decimetre (Derived). A road reservation needs the clock error to stay below its guard of 0.1 s. The reservation's real margin is set by position, not by the clock: a vehicle whose position is off by the separation budget at walking pace is seconds early or late, many times the guard (Derived, [68]). Section 5.9 reports how long each oscillator holds each budget and what the reservations do.

3.7 The shared map and its frame

The frame. Two lunar reference systems are in use, and they differ. Every terrain and image product of the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter is in the Mean Earth / Polar Axis system (MOON_ME in the SPICE toolkit), and NASA's 2024 architecture review endorsed it “as the standard for initial surface operations, including planning and user location data exchange” [89]. Orbits, gravity and the new International Lunar Reference Frame use the Principal Axis system (MOON_PA), and the LunaNet working group has said that its reference-system standard will point to that frame and broadcast the transformation; the standard was announced for November 2026 and we could not find it published on 11 November 2026 [91]. The two systems share an origin and differ by a constant rotation of 0.02886° [88]. Lunar ATC stores every coordinate in MOON_ME on the 1,737.4 km sphere, in the south polar stereographic grid of the LOLA products. Every sender declares the frame and the datum of the positions it sends, a receiver refuses a position from a sender that has not, and a position from a navigation service in the other frame is converted once, in the receiver (Design, [68]). The identifiers are not repeated in every report: they are in the sender's heartbeat and in its signed manifest, bound to its key (Section 3.8). In the first version of the protocol the positions were metres in “the site grid” and above “the site datum” by convention only. Proposition 5 gives the size of the mistake the identifier prevents: the same numbers read in the other frame move a point by 662.9 m at the south pole, 659.2 m at the centre of our terrain tile and 875.2 m at most, computed from the three angles of the NAIF frame kernel [88] and equal to a direct evaluation with the SPICE toolkit to within 0.05 m [68]. A second trap is silent: a SPICE program that loads no lunar frame kernel gets the default body frame IAU_MOON, which is off the Mean Earth frame by up to about 155 m [88]. A whole fleet that used the other frame consistently would have no internal error; the error appears where a position in one frame meets a map in the other.

The maps that exist. The reference terrain is from the LOLA laser altimeter: 5 m grids of 27 named sites and of everything south of 87° S, with a height error of 0.3 to 0.5 m RMS per cell [76, 90] (Measured). Two figures for horizontal position are published, for two products, and they must not be mixed: the laser tracks behind the 5 m site models are placed to 0.1 to 0.2 m after adjustment [76], and the wider polar product of 2023 states 1 m as a conservative figure for its tracks [90]. E50 takes its beacons as surveyed on the site model (Assumed, Table 4); the larger figure was not run. But about 90 % of the 5 m cells are interpolated between laser tracks, and the effective resolution is about 20 m poleward of 84° S [90]: a 5 m grid drawn from 20 m sampling shows smooth ground where there may be a 3 m crater. Cameras add 0.5 m images, 1.7 m images inside permanent shadow and 5 m shape-from-shading models where the Sun reaches [92]; images are pictures, not heights. No public product resolves the 0.3 to 1 m rocks and craters that stop a rover. That layer has to be made by the rovers, and it is why the map is shared. It also qualifies this paper's own flag: the 5 m terrain of Section 3.1 is Measured for height and interpolated for detail.

One map, held by the base. The base holds one authoritative map of versioned tiles, with a standby copy, and every rover carries a full copy that it can drive on alone (Design, [68]; the one flight design for several rovers merges its maps on an elected leader and has no rule for two owners [28]). The layers are the orbital elevation at 5 m; rover-made elevation, hazards and traversability cost at 1 m in tiles of 256 m; the road network; keep-out zones with an end time; and radio coverage. Each tile has a version and a hash, and the tiles of a layer form a tree in which each node stores the hash of its four children. An owner contributes signed derived cells only, a hazard, a free cell or a height with its error, never raw images. The base checks the frame and datum identifiers, which a map contribution carries by design, refuses a contribution whose stated position error is above 2 m or whose heights differ from its own by a median of more than 1 m (Assumed limits), accepts a hazard at once as provisional and grows it by three times the reported position error, confirms it when a second owner or a second pass reports it, and logs every decision with the owner and the reason. After time out of contact a rover and the base compare tree hashes, not maps, and then send the changed tiles in a fixed order: keep-out zones and the hazards on the rover's route first, height refinements last [93]. On the wire a 256 m tile of 1 m cells weighs 2,048 B as a hazard layer, at 0.25 bit a cell (Assumed; a slope mask of our own terrain compressed to a median of 0.12 bit a cell), 11,796 B as a traversability layer, at the 1.44 bits a cell that five slope classes of our terrain compressed to, and 32,768 B as rover-made elevation, at 4 bits a cell (Assumed). These are computed from tile sizes, not from a flown map [68]. The 16 km site has 3,969 such tiles a layer. With 42 of them changed in a day (Assumed: half the tiles the haul routes cross), the hash-tree walk costs at most 8,072 B a layer by Proposition 6, against 39,690 B for a flat list of every tile's version and hash; the tree is the smaller for up to 206 changed tiles (the bound is n below 206.7). Both counts are hash bytes in one direction, without the replies that say which children differ and without message headers. The tiles themselves then come to 86.0 kB of hazards, which go first, and 1.96 MB for the three layers together.

3.8 The communication stack and its security

The stack. Below the application every layer of the stack is an existing standard, and Lunar ATC reuses each unchanged (Table 5). What no space standard defines is what two rovers of different owners say to each other. Two terrestrial standards for robots of mixed vendors do define such messages, and the message set here follows them topic for topic: VDA 5050 version 3.0.0 for factory robots [117], whose visualisation, state, order, factsheet and connection topics are our position, status, task, capability and heartbeat messages, and the MassRobotics interoperability standard [118], whose identity and status reports are the smallest set any vendor has agreed to publish. What the lunar case adds to them is what a factory does not need: a link that is narrow, lossy and often absent, so a bit-packed encoding, authentication per message, lifetimes and a lost-link ladder; several owners with no common fleet manager, so signed awards and grants; and positions and times that have to name their frame and scale. The LunaNet specification carries messages from a provider to a user only, says that “the specific standards for messaging are still being determined”, and names a security specification that is not yet written [15]. The surface-wireless standard of the space agencies, CCSDS 883.0-B-2 of May 2024, fixes Wi-Fi 6 and LTE Release 12 for the surface radio [97], so the statement of Section 2.2 that the surface link is left to bilateral agreement is true of LunaNet 1.0 and no longer of CCSDS; the gap that remains is the application layer. The Lunar Rover Interaction Protocol (LRIP, versions 0.1 and 0.2; Design, [68]) fills it with position, status and intent reports, alerts, the task messages of the market (announce, bid, award, state), the reservation messages of the roads (request, grant, release), a capability query and manifest, a map notice and a heartbeat. The messages are implemented and serialised, unlike the assumed layouts of Section 3.4: 15 message types in one schema with two encodings, bit-packed for the narrow channel and CBOR for the broadband one, with real AES-CMAC and Ed25519 and 45 conformance vectors per version in the repository; the vectors test the codec, not a radio. There are two versions, and what each puts on the wire has to be said exactly. In both, a position report holds east and north in the site grid (24 bits at 0.01 m each), the height above the site datum (16 bits at 0.1 m), heading (12 bits), speed (10 bits), one scalar one-sigma horizontal uncertainty (8 bits at 0.05 m), a 3-bit code for the navigation source and a moving flag, and the 8-byte protocol header adds the version, the message type, the sender and one time stamp in tenths of a second. Version 0.1, with which the experiments of this paper were first run, stops there: 6 bits of the report are spare, and nothing in any message names the frame, the datum or the time scale. Version 0.2 closes that gap in two ways. The uncertainty travels in the report itself: the 6 bits that were spare carry a 95 % horizontal bound on a logarithmic scale, 0.05 m times 2 to the power of a quarter of the code, rounded upward so that the decoded bound never understates, with one code for a bound above 1.95 km and one for unknown. The frame, the datum and the time scale do not travel in every report: each sender declares them, in 6, 6 and 4 bits, in a heartbeat sent at least every 10 s and in its signed manifest, and the declaration is bound to the security context: it holds for the key epoch under which it was authenticated, and a receiver refuses a position report whose sender has no declaration under its current key epoch, as it refuses an unauthenticated one. A covariance is not carried in either version: the report has a one-sigma value and, in version 0.2, a 95 % bound, both scalars. The report is 104 bits, 13 B, in both versions: 27 B with its packet and protocol headers, 41 B with the security header and the 8-byte code, and 49 B with the 16-byte code; these are packet sizes before link framing and coding, and a Proximity-1 frame adds 12 B [94]. A status report is 43 B, a bid 37 B, a reservation request 35 B, and the signed award and grant 92 and 94 B. Two sizes differ between the versions: the heartbeat, 30 B in version 0.1 and 32 B with the declaration, and the capability manifest of the small rover, 283 and 292 B in compact binary (1,200 and 1,235 B as JSON). The identifiers are the site's own registry (Assumed; not registered with any agency). The traffic model of Section 4.2 sends 11 of the 15 types: the three reports, the alert, the bid, the award and the task state, the three reservation messages and the heartbeat; the task announcement, the capability query and manifest and the map notice are not in it. A vehicle's manifest states its size and limits, its radios, its navigation accuracy and the message versions it speaks; a receiver skips fields it does not know and refuses another major version.

What has flown. Less has flown than the list of standards suggests. No rate or range of a cellular link has been measured on the Moon. Nokia's network on the IM-2 lander, on 6 March 2025, ran through its 25-minute power window with base station, radio and core on the air and never connected a device, because the device module on the hopper had cooled below its operating range (the company's release of 10 March 2025 [101]). Bundle Protocol version 7 has flown in lunar orbit: CAPSTONE, whose extended mission ended in June 2026, was “the first to fly the latest DTN protocols beyond Earth orbit” and ran them in NASA's core Flight System (NASA, 6 July 2026 [102]); that these were version 7 with custody transfer is from the abstract of a conference paper that we did not read [68]. NASA has run delay-tolerant networking as an operational service on its near-space and deep-space networks since January 2026 [22]. We found no demonstration of it on the lunar surface. No path loss between antennas at rover height has been measured on the Moon; the propagation law of Section 4.2 rests on one NASA model [21] and the one coverage atlas of the south pole is a preprint [103].

Security. Every message carries a security header of 6 bytes, a 2-byte key index and a 4-byte counter, and a message authentication code, AES-CMAC truncated to 8 bytes on the narrow channel or 16 on the broadband one; 8 bytes is the shortest code the link-security standard allows and the 64-bit floor of the NIST recommendation [96, 99]. A receiver accepts a report only if its code verifies, its counter is above the highest it has accepted from that sender under that key, and its time stamp is within 10 s of the receiver's clock (Assumed window); Proposition 7 gives what that buys. The window and the rising counter apply to the reports, which travel one hop and are never queued. They cannot apply to what is stored and forwarded: a bundle delivered after an hour would fail the window, and a held award that arrives after a newer message from the base would fail the counter, while a custodian cannot restamp an award the base signed. A bundle is protected instead by its own identifier, by its lifetime and by the signature or code of its originator, checked at the destination (Design; this split is not tested here, and Proposition 7(b) covers the reports only). The base, or a site authority on Earth, is the trust anchor (Assumed: LunaNet's own security document does not exist yet [15]). Each owner holds a certificate signed by the anchor and each vehicle a key certified by its owner. A message that moves a right, a task award, a reservation grant, a capability manifest, carries an Ed25519 signature of 64 bytes [100] in place of the code, so that one owner can show another what was granted; the audits of Section 3.9 rest on those signatures. Reports are authenticated with a site key that changes daily (Assumed). A shared key stops outsiders and not a compromised insider: a vehicle that reports a false position with a valid code has to be caught by comparing its reports with ranges to it, and where several vehicles must agree on a value, each drops the highest and the lowest values it hears before averaging, which tolerates one false value only if it hears at least three others (the condition is proved in [104] for the continuous-time form of the rule; the paper on the discrete rule states the same condition and was not opened). Nothing in the protocol can command another owner's vehicle; it can inform, ask and agree. Jamming is not stopped by any of this, only survived, by a second band and by the lost-link rule.

Store and forward, and the lost link. A position report is worthless after a second and is never queued. What must arrive, an award, a grant, a ledger entry, an alert, travels as a bundle that each node keeps until the next hop is reachable, with a lifetime after which it is dropped; a bundle adds 48 bytes, or 108 with an integrity block (Computed in [68] from [98]), which is why the once-a-second report is not one. A vehicle that hears no authenticated message from the base follows a fixed ladder that every vehicle knows: it takes no new reservation, finishes the segment it has reserved, stops at the next safe point and holds, then retraces its path to where the link last worked, then drives to a meeting point in view of the mast, then parks with a beacon (Design, [68], after the lost-link procedures of uncrewed aircraft and the radio-failure rule of instrument flight [105]). For the others the rule is one, and it is the same in the three papers of this series [60, 61]: a silent holder's reservation is not released. After the reservation's timeout plus a grace period the segment is marked unconfirmed; another vehicle may enter it only at caution speed, the speed from which it can stop within its own perception range [61], and reports what it finds. An earlier version of this rule had the base release the reservation at that moment, which is unsafe if the silent vehicle is disabled inside the segment. In the model of E54 a custodian retransmits after 10 s while the link lasts and holds the bundle, in a store of 64 MB, while there is no path; a vehicle unheard for 10 min retraces its path at half speed to the last point where it had a link; a segment whose holder is still unheard 60 s after the reservation's timeout is marked unconfirmed; and the silent vehicle's task returns to the auction when its 1 h lease expires (Assumed values, [68]). E54 was run with the rule as first written, in which the base released the reservation at that moment; the release is withdrawn, as above, and Section 5.12 reports what E54's counts mean under the rule as it now stands.

3.9 The marketplace

Jobs and bids. The base computer posts every waiting item with its mass, origin, destination and urgency. In each auction round every free vehicle computes its bid, the marginal cost in hours of inserting the job into its plan, weighted by the item's urgency [56], adjusted by the learned correction [60], and sends it, relayed over the mesh; the lowest bid wins, ties going to the lower vehicle number. Under the leader regime the base computer or the elected leader collects the bids; under the auction regime the robots settle the winner among themselves by the rebroadcast rule of [42, 43, 44]. A team item is won by a pair that agrees by a two-phase commit over the radio, with a rendezvous timeout of 1 h (Assumed) [60]. All of this is the companion paper's machinery; what this paper adds is the money.

Private costs. Each owner has a private cost of running a vehicle: energy, wear and the opportunity cost of the vehicle's time, drawn per owner from an assumed distribution: the engine's job cost, wear plus operator time, times a multiplier exp(N(0, σ)) with σ = 0.2 (Assumed), one draw per owner per seed, the same in every run of that seed; a range of 0.1–0.3 is declared and not swept in E46, because a sweep needs new physical runs (Section 7). A bid is a vehicle's cost in hours; its owner's money cost is that time at the owner's private rate. An honest owner bids its true marginal cost. Every amount of money in this paper is priced with three parameters of the scenario, none of them swept. The delay of an item is priced at the value of time of its priority, 5,000, 1,000 and 100 USD an hour for priorities 1, 2 and 3. An item that is not delivered by the end of the year is priced as if it were delivered at that moment, so its delay is censored at the horizon. A carrier's true cost is its wear, half the vehicle's unit cost per hour of life used, plus its operator's time at 1,000 USD an hour. All three are Assumed: they are the scenario values of [56], which the experiment's design file flags as the scenario's specification, and none has an outside source.

Four payment rules. E46 runs the pooled market under four rules. A rule prices a run after it has been simulated: the auction allocates the jobs on the asks (honest, or scaled for the one deviating owner), and the rule then decides what is paid for the jobs so allocated, so with honest asks the four rules share one allocation. None: no money changes hands; each owner's vehicles work for everyone and the owner bears its own costs, the cooperative case of [60]. First price: the carrier of each job is paid its own ask, and the shipper of each item pays the posted tariff times the item's tonne-kilometres; it is the rule under which an owner gains by asking above its cost, which H5's first clause tests. Per-item second price: the carrier of each job is paid the larger of its own ask and the lowest ask a competing free vehicle would have made for the job at the dispatch instant (that vehicle's cost times its owner's private multiplier, and times the scaling factor if the vehicle belongs to the deviating owner, so a deviator's own other vehicle competes at its scaled ask). That alternative is reconstructed from the run, not bid, because the auction collects no second bid; a team lift is paid the sum of the two lowest such alternatives, split equally between the two carriers, each floored at its own ask; when fewer competing vehicles than the job needs could have carried the item, the tariff times the item's tonne-kilometres is the reserve; the shipper pays the tariff. It is Vickrey's rule [30] applied item by item, and because the auction is sequential across items and rounds it is not strategyproof across rounds, which the manipulation test measures. The shares of jobs with a competing alternative and priced by the reserve are reported in Section 5.5. Posted price per tonne-kilometre with Shapley sharing (in effect Shapley sharing: the posted price sets only the cash that flows before the transfers, Section 5.7): Earth publishes a tariff per tonne-kilometre before the year; the carrier of a delivered item is paid the tariff times the item's mass and road distance and the item's shipper pays the same, so the operator's budget is balanced by construction; at the end of the year the coalition's value, its true carrier costs plus its cargo's delay costs with the sign reversed, is shared among its members by their Shapley values [37] through transfers, computed exactly from the seven non-empty coalitions' values, which five runs per seed give (the owners-alone run supplies the three singletons, three pair runs the pairs, and the pooled run the grand coalition), the rule that freight alliances most often use [36]. In E46 the posted price is 19,828.85 USD per tonne-kilometre with equal shares and 19,908.58 USD per tonne-kilometre with 50/35/15 shares, set before the main runs as the price at which the carriers' true costs are recovered over ten calibration seeds (101–110) that the main runs do not use; the audit penalty multiple is m = 3 (Assumed; the design file declares 2–5 and E46 runs one value).

Money flow and settlement. No money moves on the Moon. The base computer's ledger records every posting, bid, grant, delivery and payment due, signed by the vehicle that produced the entry (Design; the signing is not simulated). Earth receives the ledger in every window and settles afterwards: each owner's cash, what its vehicles were paid as carriers less the tariffs it paid as a shipper and any fines, is netted across owners, and the operator's budget, its receipts (the tariffs shippers pay plus fines) less its payouts to carriers, is reported per year as a surplus or a deficit and never assumed balanced [29]. Under the posted price the Shapley transfers of the year's end are settled on Earth with the cash. The amounts settled on Earth and left open at the end of the year (pay due on jobs unfinished at the horizon) are outcomes of E46.

Individual rationality. An owner is individually rational in the market if its profit over the year, earnings less its private costs, the tariff it pays for its own cargo and its cargo's delay cost, is at least what it would have made working alone with its own vehicles on its own cargo: the owners-alone regime of [60], decided on the Moon, on the same seeds, so that the comparison carries no Earth-availability effect (change 7 of [59]). The alone profit is dominated by the owner's cargo's delay cost at the value of time stated above, including the censored delay of cargo it never delivers, because an owner alone has few vehicles for its cargo and no one pays it (Section 5.6 gives the shares). E46 reports each owner's mean profit under each rule against its mean alone profit and counts the owners, per cell, whose market profit fell below alone; under the posted price the quantity compared is the owner's Shapley value of the coalition's value, and H5's third clause tests it with a bootstrap interval for every owner in every coalition. As the harder comparison, E46 also reports each owner's profit under each paid rule against its profit in the pooled market with no payments, paired by seed (Section 5.6), which asks whether a payment rule leaves an owner worse off than the cooperative market does.

Manipulation. One owner scales every bid of its vehicles by a factor k in {0.8, 0.9, 1.1, 1.25} while the other two bid honestly (Assumed levels, from the research design); the test is run for each owner in turn. The outcome is the largest gain in that owner's profit over honest bidding across the four factors, the factor that gives it, and the cost to the system: the change in the mean wait of urgent cargo, the primary outcome of [60], on the same seeds.

Audits. Because every job is in the ledger with the vehicle's reported cost and its logged energy and distance, Earth can audit afterwards. The model charges the expected penalty of an audit rather than drawing one. At an audit share q in {0, 5, 20 %}, every job of an owner that asks under first or second price is charged q times m times the absolute difference between its ask and its true cost, so asks above and below cost are fined alike and no random draw enters the paired comparison; the multiple m is stated with the tariff above. The outcome is what the audits recover of the manipulating owner's gain, and whether the gain net of expected penalties is still positive at each q.

4. Experiment design

4.1 Questions and hypotheses

The hypotheses follow the paper's two halves: H5 is the market's, and H10 and those of the second registration are the design's and its layers'. The pre-registration [59] states two hypotheses for this paper. H5 (incentives). First-price bids let at least one owner gain by shading; per-item second price reduces the largest gain; posted price with Shapley sharing leaves no owner worse off than alone in any tested coalition; the operator's budget is reported, not assumed balanced. H10 (sizing, exploratory). The safety check, the bidding and the radio traffic of Lunar ATC fit a flight-class computer, reported as measured CPU time, memory and bytes per robot-hour scaled to a reference radiation-tolerant processor with a stated factor, and a 16 kbit/s-class link for 20 robots. H5 was written before any run as part of the original plan and was moved to this paper on 27 October 2026, when the three papers were regrouped (change 1 of [59]). H10 was added on the same date, before any run of either experiment. Two things must be said about H5 as registered. First, the reduced design of 27 October had no first-price rule, which would have left H5's first two clauses untestable; a first-price cell was restored on 29 October (change 7 of [59]), before any E46 row, so H5 is tested as registered. Second, H5 carried no numeric decision rule. The rules, registered as change 7 of [59] before the first E46 row, are: clause 1 is supported if, under first price, the largest gain in an owner's annual profit over bidding truthfully across the tested k has a 95 % interval above zero; clause 2 if the largest such gain under per-item second price is smaller than under first price with the interval of the paired difference excluding zero; clause 3 if, for every owner in every tested coalition, the 95 % interval of the owner's profit in the coalition less its profit alone does not lie entirely below zero, with the point estimates reported either way; intervals are percentile bootstraps over seeds with 20,000 resamples, and the operator's budget is reported per rule, not tested. H10 is exploratory and has no decision rule; it is reported as the measured values against the stated capacities. Since a correction of 11 November 2026 its link clause is reported per channel case, as the airtime the traffic needs against the 16 kbit/s target (Sections 4.2 and 4.6; change 11 of [59]).

The revision of November 2026 reports five further hypotheses, written on 11 November 2026 before any of their results existed [67]: the registration was committed to the repository at 10:14 that day and the first result at 17:43, seven and a half hours later (commits b1e2266 and 99c2a0d). That second pre-registration numbers its hypotheses H9 to H16, and its H9 and H10 are not those of [59]. In the three papers of this series the H9 and H10 of [67] are therefore written H9′ and H10′; H5 and H10 without a prime are the hypotheses of [59], and H11 to H16 exist only in [67]. Not all five turned out to be tests. An internal referee round found that H12, H13, the ranging half of H11 and the second clause of H14 could not have come out otherwise, and that the second clause of H9′ was decided by a reading chosen after the results; dated entries of [67] record each, and Section 5 reports them as design calculations, as not testable as written, and as dependent on the reading. H9′ (positioning). With dead reckoning alone the 95th percentile of the position error exceeds the separation budget within one eight-hour shift of driving; with ranging it stays inside the budget “within radio range of the base beacons”; with absolute fixes it stays inside the budget away from the base. Each clause is supported if the 95th percentile over seeds is on the predicted side of the budget, which a dated change of [67] fixed at the 5 m that rule R1 of [61] keeps free. H11 (time). A resynchronisation interval exists for the cheaper oscillator at which clock error causes no reservation conflict while it still adds more to ranging than the ranging radio's own noise; supported if the sweep finds one. H12 (map). After 24 h out of contact a hazards-first sync of changed tiles needs under 10 % of the bytes of a full resend and brings the hazard layer current sooner than oldest-first at every link rate. H13 (protocol). At 1 Hz position reports, 20 rovers and authentication on, the busiest link carries no more than 16 kbit/s; at 10 Hz it carries more. H14 (lost link). At a message loss of 0.25 with a 14-day Earth blackout, store-and-forward with the lost-link rule loses fewer jobs than drop-and-retry, with a paired interval that excludes zero, and leaves no reservation stuck past its timeout. The confirmatory family of [67] is its H9, H10, H13, H14 and H15a, of which the first two are written H9′ and H10′ here. Holm's step-down procedure at a level of 0.05 is applied over the three of them that have a sampling test, H10′, H14 and H15a. That reduction is ours, made after the results: the registration says only that the five are confirmatory, with Holm. H9′ compares a value with a threshold and H13 is a calculation, so neither has a p-value. In the family H14 is supported (adjusted p = 0.004), H15a is supported (adjusted p at most 0.003) and H10′ is not supported as a whole (adjusted p = 0.93); H10′ and H15a are the companion paper's [61]. With all five counted and the two without a test taken last, the adjusted values would be at most 0.005 for H15a and 0.008 for H14, and no verdict changes. H11 and H12 are exploratory and outside the family.

4.2 Experiments

The experiments are listed in the order they were run: E46 belongs to the market, and E48 and E50 to E55 to the design and its layers.

E46, the market. Three owners, with equal shares of the cargo and with 50/35/15 shares (the split of [39]), at the middle load, under the four payment rules of Section 3.9; for the two bid rules, first price and second price, the manipulation test with k in {0.8, 0.9, 1.1, 1.25} for each owner in turn, and the audit share q in {0, 5, 20 %}; the owners-alone regime on the same seeds as the individual-rationality baseline, and every subset of owners for the Shapley shares. Seeds 1–30 in every cell, a simulated year each. 1,080 physical runs, 30 seeds by 2 splits by 18 run kinds (the pooled market, the oracle, the owners alone, the three pair coalitions and one owner at a time scaling its asks by each of the 4 factors); payments are computed afterwards from each run's job log, so the four rules and the 3 audit shares share one allocation per seed and cost no further runs, giving 152 cells of 30 seeds. Of the 1,080 simulated years, 780 are runs of the market (the pooled run and the scaling runs) and 300 are the oracle, the owners alone and the pair coalitions. The first-price cell is the one restored by change 7 of [59]. The dispatcher is the pooled market run by the base computer or leader over the mesh of [60]. E46 sets the message loss to 0.00 in every cell, the owners-alone and coalition runs included: a perfect radio by design, so that paired differences show incentives and not message-loss noise, which the companion paper [60] covers. The road layer of [61] is on with its defaults.

E48, sizing. Our implementation is instrumented and run, not changed: processor time per safety_check call and per dispatch call of the simulator, as the median and the 99th percentile over every call; an estimate of the on-robot data from the bytes of its tables at the assumed layouts; and the radio traffic computed from each run's event counts (road traversals, auction rounds, landings) at the assumed byte layouts of Section 3.4, plus a status message at 1 Hz per robot, from runs at one, four and eight times today's fleet and demand, the density ladder of [61]; the ledger's size is the year's jobs times the entry's bytes. The instrumented runs last 365 days at 1 times today's fleet, 90 days at 4 times today's fleet, 60 days at 8 times today's fleet; the ledger's yearly growth at four and eight times is extrapolated from jobs per hour. A later addition, made after the internal referee round, runs one 30-day month of one seed at each fleet size and times one robot's own bid computation in every auction round, its loop over the waiting items, and measures with the interpreter's allocation tracer the peak of what the on-robot parts allocate: the planner's working memory during a bid, one safety check, the reservation entries of the next 24 hours, the month's ledger records and the loaded bid policy. The timings are scaled to a reference radiation-tolerant flight processor by the ratio of its published benchmark to this laptop's, flagged Assumed with a range. The reference flight processor is the Frontgrade Gaisler GR740 (rad-hard quad-core LEON4FT, 250 MHz), at 492.5 CoreMark per core (source: Cobham Gaisler, GR740 benchmark results, ESA DSP Day 2016: 1.97 CoreMark/MHz/core x 250 MHz) with 256 MB of memory (Assumed: SDRAM of a typical GR740 single-board computer); the laptop is an Intel Core i5-10500H (6 cores, 2.5 GHz base, 4.5 GHz turbo) at 27,000 CoreMark per core, itself assumed (Assumed: 4.5 GHz turbo (Intel spec) x 6.0 CoreMark/MHz (typical for this core generation; no published CoreMark for this chip found), range 4-7 CoreMark/MHz); the factor is their ratio, 54.8, with the range 36.5–64.0 from the laptop's CoreMark range, and the CPU share uses the 99th-percentile call time at every call, which overstates the mean load (Assumed). The flight processor's benchmark is quoted as the experiment's design file gives it [121]; our research notes do not record that its source was opened. The scaled values are compared with the design targets of Section 3.4. The traffic is also stated as airtime on one shared channel, because every relayed copy of a message is another transmission in the same band: 20 robots need a channel rate of at least their load at the sources, at the authenticated sizes of Section 3.4, times the mean number of hops a message travels, divided by the access efficiency, 0.80 for a channel the base schedules in time slots with a 20 % guard time and 0.37 (1/e) for slotted random access (Assumed). The hop count is one, two, or the mean number of hops to the base computer, among the robots with a path to it, over the 1,029 decision epochs of a one-month trace of the auction regime with the road layer on (seed 1, 30 % load, message loss 0.10, the month of 2028 with the least direct-to-Earth visibility at the base). The link budget per vehicle class takes the antenna height, frequency and transmit power of Section 3.3 at the mesh's worst hop, under the flat-ground two-ray law with the smooth-Moon horizon check, with free space as an upper bound on the rate. The hop is 5 km long, between two antennas of the class's own height (for the base computer, the mast down to a 1.0 m robot antenna). Its loss follows the flat-ground two-ray law: free-space loss up to the breakpoint 4h1h2/λ and, beyond it, 40 log10 d − 20 log10(h1h2) with the distance d and the antenna heights h1, h2 in metres, never less than free-space loss and independent of frequency (Derived from a direct ray and a ground-reflected ray with a reflection coefficient of −1 at grazing incidence; a NASA Johnson Space Center model of lunar surface propagation shows the same fourth-power law and its independence of frequency at long range [21]). A hop longer than the smooth-Moon horizon of its two antennas, √(2Rh1) + √(2Rh2) [63], carries nothing; for a class in that case the rate is reported at the longest hop the horizon allows. The rate is the Shannon capacity of a 1 MHz channel with 3 dBi antennas, a 5 dB noise figure and a 10 dB margin (Assumed). The rate under free-space loss at the same hop is kept beside it as an upper bound. The budget reports the data rate at that hop, the latency of a three-hop exchange of a 100 B message at the hop latency of Section 3.3 and the rate of the longest hop the horizon allows (one hop for the base computer) plus one scaled safety check, against a decision interval of 1 s (Assumed; a target of this paper's design that no design file holds), and the share of route sample points from which the mast is in reach: in terrain line of sight within the range, with the uplink closing 16 kbit/s at the class's power with a 10 dB margin under the same law, which is the same share as line of sight within the range alone, because every such link closes. Failover sweeps the base computer's mean time between failures. The failover sweep runs the base computer at an MTBF of 500 h, 2,000 h, 8,000 h, a repair time of 24 h and a re-election delay of 0.5 h, on five seeds of the leader regime of [60] at the middle load, all three values Assumed; the re-election delay is an input, not a measurement. The sweep measures the base computer's downtime share and the share of time without a leader, and checks the assignment table after every rebuild for a job assigned twice. 21 runs were instrumented: six timing runs, seeds 1, 2 at 1, 4, 8 times today's fleet (the auction regime of [60] with the road layer of [61], message loss 0.1), and 15 failover runs, seeds 1–5 at the three MTBF levels (the leader regime of [60], message loss 0.1, the middle load, today's fleet, one year); the dispatch call is timed in every timing run and pooled, and the on-robot data are estimated once, from the runs at today's fleet.

E50, positioning. The run is the auction regime of [60] with the road layer of [61] on, three owners with equal shares, the middle load and a message loss of 0.10, in July 2028. Each seed is first run with perfect position; its recorded trajectories are then replayed through the navigation model of Section 3.5 in steps of 60 s, once per layer set: dead reckoning alone, with ranging, with ranging and terrain fixes, and with the navigation service added, which in 2028 is never available, so that set equals the third and a variant with the service as assumed for 2030 is run beside it. Errors start at zero at the window's start (every vehicle surveyed then, Assumed). Seeds: 30 at today's fleet and 10 at four times the fleet and 5 at eight times the fleet, fewer at the larger fleets because of run time, as the dated changes of [67] record. The results are split by region: a vehicle-step is called near the base when its route sample is in radio line of sight of at least two of the four beacons, and away from it otherwise. That rule was chosen with the experiment; it is not in the registration, whose words are “within radio range of the base beacons”, and it covers 23.1 % of the driving steps (Section 5.8 reports the clause under other readings; a dated entry of [67] records the choice). The replay is a covariance analysis with a matched filter: the errors are drawn from the same linear-Gaussian model that the filter assumes, with the bias of a leg as a filter state, no outlier, no range bias from a blocked path, no wrong fix accepted as good, and the beacons' survey errors treated as white. The error it reports is therefore the best any estimator could do if the model were the world. The outcomes are, per layer set, the median and the 95th percentile of the horizontal position error over the vehicle-steps in which a vehicle is driving, the share of those steps in which the vehicle's own 95 % bound exceeds the budget, and the hours a month the fleet stands still for terrain fixes; each is a mean over seeds with a bootstrap interval. What the error costs the rules of the road, a wider reservation margin and its delay, is the subject of the companion paper [61].

E51, time. E51 replays the month of E50 at today's fleet, the perfect-position run of each of 30 seeds, and gives every vehicle a clock of one class. Resynchronisation is attempted every 10 s, 1 min, 10 min, 1 h, 6 h or 1 day, or never, and succeeds only when the vehicle has a radio path to the base mast over the mesh; 6.8 % of the attempts find none. The measures are the error a clock adds to one-way ranging against a beacon on the base's clock (the speed of light times the clock error), to single-sided two-way ranging between two vehicles with a reply delay of 1 ms, and to a time-stamped position at 1 m/s; the largest clock error; and the reservation conflicts caused by clock error alone. Such a conflict is a pair of conflicting single-lane reservations, of 303 such pairs in the 30 seeds, whose two clock errors close the nominal gap between them by more than twice the guard; the guard is 0.1 s, with a row for no guard. This is a check on the logged reservations, not a new run of the engine, and it is made at today's fleet only: the fleets of four and eight times, where reservations are more frequent, were not checked. Four things were added after the first results and are labelled post hoc (Section 4.6): guards of 0.01 and 1 s; every vehicle on Earth seconds; a transfer that also steers frequency, with a daily thermal cycle of 4 × 10−7 on the crystal and 3 × 10−10 on the atomic clock (Assumed period of 24 h); and a mixed fleet in which half the vehicles count Earth seconds, with and without the conversion of Proposition 3.

E52, the shared map. E52 keeps the base's map and one rover's copy of it for the 16 km site: 3,969 tiles of 256 m in each of the hazard, traversability and rover-made elevation layers and a radio-coverage layer in 1,280 m tiles of 2,000 B, at the wire sizes of Section 3.7. The rover loops the haul routes at 1 m/s; the routes cross 60 tiles. While it is out of contact, 42 route tiles a day get a new version in each of the three layers, and new hazards appear on the routes at 2 a day, keep-out zones at 0.3 a day and road-edge changes at 5 a day; 50 % of the hazards are invisible to the rover's own sensors (all Assumed). After 1 h, 6 h, 1, 3, 7 or 14 days the rover regains a link of 10, 16, 100 or 1,000 kbit/s with a packet loss of 0.2 and synchronises, either hazards first (keep-out zones, hazard tiles on its route, other hazards, roads, traversability, radio coverage, elevation) or oldest change first, over 30 seeds. The outcomes are the bytes sent against a full resend of every raster layer (185.5 MB), the time until the hazard layer is current, and the hazards the rover drives into because its copy is stale, during the outage and after contact. E52 is a model of the map's bookkeeping on the real routes; it does not run inside the logistics engine, and its hazards are drawn, not sensed.

E53, the protocol's traffic. E53 computes the traffic of the protocol from the serialised sizes of version 0.1. Each rover sends a position report at 0.1, 1 or 10 Hz whether it moves or not (the protocol's own default is 1 Hz moving and 0.1 Hz parked), status and intent reports at 0.1 Hz, and the market and reservation events at the rates E48 counted at today's fleet. Reports travel one hop; events are relayed to the base. For 5, 10, 20, 40 and 80 rovers, 30 draws place the rovers on the terrain mesh, every one with a 2.5 m antenna although half of today's fleet carries 1.0 m, in two geometries: at positions taken from the recorded trajectories of E50's first seed with every vehicle-step equally likely, which is the registered geometry of H13, and uniformly over all route samples (added after the first results, Section 4.6). In that trace 97.4 % of the vehicle-steps are parked (347,669 against 9,451 driving), so the registered geometry is a fleet standing at its sites. The outcome is the load on the busiest link, the largest sum of what any one node hears, at the 95th percentile over the draws, with authentication on and off, against the 16 kbit/s target of Section 3.4 as load and as airtime at the access efficiencies of E48.

E54, store-and-forward and the lost link. E54 is a simulation at the level of messages over the recorded month of E50, not a new run of the logistics engine: inside the engine a lost message is retried and the regimes know at once that it failed, so a lost job cannot occur there. The contact plan is the terrain mesh along the recorded trajectories, per 60 s step. The traffic is the month's real events: a task award from the base to each job's vehicle, which lives for the 1 h lease, and whose expiry is counted as a lost job (hypothetically: in the recorded run every job was done); a reservation grant, which lives 60 s; a task-state report at the job's end; a daily ledger bundle of 10 kB to Earth with a lifetime of 14 days; and a status report every 10 s, from which the age of the base's information is taken. Earth is blacked out from day 8 to day 22 on top of the month's own direct-to-Earth gaps. Three protocols carry the traffic: drop-and-retry end to end, a try every 180 s and at most five tries, so that it gives up after 12 min on an award that lives an hour (the retry budget is our choice; the registration does not fix it, and it was not swept); store-and-forward hop by hop with custody; and store-and-forward with the lost-link ladder of Section 3.8. The per-hop loss is 0.10, 0.25 or 0.40, on 30 seeds with 199 jobs and 88 single-lane reservations a seed. Two things are approximations: the return of a silent vehicle is applied to the contact plan and is not driven in the engine, and a returning vehicle is given a direct link to the base, which is optimistic when its last link went through another vehicle. E54 also counts the reservations whose holder has no path to the base 60 s after the reservation's timeout: the base cannot confirm that the segment is clear, although in the replay every vehicle left on schedule. Under the rule as it now stands these are the segments marked unconfirmed. The run itself used the rule as first written, which released them and so recorded none by construction; that zero is not a result. The registration also lists the wait of urgent cargo among E54's outcomes; a replay of messages does not move cargo, so that outcome is not reported. The ledger is simulated separately over 61 days, so that every bundle created in the month resolves, with lifetimes of 14 days, 30 days, and the longest Earth outage plus one day.

E55, authentication. E55 has three parts, and this paper uses the first: forged messages are offered to a receiver that verifies the real AES-CMAC truncated to 8 and to 16 bits, so that acceptances can be counted against Proposition 7, and recorded messages are replayed after 0 to 60 s among 20 senders at 1 Hz and 10 receivers at message losses of 0.10 and 0.25. Its second part, the detection of wrong position reports, and its third, the trimmed-mean rule with faulty rovers (H16 of [67]), are the companion paper's [61]; one count from the third is quoted in Section 5.11.

Checks on real data (task B-97). Two checks use real data, and each is named for what it is. The first takes six raw frames of the optical-navigation camera of the Artemis II flyby, taken between 2 April 2026 and 10 April 2026 [115], fits a circle to the bright limb of the disc in each (a threshold, a robust circle fit, a least-squares refinement), and turns the disc's radius and centre into a range and a direction with the focal length of the mission's instrument kernel, 7,312 pixels; the truth is the mission's own trajectory and attitude kernels [116]. Nothing is calibrated, and the body in view is taken from the geometry, not from the archive label. This is limb navigation of a crewed spacecraft in cislunar space, 79,000 to 186,000 km from the body in view; it is not surface navigation and not rover data. It tests a camera model, the frames and the kernels of the kind that position-from-image code would rest on; no result of this paper uses such code, because the terrain fix of E50 is a Gaussian draw. The second check compares the Earth-link geometry code of [58], which the Earth windows of this paper come from, with the SPICE toolkit's own routine for azimuth and elevation, for the spacecraft seen from the base at 212 hourly epochs of its trajectory, and measures how far the base moves when the lunar frame kernel is left out (Section 3.7). The spacecraft is below the base's horizon at every one of those epochs (elevation −9.26° to −0.04°), so the check exercises the transformation to azimuth and elevation and not the terrain mask that makes the Earth windows. A third check of that task, a detection method on telemetry of two NASA spacecraft, belongs to the companion paper [61].

4.3 Seeds and common random numbers

Every cell of E46 uses seeds 1–30, so a seed's demand, landings and faults (and message losses, of which E46 has none) are identical across payment rules, share splits, scaling factors and audit shares, and the difference between two cells on one seed is the effect of the rule alone; the owners-alone and coalition runs of a seed share its demand too. Pairs are formed on the seed. E48 instruments existing runs and adds no randomness of its own; where it times a fresh run it uses the seeds of the run it reproduces. No run is excluded after the fact; a run that crashes is fixed and repeated with the same seed, and the crash is reported in Section 5.14. The experiments of the revision share one recorded month: E51 and E54 replay the perfect-position runs of E50's seeds at today's fleet, so a seed is the same month in all three; E50b runs the same seeds again with the fix stops in the engine; E52 draws its own seeds, and E53 draws rover positions from the trace of E50's first seed.

4.4 Outcomes

The outcomes of E46 are, per owner and year: profit under each rule; the difference between the owner's Shapley value in each coalition and its alone profit on the same seed (individual rationality); the operator's budget surplus or deficit; the money settled on Earth and left open at the year's end; the efficiency loss against the oracle of [60] in system cost, true carrier cost plus delay cost; and, for the manipulation cells, the largest gain from scaling, its factor, its cost to the system in cargo delay hours and in system cost, and what each audit share recovers of it. The outcomes of E48 are the timings, counts and estimates of Section 4.2, the scaled values, the link-budget quantities per class (the rate at the worst hop or at the longest hop the horizon allows, the latency of a decision, the share of route sample points with the mast in reach), the channel rate the traffic of 20 robots needs per channel case, the base computer's downtime share and the share of time without a leader per MTBF level at the assumed re-election delay, and the count of jobs found assigned twice after a rebuild of the assignment table, which must be zero.

4.5 Statistics

Differences between cells are paired means over seeds with a percentile bootstrap 95 % confidence interval of 20,000 resamples [62]. H5's clauses use the one-sided rules of Section 4.1. H5 is outside the confirmatory family of [59], which is its H1, H2, H3a, H4 and H6, so no correction for multiplicity is applied to it; its six verdicts, three clauses in two splits, are reported uncorrected and should be read with that in mind. The first clause takes the largest of twelve cell means, three owners by four factors, and then bootstraps that cell: the interval of a selected maximum is biased upward, which here can only strengthen the verdict that the clause is not supported. Sizing values are reported as medians and 99th percentiles over calls with their counts, scaled values as a point estimate with the range the scaling factor's range implies, and the H10 comparison as the ratio of each scaled value to its capacity. All intervals are descriptive of the simulated population of seeds, not of a lunar fleet. The analyses are descriptive (cell means), inferential (paired bootstrap intervals), causal by design (same seeds, one factor changed) and, for the link budget, mechanistic. The experiments of the revision report means over seeds with the same percentile bootstrap intervals; H14's registered test is a paired difference on seeds, and the p-value used in the family is from a one-sided exact sign test; E55's counts of accepted forgeries are compared with the count Proposition 7 predicts and its Poisson spread; and the only p-values are those of the confirmatory family's Holm step (Section 4.1).

4.6 Choices made after looking at results

Two choices were made before either experiment ran and are recorded here. First, the market experiment was reduced on 27 October 2026, when the three papers were regrouped: the McAfee trade-reduction rule, charger hogging, false availability and cherry-picking of easy items were dropped, and the first-price rule with them; the first-price rule was restored on 29 October (change 7 of [59]) because H5 needs it, leaving four rules, one manipulation and the audits; the reduction was made for the time the runs would take, before any E46 row existed, and its consequence for H5 is stated in Section 4.1. Second, the dispatch fixed-point fix of the companion papers (change 5 of [59]) was adopted before any run of E46 or E48, so neither experiment has a before-and-after. Two choices were made after the runs. First, the decomposition of the largest first-price gain into the part the scaled asks pay with the allocation held fixed and the change in the deviator's own cargo delay once its asks move jobs was made after E46 had run, at the lead's request, when the registered interval was seen to cover zero; it is reported in Sections 5.7 and 6.1, in the abstract and in the conclusion, labelled post hoc each time, and no registered verdict depends on it. Second, E48's analysis was corrected. The first version of this paper took free-space loss at the worst hop of the link budget and compared the 16 kbit/s target with the traffic at the sources. 5.2 Messages and the ledger

With the assumed byte layouts of Section 3.4 the message types weigh: status 48 B; reservation_request 32 B; grant 32 B; release 16 B; bid 40 B; award 32 B; claim 32 B; landing_announcement 64 B; ledger_entry 128 B. Computed from each run's event counts and the status rate, the fleet generated 3,600.3 messages and 172,812 B per robot-hour at today's fleet, 3,604.2 and 172,945 B at four times, and 3,613.9 and 173,280 B at eight times; at today's fleet that is 48.0 B/s per robot against the 100 B/s target and 7.68 kbit/s for 20 robots at the sources, before authentication and before any relaying (the next paragraph adds both). The ledger grows by 0.3 MB a year at today's fleet, 1.4 MB at four times and 2.7 MB at eight, extrapolated from each run's jobs per hour. The market's messages come to 0.005 MB a day at today's fleet. The status message at the assumed 1 Hz is 99.99 % of the messages and 99.99 % of the bytes at 1 times, 99.88 % of the messages and 99.92 % of the bytes at 4 times, 99.62 % of the messages and 99.72 % of the bytes at 8 times; the traffic counts reservation requests, grants and releases from road traversals, bids, awards and claims from auction rounds, and landing announcements, and omits rebroadcasts, team-lift proposals, answers and commits, and ledger entries.

With the authenticated layout of Section 3.4 the types weigh: position 41 B; status 43 B; intent 38 B; reservation request 35 B; grant 94 B; release 31 B; bid 37 B; award 92 B; claim 33 B; landing announcement 42 B (with the 16 B code, in the same order: 49, 51, 46, 43, 94, 39, 45, 92, 41, 50 B). The same event counts then give 4,320.4 messages and 176,779 B per robot-hour at today's fleet (211,341 B with the wider code): 49.1 B/s per robot against the 100 B/s target, and 7.86 kbit/s for 20 robots at the sources (9.39 kbit/s with the wider code). On one shared channel, at the mean of 1.024 hops to the base computer in the month's trace of Section 4.2 (robot-epochs with a path to it: 5,686 at one hop, 140 at two hops; 5.6 % of the robot-epochs with the base computer up had no path and are not in the mean, and over all route points the median is two hops, Section 3.3), 20 robots need 10.06 kbit/s of a channel the base schedules, within the 16 kbit/s target, and 21.87 kbit/s on slotted random access, above it. At exactly one hop the scheduled channel needs 9.82 kbit/s, within the target; if every report is relayed two hops it needs 19.64 kbit/s, above it. Every one of these figures is conditional. A base cannot give a time slot to, or hear a one-hop report from, a vehicle that has no path to it, and the hop count above is a mean over the robots' positions at the decision epochs of one seed's month. In E50's trace of the same seed those positions are parked in 97.4 % of vehicle-steps (347,669 parked against 9,451 driving), while the bytes are reports sent once a second by every vehicle. While driving, a vehicle had a radio path to the base in 56.6 % of its steps (47.7 % to 62.9 % over the seeds), against 93.2 % over all time (E54's contact plan of the month). The claim is therefore this: the authenticated traffic of 20 robots, 7.86 kbit/s at the sources, would need 10.06 kbit/s of a base-scheduled channel if every robot were within one hop of the base; in the recorded month that holds for robots standing at sites, and a driving robot has a path to the base 57 % of the time. The report rates matter as much as the hops. The traffic counted has position at 1 Hz and status and intent at 0.1 Hz; if all three reports went at 1 Hz a robot would send 41 + 43 + 38 = 122 B/s and 20 robots 19.52 kbit/s at the sources, above the target before any airtime factor (a calculation from the serialised sizes, not a run). A further calculation splits these figures by driving and parked positions. Of the 6,174 robot-epochs behind the mean hop count, 54 are of a driving robot (0.9 %), too few for a driving hop count: the 1.024 hops are a figure for parked robots. Drawing 20 rovers from the driving steps of the recorded month only (2.6 % of its vehicle-steps), with the traffic model's 2.5 m antennas, the rovers that reach the base are 1.58 hops from it on average, against 1.01 from parked steps; 93 % have a path, against 99 %; and the busiest link carries 5.89 kbit/s at the 95th percentile, against 7.86, because driving rovers are spread along the routes and share fewer neighbourhoods. As airtime that is 7.37 kbit/s on a scheduled channel, within the target, and 16.02 kbit/s on slotted random access, above it. The 93 % is not the 56.6 % above: here 20 rovers with 2.5 m antennas relay for one another, there the real fleet of eight vehicles, half of them with 1.0 m antennas, drives its recorded routes over 30 seeds. With status and intent at 1 Hz as well, the same calculation gives 19.52 kbit/s at the sources and a scheduled airtime of 24.4 kbit/s at the fleet's positions and 18.3 at driving positions, both above the target: at those rates the 1 Hz case of the link claim does not hold on any channel. The 1.0 m antennas of the small classes and a report rate tied to motion were not run.

5.11 What authentication costs, and what the channel carries (H13, a design calculation)

At the sizes of version 0.1 and with authentication, a rover that reports its position once a second generates 49.1 B/s, 0.393 kbit/s, against 32.3 B/s without it: the security header and the code add 52 % to the bit rate, because they are 14 B on reports of 10 to 15 B of content. For 20 rovers in the fleet geometry the busiest link carries 7.86 kbit/s at 1 Hz with authentication and 5.17 without, and 66.9 and 44.0 kbit/s at 10 Hz; that link carries the sum over the whole fleet, because the fleet works within one radio neighbourhood of the base. As airtime the 1 Hz traffic of 20 rovers needs 9.82 kbit/s of a scheduled channel, within the target, and 21.36 on slotted random access, above it, the same verdicts as E48's event counts gave in Section 5.2. On a scheduled channel the authenticated 1 Hz traffic fits the target for up to 20 rovers of the sizes run and not for 40; at 10 Hz 5 rovers already load the busiest link with 16.7 kbit/s (Table 14). The figures above are for version 0.1. With version 0.2 the report keeps its size and the only change in the traffic is the heartbeat with its declaration, now sent at least every 10 s: a rover generates 52.3 B/s in place of 49.1, authentication adds 53 % in place of 52 %, and for 20 rovers at 1 Hz the busiest link carries 8.37 kbit/s in place of 7.86 and needs 10.46 kbit/s of a scheduled channel in place of 9.82, within the target; at 10 Hz it carries 67.4 kbit/s. All of these are packet sizes. With every packet in a Proximity-1 frame, 12 B more, the same 20 rovers offer 10.87 kbit/s and need 13.58 kbit/s of a scheduled channel, within the target, and do not fit it on slotted random access; channel coding is not counted. The two comparisons of H13 come out the same under version 0.2. These are calculations on a traffic model; the split by driving positions and the case with status and intent at 1 Hz (Section 5.2) were computed with the sizes of version 0.1 only, which differ from version 0.2 by the heartbeat. H13 of [67] says that with authentication on and 20 rovers the busiest link carries no more than 16 kbit/s at 1 Hz position reports and more at 10 Hz. It is not a test but a design calculation, as a dated entry of [67] records: in the registered geometry every rover is in one radio neighbourhood, so the busiest link carries the fleet's sum in every draw, 20 × 49.1 B/s × 8 = 7.86 kbit/s at 1 Hz and 66.9 kbit/s at 10 Hz, the product of the serialised sizes and the assumed rates that Section 5.2 already gives. It could not have come out otherwise. Its comparison is of load with the target; as airtime, the comparison that change 11 of [59] adopted, the 1 Hz case is above the target on slotted random access (21.36 kbit/s) and within it on a channel the base schedules (9.82 kbit/s). It has no sampling test, and the family's file shows why: the value is on the same side of the threshold in every draw at both rates, because it is the same sum in every draw. With the rovers spread uniformly over the route samples (post hoc) the busiest link at 1 Hz carries 4.54 kbit/s for 20 rovers, 58 % of the fleet geometry's, because each node hears fewer neighbours; the rovers that reach the base need 1.1 to 2.2 hops on average against 1.01 to 1.03; and only 30 %, 43 %, 62 %, 83 % and 93 % of 5, 10, 20, 40 and 80 rovers reach the base at all, against 97 % to 99 % in the fleet geometry.

The acceptance of forged messages was measured with the real AES-CMAC truncated to a short code, because the 8-byte code cannot be tried often enough: 395 of 100,000 forged messages passed a code truncated to 8 bits, where 2−8 predicts 391; 11 of 1,000,000 forged messages passed a code truncated to 16 bits, where 2−16 predicts 15.3. For the 64-bit code of the narrow channel the same law gives 5.4 × 10−20 per attempt and 1.7 × 10−11 for ten attempts a second over a year (formula, not a trial). Of 1,172 recorded messages replayed after 0 to 60 s to receivers that keep the per-sender counter and the 10 s freshness window, 574 at a loss of 0.10 and 598 at a loss of 0.25, none was accepted. The one replay the rule admits is to a receiver that missed the original and gets the copy within the window: it then learns a true but stale state. For agreement by averaging, the trimmed-mean rule of Section 3.8 needs every honest rover to hear at least three others when one value may be false. On the terrain mesh that held in 10 % to 26 % of the draws at fleet positions and in 0 % of the draws spread over the routes, so the rule needs more links, relays or masts, or the base as the averaging node. The pre-registered test of the rule, H16 of [67], is not supported as stated, and the companion paper reports it [61]. With the base mast added as an honest node (a variant run after the first results), and no more faulty rovers than the rule is set for, the graph of the fleet geometry was connected in 58 % to 96 % of the runs, up from 0 % to 10 %, but the honest rovers still reached agreement in only 0 % to 16 %: agreement by averaging needs more than one relay. A well-connected node also spreads a false value: with two faulty rovers against a rule set for one, the share of runs in which the honest rovers stayed inside the honest range fell from 48 % to 66 % without the mast to 2 % to 24 % with it. In this design a shared estimate is therefore computed at the base, which is then the node that must not be wrong, and the companion paper gives the details [61].

5.13 Checks on real data

Artemis II optical navigation: of the six frames, one is saturated and five usable; four of those have attitude data and one gives a range only. The range from the fitted disc was 0.25 % to 1.29 % above the kernels' range, and the direction within 0.08° to 0.16°. The errors are not random: the fitted radius was 0.8 to 1.3 pixels small in every frame, which puts every range high, and the angle off the camera's axis was 0.08° to 0.11° low in every frame, which may be the alignment of the camera frame (the mission's frame kernel carries a warning about those angles). We report the bias as it is and do not fit it away; a correction fitted on other frames is the next check. Two of the five discs are Earth, although every archive label names the Moon. For the two frames of the Moon at about 79,000 km the error is 408 to 661 km in range and, at the direction errors above, about 110 to 230 km across the line of sight. That says the geometry chain recovers a real body's range to about a percent and its direction to a tenth or two of a degree from one uncalibrated frame. It says nothing about the 1.7 m of a rover's terrain fix (Table 4): that matches craters metres away against a map, a different measurement at a different scale. Earth-link geometry: over 212 epochs the code's azimuth and elevation agreed with the routine of the toolkit it is built on to 6 × 10−14°; without the lunar frame kernel the base moved by 77.4 m, the silent error of Section 3.7 measured at our site.

6. Discussion

6.1 What the results say about the question

The discussion follows the two halves: the vehicles and what Lunar ATC needs inside them come first, the market third, and the layers under the market last. The question had three parts: what the vehicles of 2028–2032 can carry, what Lunar ATC needs inside each of them and whether it fits, and under which payment rules a market among owners is fair and hard to game. On the first part the answer needed no experiment and is the least comfortable: the fleet's capacity is a plateau at 2,800 kg per lift, set by two cargo rovers nobody has bought, and the crew rovers NASA has bought carry 80 kg and drive 10 km on a charge. Whatever a dispatcher does, elements heavier than the plateau wait for a vehicle that does not exist, and the base's stated need runs to 15,000 kg [5, 6]. The sizing of this paper is for the fleet that will exist, which is small; the network it needs is correspondingly small.

On the second part, the safety check fits one core of the reference processor with the stated factor, and the on-robot data are small, by an estimate and by a laptop measurement; one robot's bid computation in an auction round, timed on a laptop in one month of one seed and scaled by the same factor, takes 0.29 s at the 99th percentile at today's fleet, within the one-second decision, and 1.09 s and 1.19 s at four and eight times the fleet, above it; and the authenticated radio traffic of 20 robots would need 10.06 kbit/s of a channel that the base schedules if every robot were within one hop of the base, within the 16 kbit/s target, and 21.87 kbit/s on slotted random access and 19.64 kbit/s if every report were relayed two hops, both above it. The one-hop condition is taken from positions that are parked in 97 % of vehicle-steps; a driving vehicle has a radio path to the base 56.6 % of the time, so the condition holds for robots standing at sites and not for a fleet on the move. The safety program is explicit switching by design, the form that costs least in computation in the measured comparison we found [45], and the planner's learned part is 13 weights; the sizing question was always the bookkeeping of the market and the reservation table rather than any arithmetic. E48 answers it on a laptop, scaled: the safety check fits, the tables are small by an estimate and by a measurement, and one robot's bid computation fits the one-second decision at today's fleet and not at four and eight times it at the 99th percentile, because a robot tries every waiting item in a round. The bookkeeping that grows with the fleet is the bid, not the safety check.

On the third part, every rule left every owner's mean profit above its alone profit; the operator's budget under first price, −2.50 M USD a year with equal shares, is the gap between the calibration seeds and the test seeds and not a property of the rule (Section 7); under the second price the operator ran a deficit of 12.71 M USD, and under the posted price a balanced budget; the largest gain from scaling bids was +7.73 M USD a year, and audits at 20 % did not remove it under either bid rule. A decomposition made after the runs, not pre-registered, separates the two parts of that gain: with the allocation held fixed, the scaled asks alone paid the deviator +2.57 M USD a year [2.43, 2.73] under first price with equal shares and +5.40 M USD [5.01, 5.80] with 50/35/15 shares (+1.75 M USD and +3.05 M USD under the second price); the rest of the paired gain is the change in the deviator's own cargo delay once its asks move jobs, a term with a mean of +5.16 M USD and −0.17 M USD and a standard deviation over seeds of 29 M USD and 41 M USD, at least 7.6 times the cash, which is why the registered interval covers zero although shading pays in cash. The registered test of the first clause was therefore underpowered: the measure it registered, the deviator's whole profit, carries the deviator's own delay term, whose spread over seeds is many times the cash, and the cash part on its own is positive. That is a statement about the test's power, made after the fact, not a change of the verdict, which stands as registered. The Shapley sharing of the posted price moves money from the owner of the small vehicles to the two cargo-rover owners: the Shapley transfers move 200 M USD a year from owner C with equal shares and 78 M USD a year from owner C with 50/35/15 shares to the other two owners. With equal shares the pooling gain over alone is 804 M USD a year; under no payments owner C keeps 85 % of it (A 7 %, B 8 %, C 85 %), under the Shapley sharing owner C receives the largest share, 59 % (A 21 %, B 20 %, C 59 %), and the transfer of each owner is the difference between the two (A +112 M USD, B +88 M USD, C −200 M USD); with 50/35/15 shares the pooling gain over alone is 438 M USD a year; under no payments owner C keeps 68 % of it (A 18 %, B 13 %, C 68 %), under the Shapley sharing owner C receives the largest share, 49 % (A 26 %, B 25 %, C 49 %), and the transfer of each owner is the difference between the two (A +34 M USD, B +44 M USD, C −78 M USD). Owner C's loss against no payments (Section 5.6) is the mirror image of A and B being paid for carrying C's cargo, and it follows from the fleet split, not from the payment rule: owner C holds the two small rovers and the two hoppers (Section 3.2), which cannot carry most of its own cargo, so under no payments the cargo rovers of A and B carry that cargo for nothing, under the posted price at the cost-recovery tariff, and the Shapley sharing charges C for the value those vehicles add. That is what the Shapley rule's definition of fairness means, each owner's marginal contribution averaged over the orders in which owners could join, and it is a definition, not a measurement: it gives owner C the largest share of the pooling gain over alone, and C's transfer is the difference between that share and the larger one C keeps when nobody is paid. The alone baseline is weak, because an owner alone is dominated by its cargo's delay cost, so every pooled rule clears it by a wide margin. That cost is delay priced at an assumed hourly rate, and for owner C it is almost wholly cargo that is never delivered alone and is censored at the year's end (Section 5.6): the Shapley values, the pooling gain and the transfers above all carry that assumption, and none of them was computed at another value of time. The harder comparison is against the pooled market with no payments, and there against no payments owner C is worse off under every paid rule and both splits, with the interval below zero; no other owner's interval lies below zero (A's under first price with 50/35/15 shares includes it). The market's purpose in this design is not to extract the last hour of efficiency but to let three companies share eight vehicles without a treaty; the number that matters for that is whether any owner, in particular the smallest, loses by joining, and E46 measures it directly against both baselines.

On the fourth part, the layers under the market, the results draw one line, between the base and everywhere else. Where two or more of the four beacons are in line of sight, ranging holds the 95th percentile of the position error at 4.22 m, inside the 5 m budget; that region is 23.1 % of the driving steps, and under wider readings of “near the base” the budget is not held (Section 5.8). In the recorded months a crystal resynchronised at every contact caused no reservation conflict with a guard of 0.1 s; a day of map changes is 1.97 MB at the assumed change rate; and the authenticated reports of 20 rovers would need 9.82 kbit/s of a scheduled channel if every rover were within one hop of the base. Elsewhere, terrain fixes leave the 95th percentile at 15.2 m (7.54 m with a navigation service as assumed for 2030, still outside the budget), a driving vehicle has a radio path to the base in 56.6 % of its steps, and of 20 rovers spread over the routes only 62 % reach the base at all. The line is narrower than the first version of this revision drew it. “Near the base” there meant two or more beacons in line of sight, a region of under a quarter of the driving steps that was chosen after the results; under the registered words, or within a kilometre of the base, ranging does not hold the budget in any seed, so the honest statement is that the position budget is held in sight of at least two beacons and nowhere else in this model. The failure of H9′'s third clause is a statement about the sensors as the model has them: where fewer than two beacons are seen the legs are darker and steeper, where the model allows fewer terrain fixes, lets some of them fail and lets dead reckoning drift faster on a slope (Table 4). E50 does not split the error by kind of leg, so that reading is the model's construction and not a finding. The price of the terrain-fix layer is now in a delay figure, as a lower bound (E50b, Section 5.8). The same line appears in the map and in the ledger. For the map, a rover out of contact for a day drove into 0.8 hazards its copy did not show, and one out for fourteen days into 13.8, all during the outage; after contact the count was at most 1.6 in any cell. The order of a sync therefore protects a rover from the moment it is back in contact and not before; a rover that is out for days needs hazard updates from the rovers it meets, or has to stay in reach. For the ledger, a ledger bundle that lives 14 days does not outlast an Earth outage of 24.8 days, so 11 of the month's 31 daily bundles expire with store-and-forward and none with a lifetime of 25.8 days, a calculation from the calendar. In each case the protocol's margins, lifetimes and timeouts have to be set by the longest time and the farthest place a vehicle is out of reach, not by the average. Several of the revision's results are not findings at all: the map's share of a full resend, the busiest link's load, the ledger's lost bundles and the ranging half of the clock hypothesis are arithmetic on inputs we assumed, and Table 18 now says so. The two checks on real data (Section 5.13) do not change any of this. They show that the transformation to azimuth and elevation under the Earth windows agrees with the toolkit it is built on, for a spacecraft that was below the base's horizon throughout, so the terrain mask was not exercised; and that a limb fit recovers range and direction from a real image with a bias we can see and have not removed.

6.2 Comparison with earlier studies

The radio traffic of Lunar ATC at the sources, 48.0 B/s per robot (49.1 B/s with authentication), is 287 times smaller (281 times with authentication) than the 13.8 kB/s the two-rover convoy controller of [25] used; the market's own messages come to 0.34 per robot-hour at today's fleet. Nearly all of those bytes are the status heartbeat at 1 Hz (Section 5.2 gives its share), so the honest comparison is between a one-message-a-second heartbeat and a convoy controller that exchanges state many times a second, not between a market and a convoy. The mesh cannot be said to agree with NASA's viewsheds [16], only to be of the same order: a 10 m mast on this ridge sees 23.3 % of the route sample points along the roads within 5 km, and that study found a 4 m tower covering 10.6–23.7 % and a 20 m tower 12.1–29.2 % of the area of a 20 × 20 km region at another site (Section 2.2). The two are different quantities, at different sites and tower heights, and they were not matched: the verification suite's check of exactly this comparison was skipped as unfair for that reason. The rest of the network is multi-hop, as the Subterranean Challenge teams found underground [54, 55]. The smallest owner of the 50/35/15 split gained under the posted price with Shapley sharing (+213 M USD a year [192, 236] in the grand coalition against working alone) and lost 85 M USD against the no-payment market, where Kondor's smallest operator paid the largest fleet penalty [39]; the operator's budget under the second price was a deficit. Kondor's figure is a non-coordination penalty, the extra fleet an operator needs outside a pool, and ours is a gain over working alone inside one, so the two are not the same quantity and agree only in the direction of who fares worst. The second-price deficit is mechanical rather than a prediction of theory: the payout is floored at the carrier's own ask and the tariff is the reserve when no competitor exists, so on every job the operator pays out at least what the shipper pays in and on most jobs more.

6.3 Implications

For the companies that will own the vehicles, the implication of Section 3.2 is that the first base cannot be served by any one of them, and that the first thing worth agreeing is not a market but a coupling: two rovers of two owners lifting one load, which is the only way anything above 2,000 kg moves in this fleet [25, 60]. For the designers of those vehicles, Table 3 is the list of what a vehicle must carry to take part in a shared network: one small program that refuses unsafe moves, a radio that broadcasts a few dozen bytes a second of reports to whatever it can see and relays the market's and the roads' events, and a copy of the entries it has heard; Section 5.1 gives the time of the first and an estimate of the last. For the agencies that write LunaNet, the surface-to-surface link that the specification leaves bilateral [15] is where every number in Section 5.3 lives. A lower band would not lengthen a hop over flat ground: beyond the breakpoint the two-ray loss does not depend on frequency (Proposition 1). What a lunar UHF band [24] would change is diffraction around terrain, where the same ridge is a smaller obstacle at a longer wavelength [20], and with it the share of points behind a ridge that still have a link; that band may be used only outside the shielded zone of the far side [24]. The longer wavelength cuts the other way as well: its Fresnel zone is wider, and only 1.8 % of rover pairs at 2–5 km keep 0.6 of the first Fresnel zone clear at 400 MHz, against 5.4 % at 2.4 GHz (Section 3.3), so the case for the band is not made by diffraction alone. For the operator of the base, the payment rule is a policy choice with a measured price: every rule kept every owner in; the budget was a balanced budget under the posted price and a deficit under the second price. The choice is distributional: owners A and B prefer any paid rule to none and owner C prefers none, so the operator's measured price of a payment rule is owner C's loss against the no-payment market (Section 5.6). For whoever writes the site's rules, the layers under the market add three. Every sender declares the frame and the datum of its positions and the scale of its time stamps, and a position without a declaration is refused: in the protocol's second version that costs a few bytes in a periodic heartbeat and nothing in the report (Section 3.8). The lifetime of a bundle bound for Earth exceeds the longest planned Earth outage, the blackout plus the direct-to-Earth gaps around it, by a margin. And ranging beacons are not placed on one line. Earth's role in this design is the one it can play from 400,000 km away: it sets the prices and the rules, keeps the ledger, settles the accounts and audits a sample, and nothing it does has to happen within a radio window.

6.4 Limitations

The findings hold for one base, one fleet of eight vehicles in three ownerships, one radio model with assumed antenna heights, range and loss, and one implementation of the software; the sizing is two timed computations scaled to flight hardware by a factor, an estimate and a laptop measurement of memory and a computed radio load, none of them measured on a vehicle; the market has three owners, one manipulation and one private-cost spread, prices each run after one allocation with no owner responding to a rule, and tests individual rationality against a weak alone baseline, at one assumed value of time; the Shapley transfers are a definition of fairness, not a measurement; and H5's decision rules were written after the registration, before any run. The layers of the revision are tested on one recorded month by replaying it, with sensor, clock and map numbers that are assumed or taken from Earth and Mars, several of their registered hypotheses are calculations and not tests, and nothing in them has met a radio or the lunar surface. Section 7 states each in full.

6.5 Market and reality

Who has flown what. As of 11 November 2026 nobody has flown cooperating rovers on the Moon, of one owner or of several. CADRE, three rovers and a base station of one owner, was finished in February 2024 and has been with the lander's maker since February 2025 [106]; its lander, IM-3, has moved to the first quarter of 2027, as the company's chief executive said on an earnings call reported on 15 September 2026 [81], while NASA's and JPL's own pages still gave 2026 when we read them [10]. It is built and tested, not flown. NASA's Lunar Terrain Vehicle Phase 1 task orders of 26 May 2026 went to two makers, Astrolab for 219 million USD and Lunar Outpost for 220 million USD, with both vehicles to be on the Moon by 2028 [1]: two crew rovers of two makers for one customer, and no coordination method published. NASA's release of 7 November 2026 adds a nuclear-powered rover, PROMISE, with a launch target of late 2028 [107]. China's Chang'e 8, planned for 2028 to 2029, is to carry, besides its lander and rover, a robot of about 100 kg, a Pakistani rover of about 35 kg and two micro-robots, built by at least four organisations in three countries; it is the first planned mission on which robots of several makers and countries share a site, and no coordination method is published for it. That description rests on a secondary source, an encyclopedia page that cites trade press [108]. Makers already share a lander: Griffin Mission One, not yet launched, is to carry two rovers of two companies, with no stated cooperation between them, again according to an encyclopedia page [120]. A search on 11 November 2026 found no company that sells a coordination layer for lunar vehicles of different makers, which is an absence of evidence and not a proof of absence [68].

Customers. In practice there is one buyer today, NASA's Moon Base programme and its contractors. On 7 November 2026 the ceiling of the first lunar-delivery contract was raised to 4.2 billion USD and a second, ten-year contract with a combined ceiling of 10 billion USD was awarded to eight companies [107]; a ceiling is not an order. A coordination layer for a mixed fleet has a user only when three or more owners work one site, the number at which NASA's policy analysis says the difficulties compound and at which a partner it quotes said some centralised coordination “may become necessary” [51]. Current plans put that at 2028 to 2029, with the two crew rovers, the nuclear rover, the hopping drones and payload rovers of the delivery contracts near the south pole [1, 107]. The likely first buyers, in order, are the programme office that holds the task orders and can write an interface into them, the two rover makers, the lander companies that carry other owners' rovers, and the relay operators. We found no statement by NASA that mixed-fleet coordination will be required in a task order [68].

Market size. Published totals for a lunar economy to 2050 run from 94 to 127 billion USD in one consultancy's report of 2026 to 343 to 566 billion USD in another's of the same year; the same first consultancy gave 170 billion USD to 2040 in 2021 [109]. We read press reports of these three, not the reports. The one demand-side study we read, written for NASA in 2020, found most firms' business cases tied to space agencies [110]. The two reports of 2026 differ by a factor of about four over the same horizon, none has been tested against a year of revenue, none sizes surface mobility alone, and none sizes a coordination layer. This paper therefore quotes no market size for Lunar ATC. What it can state is how many vehicles and owners are planned for one site, and what coordinating them costs and saves in simulation ([60, 61] and Section 5).

Validation on Earth. Every result in this paper is from simulation. Table 16 sets out the stages that would replace simulation with evidence, each with one question and with pass criteria written before the stage is run; a stage that fails sends work back, not forward (Design, [68]). The first two stages need only the authors' time. The third needs mesh radios and a channel emulator, the method NASA Glenn used for lunar LTE studies with field measurements on a lava flow [111]. The fourth needs three to five small rovers and two software teams that see only the protocol document. The fifth needs a partner and a site, and is best timed with CADRE's flight data. No facility gives lunar gravity to a driving vehicle, so braking has to be tested at scaled speeds and said to be so. Table 17 lists whom we would ask for what.

6.6 Future work

No analysis is awaited. Two design changes follow from the results and are not made here: a covariance, or at least the two axes of the error, in the position report, which the second version of the protocol still lacks, and a cap on the items a robot evaluates in one auction round, so that its bid computation stays within the decision interval at larger fleets. After them come the experiments a reviewer will ask for and that we have not run: the readings of H9′'s second clause at eight times the fleet; E50 with the beacon survey error and the ultra-wideband reach swept; the traffic model with 1.0 m antennas for the small classes and the report rate tied to motion; the safety check in a compiled language on the laptop, to bound the language factor before any scaling; E46 with the value of time and the private-cost spread swept, a best response in place of four fixed factors, and five owners; E54 with the comparator's retry budget swept and with the lost-link return and the caution-speed crossing of an unconfirmed segment driven in the engine; E52 with its change rates swept; and E51's conflict check at four and eight times the fleet. Then the stages of Table 16 in their order. Four further pieces follow directly. The safety program and the auction should be run on the flight-class processor itself, against recorded traffic from the companion experiments, to replace the scaling factor with a measurement. The link budget should be checked against a measured lunar surface link as soon as one exists; none does today [17, 101]. The market should be run with five owners and with the manipulations this paper dropped, false availability, charger hogging and cherry-picking, which an auditable ledger may or may not catch. And the Phase-1 rovers' real battery, mass and radio should replace the assumed values when their design reviews publish them, due in late 2026 [1].

7. Limitations

The sizing is scaled, not flown. The processor time is of one Python function on a laptop, timed on the same moves for every class with no reservation entries in the call, and scaled by the ratio of a reference flight processor's benchmark to the laptop's. The factor is stated with a range, but a flight implementation in another language on another operating system could differ from it by more than that range; a compiled implementation would be expected to be faster, and that expectation is not measured. The memory has two figures, a sum of estimates and a laptop measurement of the interpreter's allocations for the on-robot parts; neither counts the interpreter, the code or the numerical library's buffers, and neither is the memory of flight software. One robot's bid computation was timed in one month of one seed per fleet size; scaled by the same assumed factor it exceeds the one-second decision at the 99th percentile at four and eight times the fleet, although at the low end of the factor's range it would stay within it (Section 5.1). The flight processor's benchmark is taken from the experiment's design file, which cites the maker's presentation [121]; our research notes do not record that it was opened. The factor used is 54.8 (range 36.5–64.0), from Intel Core i5-10500H (6 cores, 2.5 GHz base, 4.5 GHz turbo) to the Frontgrade Gaisler GR740 (rad-hard quad-core LEON4FT, 250 MHz). The laptop's benchmark is itself assumed: no published CoreMark exists for its processor, and the figure is 6.0 CoreMark/MHz times its 4.5 GHz turbo clock (Assumed, range 4–7 CoreMark/MHz), so the factor's range is the range of that assumption. The processor share reported for H10 takes the 99th-percentile call time at every call, which overstates the mean load.

The auction round and the safety loop are not allocated to cores. The reference processor has four cores, and the paper does not say which of them runs the 10 Hz safety loop and which the auction round. On a shared core, a bid computation that exceeds one second, which Section 5.1 reports at the 99th percentile at four and eight times the fleet, would stall the safety check while it ran; the design must give the safety check its own core or bound the round's time, and this paper states that requirement and leaves it open.

The radio is a model. Line of sight on a 5 m terrain grid with assumed antenna heights, a fixed range and a fixed loss probability stands in for a radio that nobody has measured on the Moon; the link budget uses the flat-ground two-ray law with a smooth-Moon horizon check and has no diffraction term: a path the terrain blocks carries nothing in the model, a path in line of sight is treated as flat ground, and the regolith's permittivity is unmeasured [21]. The share of route sample points in direct view of the mast is a property of this model on this ridge, and it is a share of points, not of time. The mesh counts a ray that grazes the ground as a link, where the knife-edge figure of our own reference is 6 dB of loss [20]; with 0.6 of the first Fresnel zone clear, fewer pairs have a link (Section 3.3). The flat-ground law is used at hops close to the horizon, where it is not conservative: a calculation over a spherical ground with the method of [20] gives a higher loss and lower rates there, and the rovers' longest hop then no longer carries a megabit a second (Section 5.3 and Table 7 give the figures). The runs use the flat-ground law; the spherical-ground column is beside it and changes no run, because every link the mesh uses still closes the narrow channel. Hidden terminals and fading are not modelled, and whether a base that sees a minority of the route points can schedule a channel for the fleet at all is not shown.

The link claim is conditional. The 16 kbit/s target is met only on a base-scheduled channel on which every robot is within one hop of the base. That condition is taken from positions that are parked in 97 % of vehicle-steps, while the traffic is that of vehicles reporting once a second whether they move or not; a driving vehicle has a radio path to the base 57 % of the time (Section 5.2). The traffic model gives every rover a 2.5 m antenna, although half the fleet carries 1.0 m, and sends status and intent at 0.1 Hz; with all three reports at 1 Hz the load at the sources alone, 19.52 kbit/s, is above the target. Drawn from driving steps only, 20 rovers are 1.58 hops from the base on average and not one (Section 5.2).

The protocol declares what the design wanted in every report, and carries no covariance. The design of Sections 3.5 to 3.7 asked for a frame, a datum, a time scale and a covariance with every position. Version 0.2 gives the first three by a declaration per sender, bound to its key epoch, not by fields in each report, and it gives a scalar 95 % bound where the design asked for a covariance; a receiver that needs the shape of the error does not get it. The identifiers are the site's own registry and are registered nowhere. The experiments were run with version 0.1; the figures of version 0.2 are a calculation on the same traffic model, which sends 11 of the 15 message types, and they are packet sizes: a Proximity-1 frame adds 12 B a packet and coding more. The 45 conformance vectors of each version test the codec against itself; no second implementation and no radio has read them.

The link budget was corrected once. The first version of this sizing took free-space loss at the worst hop and so overstated the rates of the two classes with the lowest antennas there by more than two orders of magnitude. At the 5 km worst hop it gave the small rover 5,010 kbit/s and the hopper 5,988 kbit/s; under the flat-ground law that hop would carry 18.1 kbit/s and 36.0 kbit/s if the two antennas could see each other, 277 and 166 times less, and they cannot: two 1.0 m antennas are beyond their 3.73 km smooth-Moon horizon there. For the crew rover and the cargo rover the rate at that hop fell from 6,976 kbit/s to 1,574 kbit/s, and for the base mast's downlink from 9,289 kbit/s to 4,717 kbit/s. An internal check against the literature found the error on 11 November 2026, and it was corrected: Sections 4.2, 5.2 and 5.3 hold the corrected analysis and change 11 of [59] records it. No measurement of path loss on the lunar surface exists, so every range and rate here is a model.

The fleet is the fleet of 2028–2032 as published in October 2026. A nuclear-powered rover announced on 7 November 2026 for late 2028 [107] is not in it. The cargo rovers are uncontracted, their masses, batteries and one capacity are assumed, and the Phase-1 rovers' batteries are set so that the published 10 km per charge comes out [1], because no battery size is published. The design reviews due in late 2026 and early 2027 may change every assumed value in Table 2.

Three owners, one manipulation. The market is tested with three owners in two splits; the theory's warnings about collusion and shill identities [31] concern exactly such thin markets and are not tested. Bid scaling is the only misreport simulated; an owner that misreports position, availability or job completion, or that hogs a charger, is not. The audits compare bids with logged costs and assume the logs are honest.

H5's decision rules are dated after the registration. The first-price cell that H5's first two clauses need was dropped on 27 October and restored on 29 October, before any E46 row (change 7 of [59]); the numeric decision rules for all three clauses were written then, not in the original registration, and are dated in [59].

The value of time is assumed and decides the money. Delay is priced at the hourly rates of Section 3.9, by priority, and cargo that is not delivered within the year is priced up to the year's end. Both are assumptions of the scenario, and neither was varied. The alone loss of the owner with the small vehicles is almost entirely such censored cargo (Section 5.6), so the alone baseline, the Shapley values, the pooling gain and the transfers scale with those two choices; a sweep of the value of time belongs with the sweep of the private-cost spread, and neither was run.

Private costs are assumed and their spread is not swept. Each owner's cost of energy, wear and time is the engine's job cost times a lognormal multiplier with σ = 0.2, a distribution we chose; profits and individual rationality are measured against those costs. The declared range of 0.1–0.3 was not run, because each value needs a new year of physical runs, so the results hold at one spread, and no spread replaces a company's own accounting.

Payments are priced after one allocation. Each rule prices a run that the auction has already allocated, so no owner responds to a rule and the honest allocation is the same under all four rules. The manipulation test is one deviator at a time over four fixed factors; no best response, no joint deviation and no equilibrium is computed. The gains reported are what a fixed scaling would have earned, not what a strategic owner would find.

The efficiency loss differs between the splits. The market's efficiency loss against the oracle differs between equal and 50/35/15 shares (Table 8); nothing in this paper traces that difference to a cause, and it is left untraced.

The alone baseline is weak. An owner alone is dominated by its cargo's delay cost at the assumed value of time, so individual rationality against alone is easy to pass under every rule; the comparison against the pooled market with no payments is the harder one, and Section 5.6 reports it.

The Shapley transfers are a definition, not a measurement. The transfers under the posted price are large relative to the cash the tariff moves, and they follow from the Shapley rule's definition of fairness; the paper measures what that definition pays each owner, not whether the owners would call it fair.

The first-price clause failed for power. The registered measure, the deviator's whole profit, carries the deviator's own cargo delay, whose spread over seeds is many times the cash the scaled asks earn; the cash part is positive (Section 6.1, post hoc). The clause is not supported as registered, and that is not evidence that shading does not pay.

The first-price budget is a calibration gap. With honest asks the first-price budget is the tariff receipts less the true carrier cost; the tariff was set to the mean cost per tonne-kilometre on the calibration seeds, so the budget on the test seeds is the gap between the two seed sets, not a property of the rule.

The lander carries only the radio unit and the landing announcement. In this design a lander's own Earth link is for flight; its Lunar ATC radio unit broadcasts the landing announcement on the mesh, Earth also holds the time, and the lander is otherwise a closure; it carries no planner and no safety program (change 7 of [59], Table 3), and the radio unit and announcement part it does carry are not sized here, because E48 measures the on-robot part on the driving and flying classes.

The layers of the revision are tested on one recorded month, by replay. E50, E51 and E54 take the trajectories of one month, July 2028, of one scenario, three equal owners at the middle load under the auction regime, and compute on them what position error, clock error and message loss would have been. No vehicle drives differently because its position is wrong or its message is lost. E51 and E54 run at today's fleet only; E51's check rests on the reservation pairs of that one fleet size, and the larger fleets, where reservations are frequent, were not checked. E54 is a simulation of messages, not of the engine: the return of a silent vehicle is applied to the contact plan and not driven, although the ladder calls for many returns a seed (Section 5.12); a returning vehicle is given a direct link to the base, which is optimistic; the comparator's retry budget is our choice and was not swept; and a lost job there is hypothetical, because in the recorded run every job was done. The run used a lost-link rule that released a silent holder's reservation; that rule is withdrawn, the count of segments left unconfirmed with the ladder in force was not recomputed, and the cost of crossing them at caution speed is not priced.

Several registered hypotheses are calculations. H12, H13, the ranging half of H11, the second clause of H14 as first run, and the ledger's lost bundles could not have come out otherwise: they are arithmetic on assumed tile counts, message sizes, report rates, a transfer residual and a calendar. They are reported as design calculations, and only what could have failed is called tested (Table 18).

“Near the base” is under a quarter of the driving. The region in which ranging holds the position budget, two or more beacons in line of sight, covers 23 % of driving steps and was chosen after the results. In the simulation, on assumed ranging errors, the 95th percentile at today's fleet is 24.7 m within a kilometre of the base and 50.1 m under the registered wording, outside the budget in every seed. Inside the narrow region the result rests on the long-range ranging error, an assumption without a lunar measurement: at 10 m in place of 3 m the percentile is 9.6 m. The beacon survey error and the ultra-wideband reach were not swept, and the readings were not computed at eight times the fleet. Inside the region 2.9 % of the steps still exceed the budget.

The navigation model's numbers are not lunar. Every sensor value of Table 4 comes from tests on Earth or Mars, from a requirement or from an assumption; none was swept by us, although the research notes give a range for each [68]. E50's filter is matched to its own error model, with no outlier, no biased range and no wrong fix accepted as good, so its error is a lower limit for that model and not a prediction for a rover. Ultra-wideband ranging, terrain fixes and visual odometry with lamps have not been measured on the Moon, and no published figure gives the probability of a wrong fix that is declared good, which the traffic rules need more than the mean error. The model gives a hopper the same sensors as a rover and does not model its flight. Errors start at zero. The navigation service's assumed accuracy is the providers' target, which is better than the 17.2 m (95 %) that their own simulation of five satellites gives with terrain height [77], and its availability by year is our reading of plans, one of which has already slipped. The terrain-fix stops are in one delay figure only, E50b's, and there as a lower bound, because the stops on repositioning drives are not inserted. How long reservations overlap when position is wrong, and how far apart the vehicles are at that moment (task B-102), is not in this version; the companion paper [61] will report it.

The clocks are simple. A clock in E51 has a constant frequency error and, for the atomic class, a linear ageing; random noise is left out, and temperature enters only in the post hoc variant, as a daily cycle whose period is assumed. The crystal's range comes from one receiver on one lander, read from a figure's axis [82], and using that one crystal's excursion as the spread of constant offsets across vehicles is our choice; the transfer's assumed residual alone is tens of metres of one-way range (Section 5.9); the atomic clock's values are a data sheet's, for a model its maker does not rate for space [86]. The guard of 0.1 s is our choice; the guard sweep, frequency steering and mixed scales were added after the first results. The lunar time scale itself is not defined in practice, so the scale named in a message is a field, not a fact.

The map experiment's world is assumed. The rate at which tiles, hazards, keep-out zones and road edges change, and the share of hazards a rover cannot see for itself, are assumptions that E52 does not sweep; its hazards are drawn, not sensed, and it does not run inside the engine. The tile sizes rest on compression rates measured on smooth orbital terrain and on assumed rates for rover-made layers, which real sensor noise will compress worse. The limits by which the base accepts a contribution are our choices. The reference-system standard that would fix the frame of the navigation service was not published when we looked.

Security is a design with one property counted. The acceptance of forgeries was counted at 8 and 16 bits; the figure for the 64-bit code the design uses is the formula's. An authentication code stops outsiders: a vehicle with a valid key that lies is not stopped by it, and the detection of such a vehicle is tested in the companion paper [61], not here. Key distribution, certificates and their renewal are not simulated. The trust anchor is an assumption, because no standard names one for a site with several owners. The message-level security copies the layout of a link-security standard and is not that standard. Jamming is not modelled.

The real-data checks are checks of code, not of a rover. The Artemis II frames are a handful of usable images from one camera, uncalibrated, with a bias in radius and in angle that we report and have not explained; they test limb navigation at flyby range. The Earth-link comparison tests our azimuth and elevation against the toolkit they are built on, along one trajectory on which the spacecraft never rose above the base's horizon; it does not exercise the terrain mask that makes the Earth windows. No result of this paper uses code that turns an image into a position.

9. Conclusion

We asked what the vehicles of 2028–2032 can carry, what a shared network needs in and under each, and which payment rules let distrustful owners gain by joining. We answered from published sources, a timed implementation, 1,080 simulated years under four payment rules and pre-registered experiments. The fleet's capacity plateaus at 2,800 kg per lift, set by two uncontracted cargo rovers. Scaled to flight hardware the safety check fits one core. Twenty robots' traffic fits the 16 kbit/s link only on a base-scheduled channel with every robot one hop from the base, which holds at sites and not on the road. One robot's bid takes 0.29 s (99th percentile) at today's fleet and 1.19 s at eight times it, above the one-second decision. Every owner beat alone under every rule, but owner C lost against no payments under every paid rule, and the largest gain from scaling bids was +7.7 M USD a year, interval including zero. Ranging holds the 5 m position budget where two or more beacons are in line of sight (23 % of driving steps), not under wider readings of near the base, and terrain fixes do not hold it elsewhere. Authentication adds 52 % to the bit rate, and senders now declare frame, datum and time scale.

The payment rule is a policy choice, priced in budget and transfers. The results hold for one base, one fleet and a modelled radio; several are calculations on assumed inputs; nothing is measured on the Moon.

Appendix A. Guarantees and derivations

Each result below is stated with its assumptions, derived step by step, and followed by a worked example whose numbers the build script computes from the stated constants and compares with the results files where they hold the same quantity. All eight are statements about models. None is a measurement on the Moon, and each is only as good as its assumptions, which are named so that they can be attacked.

Proposition 1 (loss over flat ground, and the radio horizon). Assumptions. Two antennas at heights h1 and h2 above flat ground, a horizontal distance d apart, d much larger than both heights and small against the radio horizon, so that the bulge of the sphere at mid-path, d2/8R, is small against both heights; a direct ray and one ray reflected by the ground with a reflection coefficient of −1, its limit at grazing incidence; wavelength λ; no atmosphere; for the horizon, a smooth sphere of radius R. Statement. (a) The received power is Pr/Pt = GtGr(λ/4πd)2 · 4 sin2(2πh1h2/λd). (b) Write Lfs(d) = 20 log10(4πd/λ) for free space and L2(d) = 40 log10 d − 20 log10(h1h2). The law this paper uses is L = Lfs up to the breakpoint db = 4h1h2/λ and the larger of Lfs and L2 beyond it. The two are equal at dc = 4πh1h2/λ = πdb, so L = Lfs between db and dc, L = L2 beyond dc, L is continuous, and beyond dc it does not depend on frequency. (c) The two antennas are in view of each other on the sphere only if d ≤ √(2Rh1) + √(2Rh2).

Derivation. (1) The direct path has length √(d2 + (h1 − h2)2) and the reflected path, by the image of one antenna below the ground, √(d2 + (h1 + h2)2). For d much larger than the heights each root is d plus its second term over 2d, so the paths differ by ((h1 + h2)2 − (h1 − h2)2)/2d = 2h1h2/d. (2) The phase difference is φ = 2π/λ times that, 4πh1h2/λd. The reflected field has the amplitude of the direct one, to first order, and the opposite sign, so the sum has power |1 − eiφ|2 = 4 sin2(φ/2) times the free-space power, which is (a). (3) The factor has its last maximum where φ/2 = π/2, at db; beyond it the sine falls steadily. (4) Where φ/2 is small the sine equals its argument, the factor is φ2, and Pr/Pt = GtGr(h1h2)2/d4: the wavelength cancels, and in decibels the loss is L2. (5) Lfs = L2 means (4πd/λ)2 = d4/(h1h2)2, that is d = dc. Between db and dc the exact factor of (a) falls from 4 to 0.92, from 6 dB less loss than free space to 0.36 dB more; the law takes free space there and so does not credit the constructive interference. Beyond dc it takes L2. Because sin x is below x, the exact two-ray loss is above L2 everywhere, by 10 log10((φ/2)2/sin2(φ/2)), which beyond dc is largest at dc itself, where φ/2 = 1/2 and the difference is 0.36 dB, and shrinks with distance. From the breakpoint outward the law is therefore optimistic by at most 0.36 dB against the two-ray model it approximates; against real ground it is a model [21]. (6) A ray from height h that grazes the sphere meets it at a distance s with (R + h)2 = R2 + s2, so s = √(2Rh + h2), which is √(2Rh) for h much smaller than R; two antennas see each other when their grazing rays can meet, which is (c). Worked example, the constants of E48: at 2.4 GHz the wavelength is 0.1249 m and free-space loss at 5 km is 114.0 dB; for two rover antennas at 2.5 m the breakpoint is at 200.1 m, the two laws meet at 628.8 m, the loss at 5 km is 132.0 dB and the mutual horizon is 5.89 km, so that hop is within the horizon; for two small-robot antennas at 1.0 m the breakpoint is at 32.0 m, the two laws meet at 100.6 m, the loss at 5 km is 148.0 dB and the mutual horizon is 3.73 km, so that hop is beyond the horizon. The build checks each of these against the link budget's file. These hops do not meet the assumption that the ground is flat. The sphere's bulge at mid-path, d2/8R, is 1.80 m of the rover antennas' 2.5 m at 5 km, and 1.00 m, the antenna height itself, at the 3.73 km horizon of two 1.0 m antennas, where Table 7 evaluates the law for the small classes. A calculation of the loss there with the smooth-sphere method of our own reference [20] (ITU-R P.526, section 3.2, checked against its text) gives 137.0 dB against the 132.0 dB above for the rover antennas, and 146.6 dB against 142.9 dB for the small ones, 4 to 5 dB higher, which puts the flat-ground rates of Table 7 2.2 to 2.4 times too high for the vehicle hops and 1.3 times for the mast (701, 24.6, 48.9 and 3,653 kbit/s in place of 1,574, 57.9, 114 and 4,717 for the crew and cargo rovers, the small rover, the hopper and the mast; Table 7 has both columns). The internal referee of this paper raised the point and the calculation confirms it; it is a calculation, not a measurement. Near the horizon the law is therefore not conservative, and the 0.36 dB of the derivation bounds its error against the flat two-ray model only.

Proposition 2 (the rate a hop can carry). Assumptions. The loss L(d) of Proposition 1; for the last sentence of the statement, the same antenna heights, transmit power and gains on every hop of the route; additive white Gaussian noise at 290 K in a bandwidth B, a receiver noise figure F and a margin M taken off the signal-to-noise ratio (all in decibels); ideal coding, so the result is an upper bound on what a real modem delivers. Statement. The capacity of the hop is C(d) = B log2(1 + 10S/10) with S = Pt + Gt + Gr − L(d) − N − M and N = −174 dBm/Hz + 10 log10 B + F. C does not increase with d, so on a route whose hops share their heights, power and gains the rate at the longest hop is a lower bound on the rate of every hop. Derivation. (1) Thermal noise of density kT is −174 dBm per hertz at 290 K; in the bandwidth B, with the receiver's figure, it is N. (2) The received power is Pt + Gt + Gr − L(d) in decibels, and S is what remains above the noise after the margin. (3) Shannon's formula gives C. (4) Lfs and L2 both grow with d, so their maximum does, S falls, and C falls with it. Worked example, the constants of E48: a bandwidth of 1 MHz, antenna gains of 3 dBi, a noise figure of 5 dB and a margin of 10 dB give a noise power of −109 dBm; at the worst hop the crew rover at 1.00 W (30.0 dBm) has a signal-to-noise ratio of +3.0 dB after the margin and a rate of 1,574 kbit/s and the small rover at 0.25 W (24.0 dBm) has a signal-to-noise ratio of −19.0 dB after the margin and a rate of 18.1 kbit/s if its two antennas could see each other, which by Proposition 1 they cannot. The last sentence of the statement needs its assumption: on a route with a 5 km hop between two rovers (1,574 kbit/s) and a 3.73 km hop between two small rovers (57.9 kbit/s) the lowest rate is on the shorter hop. Every rate here and in Table 7 is the Shannon bound of the channel after the margin, not what a modem delivers.

Proposition 3 (the rate of a lunar clock against a terrestrial one). Assumptions. Earth and Moon as point masses on a circular orbit of radius a about their common centre of mass; the Sun left out, because it acts on the difference only through tides; first order in 1/c2; the terrestrial clock on the geoid; the lunar clock at rest on the surface at radius RM, the Moon's rotation neglected. Statement. The lunar clock gains on the terrestrial one at the rate LG − LL − Lorb, where LG is the defining constant of the International Astronomical Union for a clock on the geoid (a definition, which [23] tabulates and does not derive), LL = GMM/(RMc2), and Lorb = [GME/a + (1 − μ)2v2/2 − GMM/a − μ2v2/2]/c2, with μ = MM/(ME + MM) and v2 = G(ME + MM)/a. Derivation. (1) To first order a clock's proper time runs against coordinate time at 1 − (U + w2/2)/c2, with U the Newtonian potential at the clock, taken positive, and w its speed. (2) For the terrestrial clock, Earth's own potential and rotation give LG by definition; the Moon adds the potential GMM/a; and Earth moves about the centre of mass at μ times the relative orbital speed v, which adds μ2v2/2. (3) For the lunar clock, the Moon's own potential at its surface gives LL; Earth adds GME/a; and the Moon moves at (1 − μ)v. (4) Subtracting the two rates leaves the statement. The orbit's eccentricity adds a periodic term of 0.11 µs a day in rate, about half a microsecond in accumulated time over a month [23, 84], which this derivation leaves out. Worked example, with GME = 3.986004418 × 1014 and GMM = 4.90280031 × 1012 m3/s2, a = 3.84399 × 108 m, RM = 1,737.4 km and LG = 6.969290134 × 10−10 [23]: μ = 0.012151 and v2 = 1.0497 × 106 m2/s2; the three terms are LG = 60.2147, LL = 2.7128 and Lorb = 1.4769 µs a day, and the rate is 60.2147 − 2.7128 − 1.4769 = 56.02 µs a day, 6.4844 × 10−10; more decimals would not mean anything, because a kilometre of site height moves the rate by about 0.002 and the eccentricity term left out is 0.11 periodic. The clock model of E51 uses this constant, and the build checks the two against each other.

Proposition 4 (range error from clock error, and holdover). Assumptions. Signals travel at c; a clock's error after a synchronisation with residual x0 is x(t) = x0 + yt + At2/2, a constant fractional frequency error y and a linear ageing A; random noise is left out. Statement. (a) A one-way range between a transmitter with clock error δt and a receiver with δr is wrong by c(δr − δt): 1 ns is 0.2998 m. (b) A two-way range, in which A sends, B replies after a delay T and A halves the round trip less T, does not contain the clock offsets; its error is cT(eA − eB)/2 for fractional rate errors eA and eB, plus a term of the order of the range times eA. (c) With x0 = 0 the clock stays within a budget ε for t = ε/y if A = 0, and for t = (−y + √(y2 + 2Aε))/A otherwise. (d) A position stamped with a time that is wrong by D is wrong by vD at speed v; a time slot needs a guard of at least 2D plus the path delay; and a road segment handed from one vehicle to the next needs a margin of at least DA + DB + σA/vA + σB/vB, the two clock errors plus each vehicle's along-track position error over its speed. Derivation. (a) The receiver computes c times its reading of the arrival time less the stamped sending time; each reading carries its clock's error. (b) A measures the round trip on its own clock and B the delay on its own; the offsets drop out of both differences; what remains is that A's clock runs eA fast over the round trip and B's eB fast over T, and half the difference of the two is the stated error. (c) Solve x(t) = ε for t; with A positive the quadratic has one positive root. (d) Each is a distance or a time moved at a rate. Worked example, the two classes of E51 at their 95th-percentile frequency error: the crystal at y = 7.6 × 10−7 holds the 0.1 s of a reservation for 36.5 h and the 3.34 ns of a 1 m one-way range for 4.4 ms; the atomic clock at y = 5.9 × 10−10 with A = 3.5 × 10−16 per second holds them for 259 days and 5.7 s. With a reply delay of 1 ms and the crystal's largest rate difference, single-sided two-way ranging is wrong by at most 0.12 m; E51 gave 0.084 m at the 95th percentile. Table 12 lists 258 days and 5.5 s for the atomic clock where this example has 259 days and 5.7 s: the table takes the fifth percentile of the holdover over the drawn clocks, the example the formula at the 95th-percentile frequency error, and the build checks that the two agree within 6 %. Both are the same closed form, so that agreement is of a formula with itself. Part (c) sets the residual x0 to zero. The model's own time transfer leaves 100 ns, which is 30 m of one-way range, so the budgets of 1 m and 10 m of one-way range are exceeded at the instant of synchronisation and their holdover times are not reachable with that transfer (Table 12 marks them).

Proposition 5 (the error of reading a position in the wrong lunar frame). Assumptions. The Mean Earth and Principal Axis frames share their origin and differ by a constant rotation M; positions are on or near a sphere of radius R; the rotation is small, with rotation vector θ. Statement. A position r whose coordinates are read in the other frame is displaced by δ = (M − I)r. To first order δ = θ × r, so |δ| ≤ |θ|R with equality where r is perpendicular to θ, the displacement is horizontal, and at a pole, where r lies along the third axis, |δ| = R√(θ12 + θ22): the part of the rotation about the polar axis moves nothing there. Derivation. (1) Coordinates of the same point in the two frames are related by M; using the numbers of one frame as if they were the other's places the point at Mr instead of r. (2) For a small rotation M = I + [θ]× plus terms of second order in θ, so δ = θ × r. (3) A cross product with r is perpendicular to r, that is, horizontal on a sphere; its length is |θ||r| times the sine of the angle between them. (4) At the pole r = (0, 0, ±R), and θ × r has the length R√(θ12 + θ22). The second-order terms are of the size |θ|2R, under half a metre here. Worked example: the NAIF kernel builds M from rotations of 67.8526″, 78.6944″ and 0.2785″ about axes 3, 2 and 1 [88]. Its rotation angle is 0.02886°, so the largest displacement is 875.2 m; at the south pole it is 662.9 m and at the centre of our terrain tile, 89.4632° S, 222.5104° E, 659.2 m. The build compares all three with the coordinate code of the repository, which agrees to 0.05 m.

Proposition 6 (bytes to bring a tiled map up to date). Assumptions. A layer of N tiles arranged in a quadtree of depth D = ⌈log4 N⌉, each node holding a hash of h bytes of its four children; n tiles differ between the two copies; a changed tile changes its hash and every hash above it (hash collisions are ignored); a tile travels as s bytes plus a header of t bytes, and a version number as v bytes. Statement. Finding the changed tiles by walking the tree costs at most h(1 + 4nD) bytes of hashes sent in one direction, not counting the replies that say which children differ nor any message header, against N(v + h) for a flat list of every tile's version and hash; the tree is the smaller whenever n < N(v + h)/(4Dh). Either way the tiles themselves then cost n(s + t). Derivation. (1) The two sides exchange the root hash, h bytes. (2) A node is opened, its four child hashes sent, only if its own hash differs, which happens only if a changed tile lies below it. (3) At each of the D levels above the tiles at most n nodes have a changed tile below them, so at most nD nodes are opened, each costing 4h. (4) Compare h(1 + 4nD) with N(v + h) and drop the 1. Worked example, one 256 m layer of the 16 km site: N = 3,969, D = 6, h = 8 B, v = 2 B. With n = 42 changed tiles the tree costs at most 8 × (1 + 4 × 42 × 6) = 8,072 B and the flat list 39,690 B; the tree is the smaller for n below 206.7, that is, for up to 206 changed tiles (at n = 206 it costs 39,560 B). The count leaves out the replies and the framing: if every opened node travels as one authenticated message of 28 B of headers and code around its 32 B of hashes, the walk nearly doubles. In E52 the largest walk was 4,880 B for 180 changed tile versions over the layers, far inside the bound because changed tiles lie together along the routes; the build checks every row of E52 against the bound.

Proposition 7 (forged and replayed messages). Assumptions. The authentication code is an ideal one of t bits: to anyone without the key, the code of a message not yet seen is equally likely to be any of the 2t values. A receiver keeps, per sender and key, the highest counter smax it has accepted, and accepts a message only if its code verifies, its counter exceeds smax, and its time stamp is within a window W of the receiver's clock. A sender never uses a counter twice under one key. Statement. (a) A forged message is accepted with probability 2−t, and at least one of q forgeries with probability at most q 2−t. (b) A recorded message with counter s is rejected by every receiver that has accepted that message or any later one from the same sender; a receiver that has not can accept the recording only while its time stamp is within W, and then learns a state that was true at most W earlier. (c) A counter of 32 bits at f messages a second lasts 232/f seconds. Derivation. (a) The forger must name the one right value among 2t equally likely ones; over q tries the probabilities add at most (the union bound). (b) Such a receiver has smax ≥ s, and the second condition fails; otherwise only the third condition stands in the way, and it fails once the stamp is older than W. (c) Counting. The proposition says nothing about a sender that holds a valid key and lies, nor about jamming. Worked example: for t = 64 the bound is 5.4 × 10−20 per attempt and, at ten attempts a second for a year (q = 3.2 × 108), 1.7 × 10−11; for t = 32 the same year gives 0.07, which is why a 4-byte code is excluded. E55 counted 395 acceptances in 100,000 attempts at t = 8 and 11 in 1,000,000 at t = 16, where the proposition predicts 391 and 15.3. A 32-bit counter at one message a second lasts 136 years, so the key is changed by policy long before the counter runs out.

Proposition 8 (how beacon geometry dilutes a ranging fix). Assumptions. A vehicle measures, at one epoch and with no prior knowledge of its position, two-way ranges, so no clock is unknown, to N beacons at known positions, all in line of sight, with independent Gaussian errors of the same standard deviation σr; the problem is horizontal; beacon i is seen at azimuth θi. Statement. No unbiased estimate of the position from that one epoch has a covariance below σr2(HTH)−1, where row i of H is (cos θi, sin θi), and the root of its trace is σr times the dilution Dh = 2√N / √(N2 − |S|2), with S the sum of e2iθ over the beacons. Hence (i) Dh ≥ 2/√N, with equality when S = 0, for example for beacons spread evenly around the vehicle: four such beacons give 1. (ii) If every beacon is seen within an angle α of one direction, α at most 45°, then Dh ≥ 2/(√N sin 2α); for a group of beacons of width b seen from a distance d, tan α = b/2d, and four beacons give Dh ≥ (4d2 + b2)/(4bd), about d/b far away. (iii) For beacons on one line and a vehicle on that line, |S| = N and the bound is infinite: ranges say nothing across the line. Derivation. (1) Each range's sensitivity to the position is the unit vector towards the beacon, so the Fisher information is HTH/σr2 and the Cramér–Rao bound is its inverse. (2) HTH has the entries Σcos2θ = (N + Re S)/2, Σsin2θ = (N − Re S)/2 and Σsin θ cos θ = Im S/2, so its trace is N and its determinant (N2 − |S|2)/4. (3) The trace of the inverse of a 2 by 2 matrix is its trace over its determinant, 4N/(N2 − |S|2); the root is Dh. (4) For (ii), the doubled angles lie within 2α of one direction, so the component of S along it is at least N cos 2α, and N2 − |S|2 is at most N2sin22α. (5) For (iii) every doubled angle is the same. Worked example, the four beacons of E50 (the base mast, the depot, the power plant and one landing pad): they span 1.37 km and lie nearly on one line. Evenly spread around a vehicle they would give a dilution of 1.0. With all four in view the dilution is 8.9 seen from the construction site, 0.26 km from the base; 1.2 seen from the oxygen plant, 1.2 km from the base; 5.2 seen from the science site, 2.1 km from the base; 7.0 seen from the shadowed crater, 4.0 km from the base. It does not simply rise with distance: the site nearest the base is the worst of the four, because it lies almost on the beacons' line (case iii). With the 3 m range error of the base radio the bound on the horizontal error of one epoch (one sigma) is 27 m, 3.7 m, 15 m and 21 m at those four sites. Three things limit what this says about E50. The bound assumes all four beacons in view, while E50's region away from the base is where fewer than two are: it says nothing about the 57 m that E50's ranging layer left there at the 95th percentile. It is a bound for a single epoch of ranges with no prior, while E50 runs a sequential filter with a dead-reckoning prior, which is not bound by it: where two or more beacons are seen E50's median error is 1.1 m, below the single-epoch bound at every one of these sites. And it takes the base radio's range error for every range, while E50 also has ultra-wideband ranges of 0.3 m within 100 m.
