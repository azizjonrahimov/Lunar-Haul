6. Discussion

6.1 What the results say about the question

The discussion follows the two halves: the vehicles and what Lunar ATC needs inside them come first, the market third, and the layers under the market last. The question had three parts: what the vehicles of 2028–2032 can carry, what Lunar ATC needs inside each of them and whether it fits, and under which payment rules a market among owners is fair and hard to game. On the first part the answer needed no experiment and is the least comfortable: the fleet's capacity is a plateau at {{P3-CAP-MAX-PAIR-KG}} per lift, set by two cargo rovers nobody has bought, and the crew rovers NASA has bought carry {{P3-CLV1-PAYLOAD-KG}} and drive {{P3-CLV1-RANGE-KM}} on a charge. Whatever a dispatcher does, elements heavier than the plateau wait for a vehicle that does not exist, and the base's stated need runs to 15,000 kg [5, 6]. The sizing of this paper is for the fleet that will exist, which is small; the network it needs is correspondingly small.

On the second part, {{P3-E48-DISCUSSION-CLAUSE}} The safety program is explicit switching by design, the form that costs least in computation in the measured comparison we found [45], and the planner's learned part is {{P3-LIN-N-WEIGHTS}} weights; the sizing question was always the bookkeeping of the market and the reservation table rather than any arithmetic. E48 answers it on a laptop, scaled: the safety check fits, the tables are small by an estimate and by a measurement, and one robot's bid computation stays under the one-second decision at the 99th percentile at every fleet size at the central scaling factor, passing it at four and eight times the fleet only towards the top of the factor's range. The margin shrinks with the fleet because a robot tries every waiting item in a round: the bookkeeping that grows with the fleet is the bid, not the safety check.

On the third part, {{P3-E46-DISCUSSION-CLAUSE}} {{P3-E46-POSTHOC-SENTENCE}} The registered test of the first clause was therefore underpowered: the measure it registered, the deviator's whole profit, carries the deviator's own delay term, whose spread over seeds is many times the cash, and the cash part on its own is positive. That is a statement about the test's power, made after the fact, not a change of the verdict, which stands as registered. The Shapley sharing of the posted price moves money from the owner of the small vehicles to the two cargo-rover owners: {{P3-E46-TRANSFERS-CLAUSE}}. {{P3-E46-SURPLUS-SENTENCE}} Owner C's loss against no payments (Section 5.6) is the mirror image of A and B being paid for carrying C's cargo, and it follows from the fleet split, not from the payment rule: owner C holds the two small rovers and the two hoppers (Section 3.2), which cannot carry most of its own cargo, so under no payments the cargo rovers of A and B carry that cargo for nothing, under the posted price at the cost-recovery tariff, and the Shapley sharing charges C for the value those vehicles add. That is what the Shapley rule's definition of fairness means, each owner's marginal contribution averaged over the orders in which owners could join, and it is a definition, not a measurement: it gives owner C the largest share of the pooling gain over alone, and C's transfer is the difference between that share and the larger one C keeps when nobody is paid. The alone baseline is weak, because an owner alone is dominated by its cargo's delay cost, so every pooled rule clears it by a wide margin. That cost is delay priced at an assumed hourly rate, and for owner C it is almost wholly cargo that is never delivered alone and is censored at the year's end (Section 5.6): the Shapley values, the pooling gain and the transfers above all carry that assumption, and none of them was computed at another value of time. The harder comparison is against the pooled market with no payments, and there {{P3-E46-VS-NONE-CLAUSE}}. The market's purpose in this design is not to extract the last hour of efficiency but to let three companies share eight vehicles without a treaty; the number that matters for that is whether any owner, in particular the smallest, loses by joining, and E46 measures it directly against both baselines.

On the fourth part, the layers under the market, the results draw one line, between the base and everywhere else. {{P3-P6-DISCUSSION-CLAUSE}} The line is narrower than the first version of this revision drew it. “Near the base” there meant two or more beacons in line of sight, a region of under a quarter of the driving steps that was chosen after the results; under the registered words, or within a kilometre of the base, ranging does not hold the budget in any seed, so the honest statement is that the position budget is held in sight of at least two beacons and nowhere else in this model. The failure of H9′'s third clause is a statement about the sensors as the model has them: where fewer than two beacons are seen the legs are darker and steeper, where the model allows fewer terrain fixes, lets some of them fail and lets dead reckoning drift faster on a slope (Table 4). E50 does not split the error by kind of leg, so that reading is the model's construction and not a finding. The price of the terrain-fix layer is now in a delay figure, as a lower bound (E50b, Section 5.8). The same line appears in the map and in the ledger. For the map, {{P3-E52-LIMIT-CLAUSE}}. The order of a sync therefore protects a rover from the moment it is back in contact and not before; a rover that is out for days needs hazard updates from the rovers it meets, or has to stay in reach. For the ledger, {{P3-E54-LEDGER-CLAUSE}}. In each case the protocol's margins, lifetimes and timeouts have to be set by the longest time and the farthest place a vehicle is out of reach, not by the average. Several of the revision's results are not findings at all: the map's share of a full resend, the busiest link's load, the ledger's lost bundles and the ranging half of the clock hypothesis are arithmetic on inputs we assumed, and Table 18 now says so. The two checks on real data (Section 5.13) do not change any of this. They show that the transformation to azimuth and elevation under the Earth windows agrees with the toolkit it is built on, for a spacecraft that was below the base's horizon throughout, so the terrain mask was not exercised; and that a limb fit recovers range and direction from a real image with a bias we can see and have not removed.

6.2 Comparison with earlier studies

{{P3-E48-COMPARISON-CLAUSE}} Nearly all of those bytes are the status heartbeat at 1 Hz (Section 5.2 gives its share), so the honest comparison is between a one-message-a-second heartbeat and a convoy controller that exchanges state many times a second, not between a market and a convoy. The mesh cannot be said to agree with NASA's viewsheds [16], only to be of the same order: a 10 m mast on this ridge sees {{P3-MESH-MAST-DIRECT-SHARE}} of the route sample points along the roads within 5 km, and that study found a 4 m tower covering 10.6–23.7 % and a 20 m tower 12.1–29.2 % of the area of a 20 × 20 km region at another site (Section 2.2). The two are different quantities, at different sites and tower heights, and they were not matched: the verification suite's check of exactly this comparison was skipped as unfair for that reason. The rest of the network is multi-hop, as the Subterranean Challenge teams found underground [54, 55]. {{P3-E46-COMPARISON-CLAUSE}} Kondor's figure is a non-coordination penalty, the extra fleet an operator needs outside a pool, and ours is a gain over working alone inside one, so the two are not the same quantity and agree only in the direction of who fares worst. The second-price deficit is mechanical rather than a prediction of theory: the payout is floored at the carrier's own ask and the tariff is the reserve when no competitor exists, so on every job the operator pays out at least what the shipper pays in and on most jobs more.

6.3 Implications

For the companies that will own the vehicles, the implication of Section 3.2 is that the first base cannot be served by any one of them, and that the first thing worth agreeing is not a market but a coupling: two rovers of two owners lifting one load, which is the only way anything above {{P3-CAP-MAX-SINGLE-KG}} moves in this fleet [25, 60]. For the designers of those vehicles, Table 3 is the list of what a vehicle must carry to take part in a shared network: one small program that refuses unsafe moves, a radio that broadcasts a few dozen bytes a second of reports to whatever it can see and relays the market's and the roads' events, and a copy of the entries it has heard; Section 5.1 gives the time of the first and an estimate of the last. For the agencies that write LunaNet, the surface-to-surface link that the specification leaves bilateral [15] is where every number in Section 5.3 lives. A lower band would not lengthen a hop over flat ground: beyond the breakpoint the two-ray loss does not depend on frequency (Proposition 1). What a lunar UHF band [24] would change is diffraction around terrain, where the same ridge is a smaller obstacle at a longer wavelength [20], and with it the share of points behind a ridge that still have a link; that band may be used only outside the shielded zone of the far side [24]. The longer wavelength cuts the other way as well: its Fresnel zone is wider, and {{P3-MESH-FRESNEL-CLAUSE}} (Section 3.3), so the case for the band is not made by diffraction alone. For the operator of the base, the payment rule is a policy choice with a measured price: {{P3-E46-IMPLICATION-CLAUSE}} The choice is distributional: owners A and B prefer any paid rule to none and owner C prefers none, so the operator's measured price of a payment rule is owner C's loss against the no-payment market (Section 5.6). For whoever writes the site's rules, the layers under the market add three. Every sender declares the frame and the datum of its positions and the scale of its time stamps, and a position without a declaration is refused: in the protocol's second version that costs a few bytes in a periodic heartbeat and nothing in the report (Section 3.8). The lifetime of a bundle bound for Earth exceeds the longest planned Earth outage, the blackout plus the direct-to-Earth gaps around it, by a margin. And ranging beacons are not placed on one line. Earth's role in this design is the one it can play from 400,000 km away: it sets the prices and the rules, keeps the ledger, settles the accounts and audits a sample, and nothing it does has to happen within a radio window.

6.4 Limitations

The findings hold for one base, one fleet of eight vehicles in three ownerships, one radio model with assumed antenna heights, range and loss, and one implementation of the software; the sizing is two timed computations scaled to flight hardware by a factor, an estimate and a laptop measurement of memory and a computed radio load, none of them measured on a vehicle; the market has three owners, one manipulation and one private-cost spread, prices each run after one allocation with no owner responding to a rule, and tests individual rationality against a weak alone baseline, at one assumed value of time; the Shapley transfers are a definition of fairness, not a measurement; and H5's decision rules were written after the registration, before any run. The layers of the revision are tested on one recorded month by replaying it, with sensor, clock and map numbers that are assumed or taken from Earth and Mars, several of their registered hypotheses are calculations and not tests, and nothing in them has met a radio or the lunar surface. Section 7 states each in full.

6.5 Market and reality

Who has flown what. As of 11 November 2026 nobody has flown cooperating rovers on the Moon, of one owner or of several. CADRE, three rovers and a base station of one owner, was finished in February 2024 and has been with the lander's maker since February 2025 [106]; its lander, IM-3, has moved to the first quarter of 2027, as the company's chief executive said on an earnings call reported on 15 September 2026 [81], while NASA's and JPL's own pages still gave 2026 when we read them [10]. It is built and tested, not flown. NASA's Lunar Terrain Vehicle Phase 1 task orders of 26 May 2026 went to two makers, Astrolab for 219 million USD and Lunar Outpost for 220 million USD, with both vehicles to be on the Moon by 2028 [1]: two crew rovers of two makers for one customer, and no coordination method published. NASA's release of 7 November 2026 adds a nuclear-powered rover, PROMISE, with a launch target of late 2028 [107]. China's Chang'e 8, planned for 2028 to 2029, is to carry, besides its lander and rover, a robot of about 100 kg, a Pakistani rover of about 35 kg and two micro-robots, built by at least four organisations in three countries; it is the first planned mission on which robots of several makers and countries share a site, and no coordination method is published for it. That description rests on a secondary source, an encyclopedia page that cites trade press [108]. Makers already share a lander: Griffin Mission One, not yet launched, is to carry two rovers of two companies, with no stated cooperation between them, again according to an encyclopedia page [120]. A search on 11 November 2026 found no company that sells a coordination layer for lunar vehicles of different makers, which is an absence of evidence and not a proof of absence [68].

Customers. In practice there is one buyer today, NASA's Moon Base programme and its contractors. On 7 November 2026 the ceiling of the first lunar-delivery contract was raised to 4.2 billion USD and a second, ten-year contract with a combined ceiling of 10 billion USD was awarded to eight companies [107]; a ceiling is not an order. A coordination layer for a mixed fleet has a user only when three or more owners work one site, the number at which NASA's policy analysis says the difficulties compound and at which a partner it quotes said some centralised coordination “may become necessary” [51]. Current plans put that at 2028 to 2029, with the two crew rovers, the nuclear rover, the hopping drones and payload rovers of the delivery contracts near the south pole [1, 107]. The likely first buyers, in order, are the programme office that holds the task orders and can write an interface into them, the two rover makers, the lander companies that carry other owners' rovers, and the relay operators. We found no statement by NASA that mixed-fleet coordination will be required in a task order [68].

Market size. Published totals for a lunar economy to 2050 run from 94 to 127 billion USD in one consultancy's report of 2026 to 343 to 566 billion USD in another's of the same year; the same first consultancy gave 170 billion USD to 2040 in 2021 [109]. We read press reports of these three, not the reports. The one demand-side study we read, written for NASA in 2020, found most firms' business cases tied to space agencies [110]. The two reports of 2026 differ by a factor of about four over the same horizon, none has been tested against a year of revenue, none sizes surface mobility alone, and none sizes a coordination layer. This paper therefore quotes no market size for Lunar ATC. What it can state is how many vehicles and owners are planned for one site, and what coordinating them costs and saves in simulation ([60, 61] and Section 5).

Validation on Earth. Every result in this paper is from simulation. Table 16 sets out the stages that would replace simulation with evidence, each with one question and with pass criteria written before the stage is run; a stage that fails sends work back, not forward (Design, [68]). The first two stages need only the authors' time. The third needs mesh radios and a channel emulator, the method NASA Glenn used for lunar LTE studies with field measurements on a lava flow [111]. The fourth needs three to five small rovers and two software teams that see only the protocol document. The fifth needs a partner and a site, and is best timed with CADRE's flight data. No facility gives lunar gravity to a driving vehicle, so braking has to be tested at scaled speeds and said to be so. Table 17 lists whom we would ask for what.

6.6 Future work

No analysis is awaited. Two design changes follow from the results and are not made here: a covariance, or at least the two axes of the error, in the position report, which the second version of the protocol still lacks, and a cap on the items a robot evaluates in one auction round, so that its bid computation keeps a margin under the decision interval at larger fleets. After them come the experiments a reviewer will ask for and that we have not run: the readings of H9′'s second clause at eight times the fleet; E50 with the beacon survey error and the ultra-wideband reach swept; the traffic model with 1.0 m antennas for the small classes and the report rate tied to motion; the safety check in a compiled language on the laptop, to bound the language factor before any scaling; E46 with the value of time and the private-cost spread swept, a best response in place of four fixed factors, and five owners; E54 with the comparator's retry budget swept and with the lost-link return and the caution-speed crossing of an unconfirmed segment driven in the engine; E52 with its change rates swept; and E51's conflict check at four and eight times the fleet. Then the stages of Table 16 in their order. Four further pieces follow directly. The safety program and the auction should be run on the flight-class processor itself, against recorded traffic from the companion experiments, to replace the scaling factor with a measurement. The link budget should be checked against a measured lunar surface link as soon as one exists; none does today [17, 101]. The market should be run with five owners and with the manipulations this paper dropped, false availability, charger hogging and cherry-picking, which an auditable ledger may or may not catch. And the Phase-1 rovers' real battery, mass and radio should replace the assumed values when their design reviews publish them, due in late 2026 [1].

7. Limitations

The sizing is scaled, not flown. The processor time is of one Python function on a laptop, timed on the same moves for every class with no reservation entries in the call, and scaled by the ratio of a reference flight processor's benchmark to the laptop's. The factor is stated with a range, but a flight implementation in another language on another operating system could differ from it by more than that range; a compiled implementation would be expected to be faster, and that expectation is not measured. The memory has two figures, a sum of estimates and a laptop measurement of the interpreter's allocations for the on-robot parts; neither counts the interpreter, the code or the numerical library's buffers, and neither is the memory of flight software. One robot's bid computation was timed in one month of one seed per fleet size; scaled by the same assumed factor it stays under the one-second decision at the 99th percentile at the central factor and passes it at four and eight times the fleet at the top of the factor's range, with a spread between repeated passes that is not small beside the margin (Section 5.1). The flight processor's benchmark is taken from the experiment's design file, which cites the maker's presentation [121]; our research notes do not record that it was opened. {{P3-E48-LIMITATION-SENTENCE}} The laptop's benchmark is itself assumed: no published CoreMark exists for its processor, and the figure is 6.0 CoreMark/MHz times its 4.5 GHz turbo clock (Assumed, range 4–7 CoreMark/MHz), so the factor's range is the range of that assumption. The processor share reported for H10 takes the 99th-percentile call time at every call, which overstates the mean load.

The auction round and the safety loop are not allocated to cores. The reference processor has four cores, and the paper does not say which of them runs the 10 Hz safety loop and which the auction round. On a shared core, a bid computation that comes close to one second, which Section 5.1 reports at the 99th percentile at four and eight times the fleet, would stall the safety check while it ran; the design must give the safety check its own core or bound the round's time, and this paper states that requirement and leaves it open.

The radio is a model. Line of sight on a 5 m terrain grid with assumed antenna heights, a fixed range and a fixed loss probability stands in for a radio that nobody has measured on the Moon; the link budget uses the flat-ground two-ray law with a smooth-Moon horizon check and has no diffraction term: a path the terrain blocks carries nothing in the model, a path in line of sight is treated as flat ground, and the regolith's permittivity is unmeasured [21]. The share of route sample points in direct view of the mast is a property of this model on this ridge, and it is a share of points, not of time. The mesh counts a ray that grazes the ground as a link, where the knife-edge figure of our own reference is 6 dB of loss [20]; with 0.6 of the first Fresnel zone clear, fewer pairs have a link (Section 3.3). The flat-ground law is used at hops close to the horizon, where it is not conservative: a calculation over a spherical ground with the method of [20] gives a higher loss and lower rates there, and the rovers' longest hop then no longer carries a megabit a second (Section 5.3 and Table 7 give the figures). The runs use the flat-ground law; the spherical-ground column is beside it and changes no run, because every link the mesh uses still closes the narrow channel. Hidden terminals and fading are not modelled, and whether a base that sees a minority of the route points can schedule a channel for the fleet at all is not shown.

The link claim is conditional. {{P3-LIMIT-LINK-SENTENCE}}

The protocol declares what the design wanted in every report, and carries no covariance. {{P3-LIMIT-LRIP-SENTENCE}}

The link budget was corrected once. The first version of this sizing took free-space loss at the worst hop and so overstated the rates of the two classes with the lowest antennas there by more than two orders of magnitude. {{P3-E48-CORRECTION-SENTENCE}} An internal check against the literature found the error on {{P3-E48-REVISION-DATE}}, and it was corrected: Sections 4.2, 5.2 and 5.3 hold the corrected analysis and change 11 of [59] records it. No measurement of path loss on the lunar surface exists, so every range and rate here is a model.

The fleet is the fleet of 2028–2032 as published in October 2026. A nuclear-powered rover announced on 7 November 2026 for late 2028 [107] is not in it. The cargo rovers are uncontracted, their masses, batteries and one capacity are assumed, and the Phase-1 rovers' batteries are set so that the published {{P3-CLV1-RANGE-KM}} per charge comes out [1], because no battery size is published. The design reviews due in late 2026 and early 2027 may change every assumed value in Table 2.

Three owners, one manipulation. The market is tested with three owners in two splits; the theory's warnings about collusion and shill identities [31] concern exactly such thin markets and are not tested. Bid scaling is the only misreport simulated; an owner that misreports position, availability or job completion, or that hogs a charger, is not. The audits compare bids with logged costs and assume the logs are honest.

H5's decision rules are dated after the registration. The first-price cell that H5's first two clauses need was dropped on 27 October and restored on 29 October, before any E46 row (change 7 of [59]); the numeric decision rules for all three clauses were written then, not in the original registration, and are dated in [59].

The value of time is assumed and decides the money. Delay is priced at the hourly rates of Section 3.9, by priority, and cargo that is not delivered within the year is priced up to the year's end. Both are assumptions of the scenario, and neither was varied. The alone loss of the owner with the small vehicles is almost entirely such censored cargo (Section 5.6), so the alone baseline, the Shapley values, the pooling gain and the transfers scale with those two choices; a sweep of the value of time belongs with the sweep of the private-cost spread, and neither was run.

Private costs are assumed and their spread is not swept. Each owner's cost of energy, wear and time is the engine's job cost times a lognormal multiplier with σ = 0.2, a distribution we chose; profits and individual rationality are measured against those costs. The declared range of 0.1–0.3 was not run, because each value needs a new year of physical runs, so the results hold at one spread, and no spread replaces a company's own accounting.

Payments are priced after one allocation. Each rule prices a run that the auction has already allocated, so no owner responds to a rule and the honest allocation is the same under all four rules. The manipulation test is one deviator at a time over four fixed factors; no best response, no joint deviation and no equilibrium is computed. The gains reported are what a fixed scaling would have earned, not what a strategic owner would find.

The efficiency loss differs between the splits. The market's efficiency loss against the oracle differs between equal and 50/35/15 shares (Table 8); nothing in this paper traces that difference to a cause, and it is left untraced.

The alone baseline is weak. An owner alone is dominated by its cargo's delay cost at the assumed value of time, so individual rationality against alone is easy to pass under every rule; the comparison against the pooled market with no payments is the harder one, and Section 5.6 reports it.

The Shapley transfers are a definition, not a measurement. The transfers under the posted price are large relative to the cash the tariff moves, and they follow from the Shapley rule's definition of fairness; the paper measures what that definition pays each owner, not whether the owners would call it fair.

The first-price clause failed for power. The registered measure, the deviator's whole profit, carries the deviator's own cargo delay, whose spread over seeds is many times the cash the scaled asks earn; the cash part is positive (Section 6.1, post hoc). The clause is not supported as registered, and that is not evidence that shading does not pay.

The first-price budget is a calibration gap. With honest asks the first-price budget is the tariff receipts less the true carrier cost; the tariff was set to the mean cost per tonne-kilometre on the calibration seeds, so the budget on the test seeds is the gap between the two seed sets, not a property of the rule.

The lander carries only the radio unit and the landing announcement. In this design a lander's own Earth link is for flight; its Lunar ATC radio unit broadcasts the landing announcement on the mesh, Earth also holds the time, and the lander is otherwise a closure; it carries no planner and no safety program (change 7 of [59], Table 3), and the radio unit and announcement part it does carry are not sized here, because E48 measures the on-robot part on the driving and flying classes.

The layers of the revision are tested on one recorded month, by replay. E50, E51 and E54 take the trajectories of one month, July 2028, of one scenario, three equal owners at the middle load under the auction regime, and compute on them what position error, clock error and message loss would have been. No vehicle drives differently because its position is wrong or its message is lost. E51 and E54 run at today's fleet only; E51's check rests on the reservation pairs of that one fleet size, and the larger fleets, where reservations are frequent, were not checked. E54 is a simulation of messages, not of the engine: the return of a silent vehicle is applied to the contact plan and not driven, although the ladder calls for many returns a seed (Section 5.12); a returning vehicle is given a direct link to the base, which is optimistic; the comparator's retry budget is our choice and was not swept; and a lost job there is hypothetical, because in the recorded run every job was done. {{P3-E54-V02-LIMIT}} The run used a lost-link rule that released a silent holder's reservation; that rule is withdrawn, the count of segments left unconfirmed with the ladder in force was not recomputed, and the cost of crossing them at caution speed is not priced.

Several registered hypotheses are calculations. H12, H13, the ranging half of H11, the second clause of H14 as first run, and the ledger's lost bundles could not have come out otherwise: they are arithmetic on assumed tile counts, message sizes, report rates, a transfer residual and a calendar. They are reported as design calculations, and only what could have failed is called tested (Table 18).

“Near the base” is under a quarter of the driving. {{P3-LIMIT-NEAR-SENTENCE}}

The navigation model's numbers are not lunar. Every sensor value of Table 4 comes from tests on Earth or Mars, from a requirement or from an assumption; none was swept by us, although the research notes give a range for each [68]. E50's filter is matched to its own error model, with no outlier, no biased range and no wrong fix accepted as good, so its error is a lower limit for that model and not a prediction for a rover. Ultra-wideband ranging, terrain fixes and visual odometry with lamps have not been measured on the Moon, and no published figure gives the probability of a wrong fix that is declared good, which the traffic rules need more than the mean error. The model gives a hopper the same sensors as a rover and does not model its flight. Errors start at zero. The navigation service's assumed accuracy is the providers' target, which is better than the 17.2 m (95 %) that their own simulation of five satellites gives with terrain height [77], and its availability by year is our reading of plans, one of which has already slipped. The terrain-fix stops are in one delay figure only, E50b's, and there as a lower bound, because the stops on repositioning drives are not inserted. How long reservations overlap when position is wrong, and how far apart the vehicles are at that moment (task B-102), is not in this version; the companion paper [61] will report it.

The clocks are simple. A clock in E51 has a constant frequency error and, for the atomic class, a linear ageing; random noise is left out, and temperature enters only in the post hoc variant, as a daily cycle whose period is assumed. The crystal's range comes from one receiver on one lander, read from a figure's axis [82], and using that one crystal's excursion as the spread of constant offsets across vehicles is our choice; the transfer's assumed residual alone is tens of metres of one-way range (Section 5.9); the atomic clock's values are a data sheet's, for a model its maker does not rate for space [86]. The guard of {{P3-TIME-GUARD-S}} is our choice; the guard sweep, frequency steering and mixed scales were added after the first results. The lunar time scale itself is not defined in practice, so the scale named in a message is a field, not a fact.

The map experiment's world is assumed. The rate at which tiles, hazards, keep-out zones and road edges change, and the share of hazards a rover cannot see for itself, are assumptions that E52 does not sweep; its hazards are drawn, not sensed, and it does not run inside the engine. The tile sizes rest on compression rates measured on smooth orbital terrain and on assumed rates for rover-made layers, which real sensor noise will compress worse. The limits by which the base accepts a contribution are our choices. The reference-system standard that would fix the frame of the navigation service was not published when we looked.

Security is a design with one property counted. The acceptance of forgeries was counted at 8 and 16 bits; the figure for the 64-bit code the design uses is the formula's. An authentication code stops outsiders: a vehicle with a valid key that lies is not stopped by it, and the detection of such a vehicle is tested in the companion paper [61], not here. Key distribution, certificates and their renewal are not simulated. The trust anchor is an assumption, because no standard names one for a site with several owners. The message-level security copies the layout of a link-security standard and is not that standard. Jamming is not modelled.

The real-data checks are checks of code, not of a rover. The Artemis II frames are a handful of usable images from one camera, uncalibrated, with a bias in radius and in angle that we report and have not explained; they test limb navigation at flyby range. The Earth-link comparison tests our azimuth and elevation against the toolkit they are built on, along one trajectory on which the spacecraft never rose above the base's horizon; it does not exercise the terrain mask that makes the Earth windows. No result of this paper uses code that turns an image into a position.

9. Conclusion

We asked what the vehicles of 2028–2032 can carry, what a shared network needs in and under each, and which payment rules let distrustful owners gain by joining. We answered from published sources, a timed implementation, {{P3-E46-N-RUNS}} simulated years under four payment rules and pre-registered experiments. The fleet's capacity plateaus at {{P3-CAP-MAX-PAIR-KG}} per lift, set by two uncontracted cargo rovers. {{P3-E48-CONCLUSION-CLAUSE}} {{P3-E46-CONCLUSION-CLAUSE}} {{P3-P6-CONCLUSION-CLAUSE}}

The payment rule is a policy choice, priced in budget and transfers. The results hold for one base, one fleet and a modelled radio; several are calculations; nothing is measured on the Moon.

Appendix A. Guarantees and derivations

Each result below is stated with its assumptions, derived step by step, and followed by a worked example whose numbers the build script computes from the stated constants and compares with the results files where they hold the same quantity. All eight are statements about models. None is a measurement on the Moon, and each is only as good as its assumptions, which are named so that they can be attacked.

Proposition 1 (loss over flat ground, and the radio horizon). Assumptions. Two antennas at heights h1 and h2 above flat ground, a horizontal distance d apart, d much larger than both heights and small against the radio horizon, so that the bulge of the sphere at mid-path, d2/8R, is small against both heights; a direct ray and one ray reflected by the ground with a reflection coefficient of −1, its limit at grazing incidence; wavelength λ; no atmosphere; for the horizon, a smooth sphere of radius R. Statement. (a) The received power is Pr/Pt = GtGr(λ/4πd)2 · 4 sin2(2πh1h2/λd). (b) Write Lfs(d) = 20 log10(4πd/λ) for free space and L2(d) = 40 log10 d − 20 log10(h1h2). The law this paper uses is L = Lfs up to the breakpoint db = 4h1h2/λ and the larger of Lfs and L2 beyond it. The two are equal at dc = 4πh1h2/λ = πdb, so L = Lfs between db and dc, L = L2 beyond dc, L is continuous, and beyond dc it does not depend on frequency. (c) The two antennas are in view of each other on the sphere only if d ≤ √(2Rh1) + √(2Rh2).

Derivation. (1) The direct path has length √(d2 + (h1 − h2)2) and the reflected path, by the image of one antenna below the ground, √(d2 + (h1 + h2)2). For d much larger than the heights each root is d plus its second term over 2d, so the paths differ by ((h1 + h2)2 − (h1 − h2)2)/2d = 2h1h2/d. (2) The phase difference is φ = 2π/λ times that, 4πh1h2/λd. The reflected field has the amplitude of the direct one, to first order, and the opposite sign, so the sum has power |1 − eiφ|2 = 4 sin2(φ/2) times the free-space power, which is (a). (3) The factor has its last maximum where φ/2 = π/2, at db; beyond it the sine falls steadily. (4) Where φ/2 is small the sine equals its argument, the factor is φ2, and Pr/Pt = GtGr(h1h2)2/d4: the wavelength cancels, and in decibels the loss is L2. (5) Lfs = L2 means (4πd/λ)2 = d4/(h1h2)2, that is d = dc. Between db and dc the exact factor of (a) falls from 4 to 0.92, from 6 dB less loss than free space to 0.36 dB more; the law takes free space there and so does not credit the constructive interference. Beyond dc it takes L2. Because sin x is below x, the exact two-ray loss is above L2 everywhere, by 10 log10((φ/2)2/sin2(φ/2)), which beyond dc is largest at dc itself, where φ/2 = 1/2 and the difference is 0.36 dB, and shrinks with distance. From the breakpoint outward the law is therefore optimistic by at most 0.36 dB against the two-ray model it approximates; against real ground it is a model [21]. (6) A ray from height h that grazes the sphere meets it at a distance s with (R + h)2 = R2 + s2, so s = √(2Rh + h2), which is √(2Rh) for h much smaller than R; two antennas see each other when their grazing rays can meet, which is (c). {{P3-PROP1-EXAMPLE}}

Proposition 2 (the rate a hop can carry). Assumptions. The loss L(d) of Proposition 1; for the last sentence of the statement, the same antenna heights, transmit power and gains on every hop of the route; additive white Gaussian noise at 290 K in a bandwidth B, a receiver noise figure F and a margin M taken off the signal-to-noise ratio (all in decibels); ideal coding, so the result is an upper bound on what a real modem delivers. Statement. The capacity of the hop is C(d) = B log2(1 + 10S/10) with S = Pt + Gt + Gr − L(d) − N − M and N = −174 dBm/Hz + 10 log10 B + F. C does not increase with d, so on a route whose hops share their heights, power and gains the rate at the longest hop is a lower bound on the rate of every hop. Derivation. (1) Thermal noise of density kT is −174 dBm per hertz at 290 K; in the bandwidth B, with the receiver's figure, it is N. (2) The received power is Pt + Gt + Gr − L(d) in decibels, and S is what remains above the noise after the margin. (3) Shannon's formula gives C. (4) Lfs and L2 both grow with d, so their maximum does, S falls, and C falls with it. {{P3-PROP2-EXAMPLE}}

Proposition 3 (the rate of a lunar clock against a terrestrial one). Assumptions. Earth and Moon as point masses on a circular orbit of radius a about their common centre of mass; the Sun left out, because it acts on the difference only through tides; first order in 1/c2; the terrestrial clock on the geoid; the lunar clock at rest on the surface at radius RM, the Moon's rotation neglected. Statement. The lunar clock gains on the terrestrial one at the rate LG − LL − Lorb, where LG is the defining constant of the International Astronomical Union for a clock on the geoid (a definition, which [23] tabulates and does not derive), LL = GMM/(RMc2), and Lorb = [GME/a + (1 − μ)2v2/2 − GMM/a − μ2v2/2]/c2, with μ = MM/(ME + MM) and v2 = G(ME + MM)/a. Derivation. (1) To first order a clock's proper time runs against coordinate time at 1 − (U + w2/2)/c2, with U the Newtonian potential at the clock, taken positive, and w its speed. (2) For the terrestrial clock, Earth's own potential and rotation give LG by definition; the Moon adds the potential GMM/a; and Earth moves about the centre of mass at μ times the relative orbital speed v, which adds μ2v2/2. (3) For the lunar clock, the Moon's own potential at its surface gives LL; Earth adds GME/a; and the Moon moves at (1 − μ)v. (4) Subtracting the two rates leaves the statement. The orbit's eccentricity adds a periodic term of 0.11 µs a day in rate, about half a microsecond in accumulated time over a month [23, 84], which this derivation leaves out. {{P3-PROP3-EXAMPLE}}

Proposition 4 (range error from clock error, and holdover). Assumptions. Signals travel at c; a clock's error after a synchronisation with residual x0 is x(t) = x0 + yt + At2/2, a constant fractional frequency error y and a linear ageing A; random noise is left out. Statement. (a) A one-way range between a transmitter with clock error δt and a receiver with δr is wrong by c(δr − δt): 1 ns is 0.2998 m. (b) A two-way range, in which A sends, B replies after a delay T and A halves the round trip less T, does not contain the clock offsets; its error is cT(eA − eB)/2 for fractional rate errors eA and eB, plus a term of the order of the range times eA. (c) With x0 = 0 the clock stays within a budget ε for t = ε/y if A = 0, and for t = (−y + √(y2 + 2Aε))/A otherwise. (d) A position stamped with a time that is wrong by D is wrong by vD at speed v; a time slot needs a guard of at least 2D plus the path delay; and a road segment handed from one vehicle to the next needs a margin of at least DA + DB + σA/vA + σB/vB, the two clock errors plus each vehicle's along-track position error over its speed. Derivation. (a) The receiver computes c times its reading of the arrival time less the stamped sending time; each reading carries its clock's error. (b) A measures the round trip on its own clock and B the delay on its own; the offsets drop out of both differences; what remains is that A's clock runs eA fast over the round trip and B's eB fast over T, and half the difference of the two is the stated error. (c) Solve x(t) = ε for t; with A positive the quadratic has one positive root. (d) Each is a distance or a time moved at a rate. {{P3-PROP4-EXAMPLE}}

Proposition 5 (the error of reading a position in the wrong lunar frame). Assumptions. The Mean Earth and Principal Axis frames share their origin and differ by a constant rotation M; positions are on or near a sphere of radius R; the rotation is small, with rotation vector θ. Statement. A position r whose coordinates are read in the other frame is displaced by δ = (M − I)r. To first order δ = θ × r, so |δ| ≤ |θ|R with equality where r is perpendicular to θ, the displacement is horizontal, and at a pole, where r lies along the third axis, |δ| = R√(θ12 + θ22): the part of the rotation about the polar axis moves nothing there. Derivation. (1) Coordinates of the same point in the two frames are related by M; using the numbers of one frame as if they were the other's places the point at Mr instead of r. (2) For a small rotation M = I + [θ]× plus terms of second order in θ, so δ = θ × r. (3) A cross product with r is perpendicular to r, that is, horizontal on a sphere; its length is |θ||r| times the sine of the angle between them. (4) At the pole r = (0, 0, ±R), and θ × r has the length R√(θ12 + θ22). The second-order terms are of the size |θ|2R, under half a metre here. {{P3-PROP5-EXAMPLE}}

Proposition 6 (bytes to bring a tiled map up to date). Assumptions. A layer of N tiles arranged in a quadtree of depth D = ⌈log4 N⌉, each node holding a hash of h bytes of its four children; n tiles differ between the two copies; a changed tile changes its hash and every hash above it (hash collisions are ignored); a tile travels as s bytes plus a header of t bytes, and a version number as v bytes. Statement. Finding the changed tiles by walking the tree costs at most h(1 + 4nD) bytes of hashes sent in one direction, not counting the replies that say which children differ nor any message header, against N(v + h) for a flat list of every tile's version and hash; the tree is the smaller whenever n < N(v + h)/(4Dh). Either way the tiles themselves then cost n(s + t). Derivation. (1) The two sides exchange the root hash, h bytes. (2) A node is opened, its four child hashes sent, only if its own hash differs, which happens only if a changed tile lies below it. (3) At each of the D levels above the tiles at most n nodes have a changed tile below them, so at most nD nodes are opened, each costing 4h. (4) Compare h(1 + 4nD) with N(v + h) and drop the 1. {{P3-PROP6-EXAMPLE}}

Proposition 7 (forged and replayed messages). Assumptions. The authentication code is an ideal one of t bits: to anyone without the key, the code of a message not yet seen is equally likely to be any of the 2t values. A receiver keeps, per sender and key, the highest counter smax it has accepted, and accepts a message only if its code verifies, its counter exceeds smax, and its time stamp is within a window W of the receiver's clock. A sender never uses a counter twice under one key. Statement. (a) A forged message is accepted with probability 2−t, and at least one of q forgeries with probability at most q 2−t. (b) A recorded message with counter s is rejected by every receiver that has accepted that message or any later one from the same sender; a receiver that has not can accept the recording only while its time stamp is within W, and then learns a state that was true at most W earlier. (c) A counter of 32 bits at f messages a second lasts 232/f seconds. Derivation. (a) The forger must name the one right value among 2t equally likely ones; over q tries the probabilities add at most (the union bound). (b) Such a receiver has smax ≥ s, and the second condition fails; otherwise only the third condition stands in the way, and it fails once the stamp is older than W. (c) Counting. The proposition says nothing about a sender that holds a valid key and lies, nor about jamming. {{P3-PROP7-EXAMPLE}}

Proposition 8 (how beacon geometry dilutes a ranging fix). Assumptions. A vehicle measures, at one epoch and with no prior knowledge of its position, two-way ranges, so no clock is unknown, to N beacons at known positions, all in line of sight, with independent Gaussian errors of the same standard deviation σr; the problem is horizontal; beacon i is seen at azimuth θi. Statement. No unbiased estimate of the position from that one epoch has a covariance below σr2(HTH)−1, where row i of H is (cos θi, sin θi), and the root of its trace is σr times the dilution Dh = 2√N / √(N2 − |S|2), with S the sum of e2iθ over the beacons. Hence (i) Dh ≥ 2/√N, with equality when S = 0, for example for beacons spread evenly around the vehicle: four such beacons give 1. (ii) If every beacon is seen within an angle α of one direction, α at most 45°, then Dh ≥ 2/(√N sin 2α); for a group of beacons of width b seen from a distance d, tan α = b/2d, and four beacons give Dh ≥ (4d2 + b2)/(4bd), about d/b far away. (iii) For beacons on one line and a vehicle on that line, |S| = N and the bound is infinite: ranges say nothing across the line. Derivation. (1) Each range's sensitivity to the position is the unit vector towards the beacon, so the Fisher information is HTH/σr2 and the Cramér–Rao bound is its inverse. (2) HTH has the entries Σcos2θ = (N + Re S)/2, Σsin2θ = (N − Re S)/2 and Σsin θ cos θ = Im S/2, so its trace is N and its determinant (N2 − |S|2)/4. (3) The trace of the inverse of a 2 by 2 matrix is its trace over its determinant, 4N/(N2 − |S|2); the root is Dh. (4) For (ii), the doubled angles lie within 2α of one direction, so the component of S along it is at least N cos 2α, and N2 − |S|2 is at most N2sin22α. (5) For (iii) every doubled angle is the same. {{P3-PROP8-EXAMPLE}}
