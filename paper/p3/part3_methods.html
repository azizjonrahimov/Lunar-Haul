3. The vehicles, the network and the design

This section describes the vehicles of 2028–2032 as published, the radio network as it stands and as we model it, what Lunar ATC puts inside each vehicle, how a vehicle knows where it is and what time it is, the map the vehicles share, the communication stack with its security, and how the marketplace works. Provenance follows the convention of [57, 60]: Measured values come from a cited measurement or document, Spec from a published specification or a company claim, Derived (or Modelled) from a computation on such values, and Assumed values are our choices, each swept in Section 4 or named in Section 7. Results of this study are never flagged; they come from the result files listed under Availability. Where the sources we reached hold no value, the text says so rather than supplying one.

3.1 The base and the shared engine

Everything in this paper runs on the engine of the companion papers [60, 61], which extends the discrete-event simulation of [56, 57, 58]. The base is on the Connecting Ridge between Shackleton and de Gerlache craters, on NASA Goddard's 5 m terrain tile (Measured). Its sites are the habitat and control centre (BASE), a cargo depot, a power plant, a construction site, an oxygen pilot plant, a science site and a permanently shadowed crater, with two landing pads 1 km from the base. Demand follows the landings and bulk flows of [56], scaled so that the two cargo-class vehicles are busy about 30 % of the time (load factor {{P3-K30}}, Assumed, calibrated in [60]); landed cargo belongs to the owner who ordered it; a tenth of landed items are team items that need two vehicles (Assumed). The pooled marketplace of [56] offers every job to every vehicle in auction rounds, each vehicle bids the marginal cost in hours of adding the job to its plan, weighted by the item's urgency, and the best bid wins; [60] runs those rounds on the robots over the radio mesh of Section 3.3 and adds a learned correction to the bids; [61] adds the rules of the road and the safety program. With every bid multiplier at 1 the market run reproduces the pooled auction of [60] exactly, and the E48 timers leave the event trace bit-identical (Section 3.10).

3.2 The vehicles of 2028–2032 and what they carry

Table 2 lists the {{P3-N-CLASSES}} vehicle classes of the catalogue, read from the catalogue file so that every value, flag and source in the table is the one the simulation uses. {{P3-N-CLASSES-FLEET}} of them make up the simulated fleet of {{P3-N-FLEET}} vehicles owned by {{P3-N-OWNERS}} companies: owner A has the crew rover CLV-1 and the cargo rover FLEX, owner B the crew rover Pegasus and the cargo rover Eagle, and owner C two small rovers and two hoppers (the fleet of [60]). The crew rovers are the two NASA contracted in May 2026 [1]; their payload of {{P3-CLV1-PAYLOAD-KG}} and range of {{P3-CLV1-RANGE-KM}} per charge are NASA's October 2026 statement [1], and their battery sizes are set so that the range comes out, because no battery size is published. The cargo rovers are the same companies' uncontracted designs [2, 3, 4]; FLEX's 2,000 kg is a company claim, and Eagle's 800 kg is the original Lunar Terrain Vehicle requirement [5] assumed for a vehicle whose capacity is not published. The small rover is the HL-MAPP of the same catalogue [3]. The hoppers are the MoonFall drones of 2028 [1, 7], with a propellant budget of 50 km over 14 days (Spec) and a cargo capacity we assumed, because they are survey drones; a smaller hopper, Micro-Nova, with 8–10 kg over 25 km [8], is in the catalogue and in none of the fleets simulated here. A six-wheeled nuclear-powered rover, PROMISE, was announced on 7 November 2026 with a launch target of late 2028 [107], after the catalogue was frozen; it is not in the catalogue, and the sources we read give no payload for it.

The capacity plateau. The largest single payload in the fleet is {{P3-CAP-MAX-SINGLE-KG}}, the largest pair that can couple lifts {{P3-CAP-MAX-PAIR-KG}} together (the two-rover carry of [25] is the one measured case, at about 0.3 of the pair's combined mass), and the fleet's eight vehicles carry {{P3-CAP-FLEET-SUM-KG}} in all if every one is loaded at once. Against that, NASA puts single base elements at 500–15,000 kg and occasional deliveries at 12,000–15,000 kg [5, 6], and the landers of the period put 150 kg (Blue Ghost [13]), 455 kg (Griffin as tested [12]), 1.5 t (Argonaut, from 2030–2031 [14]) and 3 t (Blue Moon MK1 [11]) on the surface at a time. Nothing heavier than {{P3-CAP-MAX-PAIR-KG}} moves at all in this fleet, whatever the dispatcher does; the companion paper found that most of the value of sharing vehicles across owners is cargo that owners alone never deliver [60]. The plateau is a fact about the fleet, not about Lunar ATC, and we state it here because the sizing of Section 3.4 is for these vehicles and not for the cargo rovers NASA has not yet bought. Larger fleets in Section 4.2 multiply every vehicle class and the demand by the same factor.

3.3 The radio network

Earth. Earth is in view of the base when any part of its disc is above the terrain horizon of a 2.5 m antenna at BASE, the criterion of [58]; the windows are those of [58] for 2028–2031 and the same pipeline's windows to 2046 after that (Modelled). A message to Earth takes 2.6 s round trip plus 2.4 s of ground processing (Measured for the light time, Assumed for the processing, as in [56]). Earth in this paper never dispatches; it publishes prices and rules, receives the ledger, settles accounts and audits (Section 3.9).

LunaNet. The surface links of Lunar ATC are not LunaNet links. LunaNet 1.0 standardises the links between orbit and the surface and the network layer above any surface radio, and leaves the surface wireless link to bilateral agreement [15]. Lunar ATC therefore assumes only what LunaNet provides to a surface user: IP routing, the Bundle Protocol for anything that can wait, and a shared time base from the navigation signal or the relay's clocks [15, 16] (Assumed that every vehicle holds it; a free-running oscillator would drift by about 86 ms a day at 1 ppm, Assumed, which the cooperative lifts of [60] cannot tolerate; Section 3.6 gives the range measured on the lunar surface and what each use of time needs). The robot-to-robot link below IP is ours, and Section 3.4 states what it carries. Its physical layer is left open by this paper; the link budget of Section 4.2 (E48) is computed at one frequency, 2.4 GHz, the Wi-Fi-class radio the Griffin lander flies [18] (Provisional), with transmit powers of 1.0 W for the crew and cargo rovers, 0.25 W for the small rover, 0.5 W for the hopper and 5 W for the base mast (Assumed, no source). A 400 MHz UHF fallback would lose less at knife-edge diffraction over a low ridge [20], and a 390–405 MHz lunar band is in draft [24], but it is not budgeted here.

The mesh. Two robots can talk directly when their antennas see each other over the terrain and are within range. The mesh is the data product of [60]: line of sight for every pair of {{P3-MESH-N-POINTS}} points (the sites, the pads and samples every 100 m along every route) on the 5 m terrain with the Moon's curvature and no refraction, for antennas 2.5 m high on rovers and 1.0 m on small robots and hoppers (Assumed) and a 10 m mast at BASE that carries the base computer (Assumed, after the surface-relay concept of [16]). The range is {{P3-RADIO-RANGE-KM}} and the clearance margin 0 m (Assumed). Line of sight is scarce on this ridge: two rover antennas within 500 m see each other in {{P3-MESH-LOS-25-0TO05}} of point pairs and at 2–5 km in {{P3-MESH-LOS-25-2TO5}}; for two 1 m antennas at 2–5 km the share is {{P3-MESH-LOS-10-2TO5}}. The mast sees {{P3-MESH-MAST-DIRECT-SHARE}} of the route points directly, none of them beyond {{P3-MESH-MAST-DIRECT-MAX-KM}} from the base. Rovers nevertheless form {{P3-MESH-COMPONENTS-2KM}} connected network from 2 km range upward; at {{P3-RADIO-RANGE-KM}} {{P3-MESH-REACH-1HOP}} of the points reach the mast in one hop, {{P3-MESH-REACH-2HOPS}} in two, and every point in at most {{P3-MESH-MAX-HOPS-5KM}} (median {{P3-MESH-MEDIAN-HOPS-5KM}}); small robots leave {{P3-MESH-SMALL-ISOLATED}} points with no neighbour at all within 2 km and need up to {{P3-MESH-SMALL-MAX-HOPS}} hops. A message hop takes {{P3-HOP-LATENCY-MS}} (Assumed, terrestrial practice; no lunar measurement exists) and is lost with probability p, independently or in bursts of mean length {{P3-MEAN-BURST}} messages (Assumed), with p = 0.10 in the runs of [60] and in E48. E46 runs with p = 0 by design, a perfect radio (Section 4.2). A robot that nobody hears drives to a rally point that sees the mast (Assumed; a lost-link procedure every robot carries).

Hops: the terrain and the fleet. {{P3-MESH-HOPS-SENTENCE}} {{P3-E53-SPREAD-CLAUSE}}.

Why the numbers are what they are. {{P3-HORIZON-SENTENCE}}; the mast's direct view above reaches less than half of that because the ridge is not smooth, which is the same finding as the relay viewsheds of [16]. {{P3-MESH-HORIZON-SENTENCE}} A rover antenna cannot clear the first Fresnel zone at kilometre ranges (5.6 m at 2.4 GHz over 1 km, 13.7 m at 400 MHz), so a link in line of sight still sits near grazing; beyond a two-ray breakpoint of about 128 m at 2.4 GHz for 2 m antennas, loss grows about 40 log d instead of 20 log d; free-space loss alone is 100 dB at 1 km and 120 dB at 10 km at 2.4 GHz, and 85 and 105 dB at 400 MHz (all Derived in the research note from standard formulae [20, 63]). The lunar regolith's permittivity at the pole is unmeasured [21]. The link budget of E48 therefore reports a data rate at the worst hop under the flat-ground two-ray law, with the rate under free-space loss beside it as an upper bound (Section 4.2), and not a measured range.

3.4 What Lunar ATC puts inside each vehicle

Lunar ATC is four pieces of software and the radio that carries their messages. Table 3 lists them per vehicle class, with the measurements E48 makes of each (Section 4.2). The design is ours; the parts of the vehicles themselves, their avionics, batteries and motor controllers, are not described here beyond what Section 3.2 needs, because few of their layouts are published.

The safety program. One function, safety_check(state, proposal), is the only place where the rules of the road of [61] are decided. It answers two kinds of proposal. A reservation proposal asks whether this vehicle may hold a resource, a one-lane road segment, a junction tile or a pad apron, for a time window, and is decided on the reservation entries the vehicle can see; a reservation that has not been confirmed is never assumed [49], so a lost message can only add delay. A move proposal is the planner's acceleration for the next step with the vehicle's kinematic situation, and is accepted only if, after the step, every rule still holds with the vehicle braking at its guaranteed deceleration; otherwise the first safe candidate, no acceleration and then full braking, is substituted. It is explicit switching in the Simplex form [47], a post-posed shield [48]: it never plans, it only refuses. The same function is called by the logistics model at job commit, by the encounter simulator at 10 Hz for every vehicle, and by the model checker for every move [61], so what E48 times is what was checked. Its state is the vehicle's own reservations, the broadcasts it has heard and the published constraints: landing closures, crew zones, the priority ladder.

The planner. The planner may be anything; the safety program does not trust it. In this study it is the nominal driving logic of [57] for moves and, for bids, the marginal-cost bid of the marketplace [56] with the learned correction of [60]: a linear model of {{P3-LIN-N-WEIGHTS}} weights, which was the model that improved on hand-built bids, or a multilayer network of {{P3-MLP-N-PARAMS}} parameters, which did not. Both are read from the policy files; both are small enough that the question for sizing is the auction's bookkeeping, not the arithmetic of a bid.

The radio unit. The unit carries two kinds of traffic, and they travel differently. State reports, position, status and intent, are broadcast one hop, in the time slots the base schedules, to whoever is in view; they are not relayed. Events, bids and winners' rebroadcasts, awards, reservation requests and grants, team-lift proposals, answers and commits, alerts and ledger entries, are relayed hop by hop through other robots or the mast until they reach their addressee, with the loss and latency of Section 3.3. The first version of this paper said that the unit floods all of this hop by hop. That cannot stand beside the finding of Section 5.2: the 16 kbit/s target holds only when the reports travel one hop on a scheduled channel, and only the events, which are a small share of the bytes, can afford relaying. A robot in a part of the network that holds the mast knows every waiting item; a robot elsewhere knows only the items at sites where one of its part's robots stands [60]. Status and intent are broadcast at 1 Hz for conformance [61]. E48 does not serialise these messages; it counts the traffic from each run's events. Every road traversal produces one reservation request, one grant and one release, every auction round its bid, award and claim, every landing one announcement, and every robot one status message a second (Assumed rate, after rule R9 of [61]); each type has an assumed byte layout, ids, times and positions as 32-bit values under an 8 B header (Assumed; no serialiser is flown). The winners' rebroadcasts, the team-lift proposals, answers and commits, and the ledger entries are not in that traffic; the ledger is sized separately from the year's jobs. {{P3-E48-SECURED-LAYOUT-SENTENCE}} The design targets we set before measuring were 100 B/s per robot, a 16 kbit/s-class link for 20 robots, and under 1 MB a day of market messages (Assumed; hypothesis H10 of [59]). The link target is compared with the airtime the traffic needs on a shared channel, not with the load at the sources alone (Section 4.2; change 11 of [59]). For comparison, the one measured inter-rover coordination load on record, a two-rover convoy controller, used 13.8 kB/s [25].

Local copies. Each vehicle keeps a copy of the reservation entries it has heard and of the ledger entries it has produced or relayed, so that it can check a proposal while out of contact (rule R7 of [61]: a vehicle out of contact keeps the reservations it holds and takes no new ones) and can give Earth its own account of every job. E48 measures the peak memory of the on-robot part, planner, safety program, reservation table and ledger copy together, in our implementation.

The base computer and its spare. The base computer on the 10 m mast holds the shared reservation table, the manifests of waiting items, the market's rounds when the leader regime runs them, and the ledger that Earth settles from. It has a spare board; in the model the computer fails as a process with a mean time between failures of {{P3-BASE-MTBF-H}} and a repair time of {{P3-BASE-REPAIR-H}} (both Assumed, swept in E48). While it is down the robots elect a leader among themselves, the robot that reaches the most others over the mesh, after a re-election delay of 0.5 h (Assumed; the value was taken from CADRE's half-hour cycle of waking and cooling, which is not its election time: CADRE selects an appointer every 10 s and is without a planner for at most 10 s after it loses its leader [106], so 0.5 h is pessimistic for an election of that kind; E48 does not measure this delay, it copies the assumed value and measures the share of time without a leader at it); robots outside the leader's part of the network act on their last instructions and, when idle, drive to the rally point. The leader runs the market for the robots it reaches; the reservation table it holds is the union of what those robots have heard. When the base computer returns after repair, or two parts of the network merge, the base computer's state is rebuilt from the robots: what merges is the assignment table, by the rule that an earlier commit keeps its reservation and a job is never assigned twice [61]. E48 checks after every rebuild that no job is assigned twice. The ledger itself is not simulated; its size is estimated from the year's jobs (Section 4.2). The auction regime of [60] needs no leader: nothing waits for one, and the base computer's failure only removes the mast from the mesh.

3.5 Position without GPS

What exists. No satellite navigation service covers the Moon. NASA, ESA and JAXA are building one interoperable service, the LunaNet Lunar Augmented Navigation Service, whose satellites all send the same signal near 2.49 GHz [77, 114]. As of 11 November 2026 none of its satellites is in lunar orbit. NASA's 2024 plan put a first navigation signal in September 2026, two by mid-2028 and four by September 2029 [78]; the first satellite is to ride with the IM-3 lander according to a secondary source, and that lander's launch has moved to the first quarter of 2027 [81]. ESA states initial operations of its Moonlight service for the end of 2028 and full operations for 2030 [79], and JAXA plans a demonstration satellite for 2029 [80]. The providers' own simulation of a five-satellite configuration at 87° S gives a horizontal error of 17.2 m (95 %) when the receiver takes its height from a terrain model and 121.6 m when it does not [77] (Simulation). Earth's own navigation signals were received on the lunar surface in March 2025, with point fixes wrong by 0.1 to 100 km [82] (Measured): they help with time, not with driving. A base that opens in 2028 therefore cannot depend on a navigation service, and the availability we assume for later years (Table 4) is our reading of the providers' plans, not a commitment by them.

The layered scheme. Lunar ATC asks every vehicle for position in layers, each bounding the error of the one below (Design, [68]). Layer 0 is one named frame and one named time scale (Sections 3.6 and 3.7). Layer 1 is dead reckoning, which every vehicle carries: wheel odometry, an inertial unit, a star tracker for heading and cameras that track the ground. Field tests on Earth for the Mars rovers gave 1 to 2.5 % of the distance driven with visual odometry [69] (Measured on Earth), and the lunar crater-matching work takes 2 % as its requirement [70, 71] (Spec); on loose or sloped ground wheel odometry alone is 10 % or worse [72] (Measured on Earth). Layer 2 is two-way ranging between vehicles and to surveyed beacons, which needs no shared clock (Proposition 4) and has been measured at 0.1 to 0.7 m over tens of metres on Earth [75], never on the Moon. Layer 3 is a fix against the orbital map: the vehicle matches craters or terrain it sees to the map it carries. This is the one layer with flight proof, on Mars, where Perseverance has matched panoramas to orbital images on board since February 2026 [73] (Measured); the lunar versions are designed for under 5 m in sunlight and were within 5 m in most dark test runs [70, 71] (Spec; simulation and field tests). Layer 4 is the navigation service when it exists. A position report carries the frame, the time scale, a covariance and the layers that contributed, so that a receiver can widen its margins instead of trusting a number. {{P3-NAV-LAYERS-SENTENCE}} Proposition 8 gives the bound that beacon geometry puts on layer 2.

3.6 Time

One named scale. Every time stamp in a Lunar ATC message names its time scale, and the base keeps one lunar scale that the vehicles follow (Design, [68]). The scale has to be named because lunar and terrestrial seconds differ: a clock on the lunar surface runs fast against the same clock on Earth's geoid by 56.02 µs a day. Three independent derivations give 56.0199 [23], 56.025 [83] and 56.0256 µs a day [84]. The White House memorandum of 2 April 2024, which asked NASA for a strategy for lunar time by 31 December 2026, quotes 58.7 µs a day [85]; that figure matches the intermediate value of [23] that leaves out the Moon's own gravitational potential, 2.71 µs a day. {{P3-TIME-SCALE-SENTENCE}} No standard realises a lunar scale yet: LunaNet requires every provider to relate its time to a LunaNet Reference Time whose defining document is not released [15], and on 11 November 2026 we found no public statement that NASA's strategy had been delivered. The scale's identifier in the message is therefore a field that can change, not a constant of the design.

Two oscillator classes. Between synchronisations a vehicle keeps time on its own oscillator. {{P3-TIME-MODEL-SENTENCE}} The crystal's range is the one number in this section measured on the Moon; it moved by several parts in 107 within a day with temperature alone [82], and a rover that drives in and out of shadow will see larger swings than a lander did.

What each use needs. The uses of time differ by seven orders of magnitude (Proposition 4; Table 12 lists the budgets). One-way ranging against a beacon's clock turns 1 ns of clock error into 0.30 m, so it needs nanoseconds. Two-way ranging needs no shared clock at all: the offset cancels and only the rate error over the reply delay remains. Radio time slots need 1.5 µs in cellular practice [87] (Spec), or some tens of microseconds for longer slots with a small guard (Derived). A position report stamped with a time is wrong by the speed times the clock error, which at the speeds of these vehicles allows milliseconds to tens of milliseconds for a decimetre (Derived). A road reservation needs the clock error to stay below its guard of {{P3-TIME-GUARD-S}}. The reservation's real margin is set by position, not by the clock: a vehicle whose position is off by the separation budget at walking pace is seconds early or late, many times the guard (Derived, [68]). Section 5.9 reports how long each oscillator holds each budget and what the reservations do.

3.7 The shared map and its frame

The frame. Two lunar reference systems are in use, and they differ. Every terrain and image product of the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter is in the Mean Earth / Polar Axis system (MOON_ME in the SPICE toolkit), and NASA's 2024 architecture review endorsed it “as the standard for initial surface operations, including planning and user location data exchange” [89]. Orbits, gravity and the new International Lunar Reference Frame use the Principal Axis system (MOON_PA), and the LunaNet working group has said that its reference-system standard will point to that frame and broadcast the transformation; the standard was announced for November 2026 and we could not find it published on 11 November 2026 [91]. The two systems share an origin and differ by a constant rotation of 0.02886° [88]. Lunar ATC stores every coordinate in MOON_ME on the 1,737.4 km sphere, in the south polar stereographic grid of the LOLA products, and every position in every message carries a frame identifier and a datum identifier; a receiver rejects a position without them, and a position from a navigation service in the other frame is converted once, in the receiver (Design, [68]). {{P3-MAP-FRAME-SENTENCE}} A second trap is silent: a SPICE program that loads no lunar frame kernel gets the default body frame IAU_MOON, which is off the Mean Earth frame by up to about 155 m [88]. A whole fleet that used the other frame consistently would have no internal error; the error appears where a position in one frame meets a map in the other.

The maps that exist. The reference terrain is from the LOLA laser altimeter: 5 m grids of 27 named sites and of everything south of 87° S, with a height error of 0.3 to 0.5 m RMS per cell and a position good to about 1 m after track adjustment [76, 90] (Measured). But about 90 % of the 5 m cells are interpolated between laser tracks, and the effective resolution is about 20 m poleward of 84° S [90]: a 5 m grid drawn from 20 m sampling shows smooth ground where there may be a 3 m crater. Cameras add 0.5 m images, 1.7 m images inside permanent shadow and 5 m shape-from-shading models where the Sun reaches [92]; images are pictures, not heights. No public product resolves the 0.3 to 1 m rocks and craters that stop a rover. That layer has to be made by the rovers, and it is why the map is shared. It also qualifies this paper's own flag: the 5 m terrain of Section 3.1 is Measured for height and interpolated for detail.

One map, held by the base. The base holds one authoritative map of versioned tiles, with a standby copy, and every rover carries a full copy that it can drive on alone (Design, [68]; the one flight design for several rovers merges its maps on an elected leader and has no rule for two owners [28]). The layers are the orbital elevation at 5 m; rover-made elevation, hazards and traversability cost at 1 m in tiles of 256 m; the road network; keep-out zones with an end time; and radio coverage. Each tile has a version and a hash, and the tiles of a layer form a tree in which each node stores the hash of its four children. An owner contributes signed derived cells only, a hazard, a free cell or a height with its error, never raw images. The base checks the frame and datum identifiers, refuses a contribution whose stated position error is above 2 m or whose heights differ from its own by a median of more than 1 m (Assumed limits), accepts a hazard at once as provisional and grows it by three times the reported position error, confirms it when a second owner or a second pass reports it, and logs every decision with the owner and the reason. After time out of contact a rover and the base compare tree hashes, not maps, and then send the changed tiles in a fixed order: keep-out zones and the hazards on the rover's route first, height refinements last [93]. {{P3-MAP-SYNC-SENTENCE}}

3.8 The communication stack and its security

The stack. Below the application every layer of the stack is an existing standard, and Lunar ATC reuses each unchanged (Table 5). What no standard defines is what two rovers of different owners say to each other. The LunaNet specification carries messages from a provider to a user only, says that “the specific standards for messaging are still being determined”, and names a security specification that is not yet written [15]. The surface-wireless standard of the space agencies, CCSDS 883.0-B-2 of May 2024, fixes Wi-Fi 6 and LTE Release 12 for the surface radio [97], so the statement of Section 2.2 that the surface link is left to bilateral agreement is true of LunaNet 1.0 and no longer of CCSDS; the gap that remains is the application layer. The Lunar Rover Interaction Protocol (LRIP, version 0.1; Design, [68]) fills it with position, status and intent reports, alerts, the task messages of the market (announce, bid, award, state), the reservation messages of the roads (request, grant, release), a capability query and manifest, a map notice and a heartbeat. {{P3-SEC-LAYOUT-SENTENCE}} A vehicle's manifest states its size and limits, its radios, its navigation accuracy and the message versions it speaks; a receiver skips fields it does not know and refuses another major version.

What has flown. Less has flown than the list of standards suggests. No rate or range of a cellular link has been measured on the Moon. Nokia's network on the IM-2 lander, on 6 March 2025, ran through its 25-minute power window with base station, radio and core on the air and never connected a device, because the device module on the hopper had cooled below its operating range (the company's release of 10 March 2025 [101]). Bundle Protocol version 7 has flown in lunar orbit: CAPSTONE, whose extended mission ended in June 2026, ran it with custody transfer in NASA's core Flight System (NASA, 6 July 2026 [102]), and NASA has run delay-tolerant networking as an operational service on its near-space and deep-space networks since January 2026 [22]. We found no demonstration of it on the lunar surface. No path loss between antennas at rover height has been measured on the Moon; the propagation law of Section 4.2 rests on one NASA model [21] and the one coverage atlas of the south pole is a preprint [103].

Security. Every message carries a security header of 6 bytes, a 2-byte key index and a 4-byte counter, and a message authentication code, AES-CMAC truncated to 8 bytes on the narrow channel or 16 on the broadband one; 8 bytes is the shortest code the link-security standard allows and the 64-bit floor of the NIST recommendation [96, 99]. A receiver accepts a message only if its code verifies, its counter is above the highest it has accepted from that sender under that key, and its time stamp is within 10 s of the receiver's clock (Assumed window); Proposition 7 gives what that buys. The base, or a site authority on Earth, is the trust anchor (Assumed: LunaNet's own security document does not exist yet [15]). Each owner holds a certificate signed by the anchor and each vehicle a key certified by its owner. A message that moves a right, a task award, a reservation grant, a capability manifest, carries an Ed25519 signature of 64 bytes [100] in place of the code, so that one owner can show another what was granted; the audits of Section 3.9 rest on those signatures. Reports are authenticated with a site key that changes daily (Assumed). A shared key stops outsiders and not a compromised insider: a vehicle that reports a false position with a valid code has to be caught by comparing its reports with ranges to it, and where several vehicles must agree on a value, each drops the highest and the lowest values it hears before averaging, which tolerates one false value only if it hears at least three others [104]. Nothing in the protocol can command another owner's vehicle; it can inform, ask and agree. Jamming is not stopped by any of this, only survived, by a second band and by the lost-link rule.

Store and forward, and the lost link. A position report is worthless after a second and is never queued. What must arrive, an award, a grant, a ledger entry, an alert, travels as a bundle that each node keeps until the next hop is reachable, with a lifetime after which it is dropped; a bundle adds 48 bytes, or 108 with an integrity block (Computed in [68] from [98]), which is why the once-a-second report is not one. A vehicle that hears no authenticated message from the base follows a fixed ladder that every vehicle knows: it takes no new reservation, finishes the segment it has reserved, stops at the next safe point and holds, then retraces its path to where the link last worked, then drives to a meeting point in view of the mast, then parks with a beacon; the others keep its reserved space blocked until it is heard (Design, [68], after the lost-link procedures of uncrewed aircraft and the radio-failure rule of instrument flight [105]). {{P3-DTN-MODEL-SENTENCE}}

3.9 The marketplace

Jobs and bids. The base computer posts every waiting item with its mass, origin, destination and urgency. In each auction round every free vehicle computes its bid, the marginal cost in hours of inserting the job into its plan, weighted by the item's urgency [56], adjusted by the learned correction [60], and sends it, relayed over the mesh; the lowest bid wins, ties going to the lower vehicle number. Under the leader regime the base computer or the elected leader collects the bids; under the auction regime the robots settle the winner among themselves by the rebroadcast rule of [42, 43, 44]. A team item is won by a pair that agrees by a two-phase commit over the radio, with a rendezvous timeout of {{P3-TIMEOUT-H}} (Assumed) [60]. All of this is the companion paper's machinery; what this paper adds is the money.

Private costs. Each owner has a private cost of running a vehicle: energy, wear and the opportunity cost of the vehicle's time, drawn per owner from an assumed distribution: the engine's job cost, wear plus operator time, times a multiplier exp(N(0, σ)) with σ = 0.2 (Assumed), one draw per owner per seed, the same in every run of that seed; a range of 0.1–0.3 is declared and not swept in E46, because a sweep needs new physical runs (Section 7). A bid is a vehicle's cost in hours; its owner's money cost is that time at the owner's private rate. An honest owner bids its true marginal cost.

Four payment rules. E46 runs the pooled market under four rules. A rule prices a run after it has been simulated: the auction allocates the jobs on the asks (honest, or scaled for the one deviating owner), and the rule then decides what is paid for the jobs so allocated, so with honest asks the four rules share one allocation. None: no money changes hands; each owner's vehicles work for everyone and the owner bears its own costs, the cooperative case of [60]. First price: the carrier of each job is paid its own ask, and the shipper of each item pays the posted tariff times the item's tonne-kilometres; it is the rule under which an owner gains by asking above its cost, which H5's first clause tests. Per-item second price: the carrier of each job is paid the larger of its own ask and the lowest ask a competing free vehicle would have made for the job at the dispatch instant (that vehicle's cost times its owner's private multiplier, and times the scaling factor if the vehicle belongs to the deviating owner, so a deviator's own other vehicle competes at its scaled ask). That alternative is reconstructed from the run, not bid, because the auction collects no second bid; a team lift is paid the sum of the two lowest such alternatives, split equally between the two carriers, each floored at its own ask; when fewer competing vehicles than the job needs could have carried the item, the tariff times the item's tonne-kilometres is the reserve; the shipper pays the tariff. It is Vickrey's rule [30] applied item by item, and because the auction is sequential across items and rounds it is not strategyproof across rounds, which the manipulation test measures. The shares of jobs with a competing alternative and priced by the reserve are reported in Section 5.5. Posted price per tonne-kilometre with Shapley sharing: Earth publishes a tariff per tonne-kilometre before the year; the carrier of a delivered item is paid the tariff times the item's mass and road distance and the item's shipper pays the same, so the operator's budget is balanced by construction; at the end of the year the coalition's value, its true carrier costs plus its cargo's delay costs with the sign reversed, is shared among its members by their Shapley values [37] through transfers, computed exactly from the seven non-empty coalitions' values, which five runs per seed give (the owners-alone run supplies the three singletons, three pair runs the pairs, and the pooled run the grand coalition), the rule that freight alliances most often use [36]. {{P3-E46-TARIFF-SENTENCE}}

Money flow and settlement. No money moves on the Moon. The base computer's ledger records every posting, bid, grant, delivery and payment due, signed by the vehicle that produced the entry (Design; the signing is not simulated). Earth receives the ledger in every window and settles afterwards: each owner's cash, what its vehicles were paid as carriers less the tariffs it paid as a shipper and any fines, is netted across owners, and the operator's budget, its receipts (the tariffs shippers pay plus fines) less its payouts to carriers, is reported per year as a surplus or a deficit and never assumed balanced [29]. Under the posted price the Shapley transfers of the year's end are settled on Earth with the cash. The amounts settled on Earth and left open at the end of the year (pay due on jobs unfinished at the horizon) are outcomes of E46.

Individual rationality. An owner is individually rational in the market if its profit over the year, earnings less its private costs, the tariff it pays for its own cargo and its cargo's delay cost, is at least what it would have made working alone with its own vehicles on its own cargo: the owners-alone regime of [60] decided on the Moon (A_MOON of the companion paper [60]) on the same seeds, so that the comparison carries no Earth-availability effect (change 7 of [59]). The alone profit is dominated by the owner's cargo's delay cost at its own value of time, because an owner alone has few vehicles for its cargo and no one pays it. E46 reports each owner's mean profit under each rule against its mean alone profit and counts the owners, per cell, whose market profit fell below alone; under the posted price the quantity compared is the owner's Shapley value of the coalition's value, and H5's third clause tests it with a bootstrap interval for every owner in every coalition. As the harder comparison, E46 also reports each owner's profit under each paid rule against its profit in the pooled market with no payments, paired by seed (Section 5.6), which asks whether a payment rule leaves an owner worse off than the cooperative market does.

Manipulation. One owner scales every bid of its vehicles by a factor k in {0.8, 0.9, 1.1, 1.25} while the other two bid honestly (Assumed levels, from the research design); the test is run for each owner in turn. The outcome is the largest gain in that owner's profit over honest bidding across the four factors, the factor that gives it, and the cost to the system: the change in the mean wait of urgent cargo, the primary outcome of [60], on the same seeds.

Audits. Because every job is in the ledger with the vehicle's reported cost and its logged energy and distance, Earth can audit afterwards. The model charges the expected penalty of an audit rather than drawing one. At an audit share q in {0, 5, 20 %}, every job of an owner that asks under first or second price is charged q times m times the absolute difference between its ask and its true cost, so asks above and below cost are fined alike and no random draw enters the paired comparison; the multiple m is stated with the tariff above. The outcome is what the audits recover of the manipulating owner's gain, and whether the gain net of expected penalties is still positive at each q.

4. Experiment design

4.1 Questions and hypotheses

The pre-registration [59] states two hypotheses for this paper. H5 (incentives). First-price bids let at least one owner gain by shading; per-item second price reduces the largest gain; posted price with Shapley sharing leaves no owner worse off than alone in any tested coalition; the operator's budget is reported, not assumed balanced. H10 (sizing, exploratory). The safety check, the bidding and the radio traffic of Lunar ATC fit a flight-class computer, reported as measured CPU time, memory and bytes per robot-hour scaled to a reference radiation-tolerant processor with a stated factor, and a 16 kbit/s-class link for 20 robots. H5 was written before any run as part of the original plan and was moved to this paper on 27 October 2026, when the three papers were regrouped (change 1 of [59]). H10 was added on the same date, before any run of either experiment. Two things must be said about H5 as registered. First, the reduced design of 27 October had no first-price rule, which would have left H5's first two clauses untestable; a first-price cell was restored on 29 October (change 7 of [59]), before any E46 row, so H5 is tested as registered. Second, H5 carried no numeric decision rule. The rules, registered as change 7 of [59] before the first E46 row, are: clause 1 is supported if, under first price, the largest gain in an owner's annual profit over bidding truthfully across the tested k has a 95 % interval above zero; clause 2 if the largest such gain under per-item second price is smaller than under first price with the interval of the paired difference excluding zero; clause 3 if, for every owner in every tested coalition, the 95 % interval of the owner's profit in the coalition less its profit alone does not lie entirely below zero, with the point estimates reported either way; intervals are percentile bootstraps over seeds with 20,000 resamples, and the operator's budget is reported per rule, not tested. H10 is exploratory and has no decision rule; it is reported as the measured values against the stated capacities. Since a correction of {{P3-E48-REVISION-DATE}} its link clause is reported per channel case, as the airtime the traffic needs against the 16 kbit/s target (Sections 4.2 and 4.6; change 11 of [59]).

The revision of November 2026 tests five further hypotheses, written on 11 November 2026 before any of their results existed [67]. That second pre-registration numbers its hypotheses H9 to H16, and its H9 and H10 are not those of [59]: in this paper H5 and H10 are the hypotheses of [59], and H9, H11, H12, H13 and H14 are those of [67]. H9 (positioning). With dead reckoning alone the 95th percentile of the position error exceeds the separation budget within one eight-hour shift of driving; with ranging it stays inside the budget within reach of the base beacons; with absolute fixes it stays inside the budget away from the base. Each clause is supported if the 95th percentile over seeds is on the predicted side of the budget, which a dated change of [67] fixed at the {{P3-NAV-BUDGET-M}} that rule R1 of [61] keeps free. H11 (time). A resynchronisation interval exists for the cheaper oscillator at which clock error causes no reservation conflict while it still adds more to ranging than the ranging radio's own noise; supported if the sweep finds one. H12 (map). After 24 h out of contact a hazards-first sync of changed tiles needs under 10 % of the bytes of a full resend and brings the hazard layer current sooner than oldest-first at every link rate. H13 (protocol). At 1 Hz position reports, 20 rovers and authentication on, the busiest link carries no more than 16 kbit/s; at 10 Hz it carries more. H14 (lost link). At a message loss of 0.25 with a 14-day Earth blackout, store-and-forward with the lost-link rule loses fewer jobs than drop-and-retry, with a paired interval that excludes zero, and leaves no reservation stuck past its timeout. {{P3-FAMILY-SENTENCE}}

4.3 Seeds and common random numbers

Every cell of E46 uses seeds 1–30, so a seed's demand, landings and faults (and message losses, of which E46 has none) are identical across payment rules, share splits, scaling factors and audit shares, and the difference between two cells on one seed is the effect of the rule alone; the owners-alone and coalition runs of a seed share its demand too. Pairs are formed on the seed. E48 instruments existing runs and adds no randomness of its own; where it times a fresh run it uses the seeds of the run it reproduces. No run is excluded after the fact; a run that crashes is fixed and repeated with the same seed, and the crash is reported in Section 5.14. The experiments of the revision share one recorded month: E51 and E54 replay the perfect-position runs of E50's seeds at today's fleet, so a seed is the same month in all three, and E52 and E53 draw their own seeds and positions.

4.4 Outcomes

The outcomes of E46 are, per owner and year: profit under each rule; the difference between the owner's Shapley value in each coalition and its alone profit on the same seed (individual rationality); the operator's budget surplus or deficit; the money settled on Earth and left open at the year's end; the efficiency loss against the oracle of [60] in system cost, true carrier cost plus delay cost; and, for the manipulation cells, the largest gain from scaling, its factor, its cost to the system in cargo delay hours and in system cost, and what each audit share recovers of it. The outcomes of E48 are the measurements of Section 4.2 per vehicle class, the scaled values, the link-budget quantities per class (the rate at the worst hop or at the longest hop the horizon allows, the latency of a decision, the share of route sample points with the mast in reach), the channel rate the traffic of 20 robots needs per channel case, the base computer's downtime share and the share of time without a leader per MTBF level at the assumed re-election delay, and the count of jobs found assigned twice after a rebuild of the assignment table, which must be zero.

4.5 Statistics

Differences between cells are paired means over seeds with a percentile bootstrap 95 % confidence interval of 20,000 resamples [62]. H5's clauses use the one-sided rules of Section 4.1; the Holm correction of the companion paper's confirmatory family does not apply here, because H5 is a single hypothesis with three clauses reported separately. Sizing values are reported as medians and 99th percentiles over calls with their counts, scaled values as a point estimate with the range the scaling factor's range implies, and the H10 comparison as the ratio of each scaled value to its capacity. All intervals are descriptive of the simulated population of seeds, not of a lunar fleet. The analyses are descriptive (cell means), inferential (paired bootstrap intervals), causal by design (same seeds, one factor changed) and, for the link budget, mechanistic. The experiments of the revision report means over seeds with the same percentile bootstrap intervals; H14's test is a paired difference on seeds; E55's counts of accepted forgeries are compared with the count Proposition 7 predicts and its Poisson spread; and the only p-values are those of the confirmatory family's Holm step (Section 4.1).

4.6 Choices made after looking at results

Two choices were made before either experiment ran and are recorded here. First, the market experiment was reduced on 27 October 2026, when the three papers were regrouped: the McAfee trade-reduction rule, charger hogging, false availability and cherry-picking of easy items were dropped, and the first-price rule with them; the first-price rule was restored on 29 October (change 7 of [59]) because H5 needs it, leaving four rules, one manipulation and the audits; the reduction was made for the time the runs would take, before any E46 row existed, and its consequence for H5 is stated in Section 4.1. Second, the dispatch fixed-point fix of the companion papers (change 5 of [59]) was adopted before any run of E46 or E48, so neither experiment has a before-and-after. Two choices were made after the runs. First, the decomposition of the largest first-price gain into the part the scaled asks pay with the allocation held fixed and the change in the deviator's own cargo delay once its asks move jobs was made after E46 had run, at the lead's request, when the registered interval was seen to cover zero; it is reported in Sections 5.7 and 6.1, in the abstract and in the conclusion, labelled post hoc each time, and no registered verdict depends on it. Second, E48's analysis was corrected. The first version of this paper took free-space loss at the worst hop of the link budget and compared the 16 kbit/s target with the traffic at the sources. On {{P3-E48-REVISION-DATE}} an internal check against the literature found that the loss on flat ground beyond the breakpoint follows the two-ray law and that two 1.0 m antennas are beyond their horizon at that hop. The link budget was recomputed under that law with the horizon check, the free-space rates were kept as an upper bound, the traffic was restated at authenticated message sizes as airtime on a shared channel, and H10's link clause is reported per channel case (change 11 of [59]). The instrumented runs were not repeated and the registered design file is unchanged. {{P3-CHANGES-SENTENCE}} No other choice was made after looking at the data of E46 and E48.

For the experiments of the revision the choices made after a first result are these, each dated in [67] or in the repository's reports. E50's seed counts, fewer at four and at eight times the fleet than at today's, were set after one timing cell, by run time alone. The budget of H9 was identified as rule R1's {{P3-NAV-BUDGET-M}} after the same cell. E50's delay was found not to contain the terrain-fix stops, and a follow-up run, E50b, was registered for them. In E51 the guards of 0.01 and 1 s, the Earth-second variant, frequency steering and the mixed fleet were added after the first results, at the lead's request, and are labelled post hoc wherever they appear; H11 was tested with the guard of {{P3-TIME-GUARD-S}} set before. While those variants were built a flaw was found in the atomic clock's model, whose ageing restarted at every resynchronisation; it was corrected, the atomic-clock rows changed and the crystal's rows and H11 did not. E53's second geometry, rovers spread over the routes, was added after the first results showed that the registered geometry keeps the fleet within one hop of the base; H13 stays on the registered geometry. E54's ledger was simulated again over 61 days, with two more lifetimes, after its first table was seen to count bundles still in transit at the window's end as lost. E55's hypothesis H16 was recorded as not supported as written, and a variant in which the base mast joins as an honest node was added; the companion paper reports both [61]. The Holm step of the confirmatory family was computed after every result was in, by the rule of [67], over the hypotheses that have a sampling test. The p-value it needs for H14 comes from an exact sign test over seeds that was added for that purpose; the registered rule for H14, a paired interval that excludes zero, gives the same verdict. No hypothesis's decision rule was changed after its result was seen.
