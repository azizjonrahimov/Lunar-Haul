3. The vehicles, the network and the design

This section describes the vehicles of 2028–2032 as published, the radio network as it stands and as we model it, what Lunar ATC puts inside each vehicle, and how its marketplace works. Provenance follows the convention of [57, 60]: Measured values come from a cited measurement or document, Spec from a published specification or a company claim, Derived (or Modelled) from a computation on such values, and Assumed values are our choices, each swept in Section 4 or named in Section 7. Results of this study are never flagged; they come from the result files listed under Availability. Where the sources we reached hold no value, the text says so rather than supplying one.

3.1 The base and the shared engine

Everything in this paper runs on the engine of the companion papers [60, 61], which extends the discrete-event simulation of [56, 57, 58]. The base is on the Connecting Ridge between Shackleton and de Gerlache craters, on NASA Goddard's 5 m terrain tile (Measured). Its sites are the habitat and control centre (BASE), a cargo depot, a power plant, a construction site, an oxygen pilot plant, a science site and a permanently shadowed crater, with two landing pads 1 km from the base. Demand follows the landings and bulk flows of [56], scaled so that the two cargo-class vehicles are busy about 30 % of the time (load factor {{P3-K30}}, Assumed, calibrated in [60]); landed cargo belongs to the owner who ordered it; a tenth of landed items are team items that need two vehicles (Assumed). The pooled marketplace of [56] offers every job to every vehicle in auction rounds, each vehicle bids the marginal cost in hours of adding the job to its plan, weighted by the item's urgency, and the best bid wins; [60] runs those rounds on the robots over the radio mesh of Section 3.3 and adds a learned correction to the bids; [61] adds the rules of the road and the safety program. With every bid multiplier at 1 the market run reproduces the pooled auction of [60] exactly, and the E48 timers leave the event trace bit-identical (Section 3.6).

3.2 The vehicles of 2028–2032 and what they carry

Table 2 lists the {{P3-N-CLASSES}} vehicle classes of the catalogue, read from the catalogue file so that every value, flag and source in the table is the one the simulation uses. {{P3-N-CLASSES-FLEET}} of them make up the simulated fleet of {{P3-N-FLEET}} vehicles owned by {{P3-N-OWNERS}} companies: owner A has the crew rover CLV-1 and the cargo rover FLEX, owner B the crew rover Pegasus and the cargo rover Eagle, and owner C two small rovers and two hoppers (the fleet of [60]). The crew rovers are the two NASA contracted in May 2026 [1]; their payload of {{P3-CLV1-PAYLOAD-KG}} and range of {{P3-CLV1-RANGE-KM}} per charge are NASA's October 2026 statement [1], and their battery sizes are set so that the range comes out, because no battery size is published. The cargo rovers are the same companies' uncontracted designs [2, 3, 4]; FLEX's 2,000 kg is a company claim, and Eagle's 800 kg is the original Lunar Terrain Vehicle requirement [5] assumed for a vehicle whose capacity is not published. The small rover is the HL-MAPP of the same catalogue [3]. The hoppers are the MoonFall drones of 2028 [1, 7], with a propellant budget of 50 km over 14 days (Spec) and a cargo capacity we assumed, because they are survey drones; a smaller hopper, Micro-Nova, with 8–10 kg over 25 km [8], is in the catalogue and in none of the fleets simulated here.

The capacity plateau. The largest single payload in the fleet is {{P3-CAP-MAX-SINGLE-KG}}, the largest pair that can couple lifts {{P3-CAP-MAX-PAIR-KG}} together (the two-rover carry of [25] is the one measured case, at about 0.3 of the pair's combined mass), and the fleet's eight vehicles carry {{P3-CAP-FLEET-SUM-KG}} in all if every one is loaded at once. Against that, NASA puts single base elements at 500–15,000 kg and occasional deliveries at 12,000–15,000 kg [5, 6], and the landers of the period put 150 kg (Blue Ghost [13]), 455 kg (Griffin as tested [12]), 1.5 t (Argonaut, from 2030–2031 [14]) and 3 t (Blue Moon MK1 [11]) on the surface at a time. Nothing heavier than {{P3-CAP-MAX-PAIR-KG}} moves at all in this fleet, whatever the dispatcher does; the companion paper found that most of the value of sharing vehicles across owners is cargo that owners alone never deliver [60]. The plateau is a fact about the fleet, not about Lunar ATC, and we state it here because the sizing of Section 3.4 is for these vehicles and not for the cargo rovers NASA has not yet bought. Larger fleets in Section 4.2 multiply every vehicle class and the demand by the same factor.

3.3 The radio network

Earth. Earth is in view of the base when any part of its disc is above the terrain horizon of a 2.5 m antenna at BASE, the criterion of [58]; the windows are those of [58] for 2028–2031 and the same pipeline's windows to 2046 after that (Modelled). A message to Earth takes 2.6 s round trip plus 2.4 s of ground processing (Measured for the light time, Assumed for the processing, as in [56]). Earth in this paper never dispatches; it publishes prices and rules, receives the ledger, settles accounts and audits (Section 3.5).

LunaNet. The surface links of Lunar ATC are not LunaNet links. LunaNet 1.0 standardises the links between orbit and the surface and the network layer above any surface radio, and leaves the surface wireless link to bilateral agreement [15]. Lunar ATC therefore assumes only what LunaNet provides to a surface user: IP routing, the Bundle Protocol for anything that can wait, and a shared time base from the navigation signal or the relay's clocks [15, 16] (Assumed that every vehicle holds it; a free-running oscillator would drift by about 86 ms a day at 1 ppm, Assumed, which the cooperative lifts of [60] cannot tolerate). The robot-to-robot link below IP is ours, and Section 3.4 states what it carries. Its physical layer is left open by this paper; the link budget of Section 4.2 (E48) is computed at one frequency, 2.4 GHz, the Wi-Fi-class radio the Griffin lander flies [18] (Provisional), with transmit powers of 1.0 W for the crew and cargo rovers, 0.25 W for the small rover, 0.5 W for the hopper and 5 W for the base mast (Assumed, no source). A 400 MHz UHF fallback would lose less at knife-edge diffraction over a low ridge [20], and a 390–405 MHz lunar band is in draft [24], but it is not budgeted here.

The mesh. Two robots can talk directly when their antennas see each other over the terrain and are within range. The mesh is the data product of [60]: line of sight for every pair of {{P3-MESH-N-POINTS}} points (the sites, the pads and samples every 100 m along every route) on the 5 m terrain with the Moon's curvature and no refraction, for antennas 2.5 m high on rovers and 1.0 m on small robots and hoppers (Assumed) and a 10 m mast at BASE that carries the base computer (Assumed, after the surface-relay concept of [16]). The range is {{P3-RADIO-RANGE-KM}} and the clearance margin 0 m (Assumed). Line of sight is scarce on this ridge: two rover antennas within 500 m see each other in {{P3-MESH-LOS-25-0TO05}} of point pairs and at 2–5 km in {{P3-MESH-LOS-25-2TO5}}; for two 1 m antennas at 2–5 km the share is {{P3-MESH-LOS-10-2TO5}}. The mast sees {{P3-MESH-MAST-DIRECT-SHARE}} of the route points directly, none of them beyond {{P3-MESH-MAST-DIRECT-MAX-KM}} from the base. Rovers nevertheless form {{P3-MESH-COMPONENTS-2KM}} connected network from 2 km range upward; at {{P3-RADIO-RANGE-KM}} {{P3-MESH-REACH-1HOP}} of the points reach the mast in one hop, {{P3-MESH-REACH-2HOPS}} in two, and every point in at most {{P3-MESH-MAX-HOPS-5KM}} (median {{P3-MESH-MEDIAN-HOPS-5KM}}); small robots leave {{P3-MESH-SMALL-ISOLATED}} points with no neighbour at all within 2 km and need up to {{P3-MESH-SMALL-MAX-HOPS}} hops. A message hop takes {{P3-HOP-LATENCY-MS}} (Assumed, terrestrial practice; no lunar measurement exists) and is lost with probability p, independently or in bursts of mean length {{P3-MEAN-BURST}} messages (Assumed), with p = 0.10 in the runs of [60] and in E48. E46 runs with p = 0 by design, a perfect radio (Section 4.2). A robot that nobody hears drives to a rally point that sees the mast (Assumed; a lost-link procedure every robot carries).

Why the numbers are what they are. On a smooth Moon the horizon of a 2.5 m antenna is 2.95 km and of a 10 m mast 5.89 km, so two rovers can never see each other beyond 5.9 km and a rover cannot see the mast beyond about 8.5 km [63]; the mesh above reaches less than half of that because the ridge is not smooth, which is the same finding as the relay viewsheds of [16]. A rover antenna cannot clear the first Fresnel zone at kilometre ranges (5.6 m at 2.4 GHz over 1 km, 13.7 m at 400 MHz), so a link in line of sight still sits near grazing; beyond a two-ray breakpoint of about 128 m at 2.4 GHz for 2 m antennas, loss grows about 40 log d instead of 20 log d; free-space loss alone is 100 dB at 1 km and 120 dB at 10 km at 2.4 GHz, and 85 and 105 dB at 400 MHz (all Derived in the research note from standard formulae [20, 63]). The lunar regolith's permittivity at the pole is unmeasured [21]. The link budget of E48 therefore reports a data rate at the worst hop under these models and not a measured range.

3.4 What Lunar ATC puts inside each vehicle

Lunar ATC is four pieces of software and the radio that carries their messages. Table 3 lists them per vehicle class, with the measurements E48 makes of each (Section 4.2). The design is ours; the parts of the vehicles themselves, their avionics, batteries and motor controllers, are not described here beyond what Section 3.2 needs, because few of their layouts are published.

The safety program. One function, safety_check(state, proposal), is the only place where the rules of the road of [61] are decided. It answers two kinds of proposal. A reservation proposal asks whether this vehicle may hold a resource, a one-lane road segment, a junction tile or a pad apron, for a time window, and is decided on the reservation entries the vehicle can see; a reservation that has not been confirmed is never assumed [49], so a lost message can only add delay. A move proposal is the planner's acceleration for the next step with the vehicle's kinematic situation, and is accepted only if, after the step, every rule still holds with the vehicle braking at its guaranteed deceleration; otherwise the first safe candidate, no acceleration and then full braking, is substituted. It is explicit switching in the Simplex form [47], a post-posed shield [48]: it never plans, it only refuses. The same function is called by the logistics model at job commit, by the encounter simulator at 10 Hz for every vehicle, and by the model checker for every move [61], so what E48 times is what was checked. Its state is the vehicle's own reservations, the broadcasts it has heard and the published constraints: landing closures, crew zones, the priority ladder.

The planner. The planner may be anything; the safety program does not trust it. In this study it is the nominal driving logic of [57] for moves and, for bids, the marginal-cost bid of the marketplace [56] with the learned correction of [60]: a linear model of {{P3-LIN-N-WEIGHTS}} weights, which was the model that improved on hand-built bids, or a multilayer network of {{P3-MLP-N-PARAMS}} parameters, which did not. Both are read from the policy files; both are small enough that the question for sizing is the auction's bookkeeping, not the arithmetic of a bid.

The radio unit. The unit floods bids, winners' rebroadcasts, status reports, reservation requests and grants, team-lift proposals, answers and commits, and ledger entries over the mesh, hop by hop through other robots or the mast, with the loss and latency of Section 3.3. A robot in a part of the network that holds the mast knows every waiting item; a robot elsewhere knows only the items at sites where one of its part's robots stands [60]. Status and intent are broadcast at 1 Hz for conformance [61]. E48 does not serialise these messages; it counts the traffic from each run's events. Every road traversal produces one reservation request, one grant and one release, every auction round its bid, award and claim, every landing one announcement, and every robot one status message a second (Assumed rate, after rule R9 of [61]); each type has an assumed byte layout, ids, times and positions as 32-bit values under an 8 B header (Assumed; no serialiser is flown). The winners' rebroadcasts, the team-lift proposals, answers and commits, and the ledger entries are not in that traffic; the ledger is sized separately from the year's jobs. The design targets we set before measuring were 100 B/s per robot, a 16 kbit/s-class link for 20 robots, and under 1 MB a day of market messages (Assumed; hypothesis H10 of [59]). For comparison, the one measured inter-rover coordination load on record, a two-rover convoy controller, used 13.8 kB/s [25].

Local copies. Each vehicle keeps a copy of the reservation entries it has heard and of the ledger entries it has produced or relayed, so that it can check a proposal while out of contact (rule R7 of [61]: a vehicle out of contact keeps the reservations it holds and takes no new ones) and can give Earth its own account of every job. E48 measures the peak memory of the on-robot part, planner, safety program, reservation table and ledger copy together, in our implementation.

The base computer and its spare. The base computer on the 10 m mast holds the shared reservation table, the manifests of waiting items, the market's rounds when the leader regime runs them, and the ledger that Earth settles from. It has a spare board; in the model the computer fails as a process with a mean time between failures of {{P3-BASE-MTBF-H}} and a repair time of {{P3-BASE-REPAIR-H}} (both Assumed, swept in E48). While it is down the robots elect a leader among themselves, the robot that reaches the most others over the mesh, after a re-election delay of 0.5 h (Assumed, after CADRE's wake cycle [27]; E48 does not measure this delay, it copies the assumed value and measures the share of time without a leader at it); robots outside the leader's part of the network act on their last instructions and, when idle, drive to the rally point. The leader runs the market for the robots it reaches; the reservation table it holds is the union of what those robots have heard. When the base computer returns after repair, or two parts of the network merge, the base computer's state is rebuilt from the robots: what merges is the assignment table, by the rule that an earlier commit keeps its reservation and a job is never assigned twice [61]. E48 checks after every rebuild that no job is assigned twice. The ledger itself is not simulated; its size is estimated from the year's jobs (Section 4.2). The auction regime of [60] needs no leader: nothing waits for one, and the base computer's failure only removes the mast from the mesh.

3.5 The marketplace

Jobs and bids. The base computer posts every waiting item with its mass, origin, destination and urgency. In each auction round every free vehicle computes its bid, the marginal cost in hours of inserting the job into its plan, weighted by the item's urgency [56], adjusted by the learned correction [60], and floods it; the lowest bid wins, ties going to the lower vehicle number. Under the leader regime the base computer or the elected leader collects the bids; under the auction regime the robots settle the winner among themselves by the rebroadcast rule of [42, 43, 44]. A team item is won by a pair that agrees by a two-phase commit over the radio, with a rendezvous timeout of {{P3-TIMEOUT-H}} (Assumed) [60]. All of this is the companion paper's machinery; what this paper adds is the money.

Private costs. Each owner has a private cost of running a vehicle: energy, wear and the opportunity cost of the vehicle's time, drawn per owner from an assumed distribution: the engine's job cost, wear plus operator time, times a multiplier exp(N(0, σ)) with σ = 0.2 (Assumed), one draw per owner per seed, the same in every run of that seed; a range of 0.1–0.3 is declared and not swept in E46, because a sweep needs new physical runs (Section 7). A bid is a vehicle's cost in hours; its owner's money cost is that time at the owner's private rate. An honest owner bids its true marginal cost.

Four payment rules. E46 runs the pooled market under four rules. A rule prices a run after it has been simulated: the auction allocates the jobs on the asks (honest, or scaled for the one deviating owner), and the rule then decides what is paid for the jobs so allocated, so with honest asks the four rules share one allocation. None: no money changes hands; each owner's vehicles work for everyone and the owner bears its own costs, the cooperative case of [60]. First price: the carrier of each job is paid its own ask, and the shipper of each item pays the posted tariff times the item's tonne-kilometres; it is the rule under which an owner gains by asking above its cost, which H5's first clause tests. Per-item second price: the carrier of each job is paid the larger of its own ask and the lowest ask a competing free vehicle would have made for the job at the dispatch instant (that vehicle's cost times its owner's private multiplier, and times the scaling factor if the vehicle belongs to the deviating owner, so a deviator's own other vehicle competes at its scaled ask). That alternative is reconstructed from the run, not bid, because the auction collects no second bid; a team lift is paid the sum of the two lowest such alternatives, split equally between the two carriers, each floored at its own ask; when fewer competing vehicles than the job needs could have carried the item, the tariff times the item's tonne-kilometres is the reserve; the shipper pays the tariff. It is Vickrey's rule [30] applied item by item, and because the auction is sequential across items and rounds it is not strategyproof across rounds, which the manipulation test measures. The shares of jobs with a competing alternative and priced by the reserve are reported in Section 5.5. Posted price per tonne-kilometre with Shapley sharing: Earth publishes a tariff per tonne-kilometre before the year; the carrier of a delivered item is paid the tariff times the item's mass and road distance and the item's shipper pays the same, so the operator's budget is balanced by construction; at the end of the year the coalition's value, its true carrier costs plus its cargo's delay costs with the sign reversed, is shared among its members by their Shapley values [37] through transfers, computed exactly from the seven non-empty coalitions' values, which five runs per seed give (the owners-alone run supplies the three singletons, three pair runs the pairs, and the pooled run the grand coalition), the rule that freight alliances most often use [36]. {{P3-E46-TARIFF-SENTENCE}}

Money flow and settlement. No money moves on the Moon. The base computer's ledger records every posting, bid, grant, delivery and payment due, signed by the vehicle that produced the entry (Design; the signing is not simulated). Earth receives the ledger in every window and settles afterwards: each owner's cash, what its vehicles were paid as carriers less the tariffs it paid as a shipper and any fines, is netted across owners, and the operator's budget, its receipts (the tariffs shippers pay plus fines) less its payouts to carriers, is reported per year as a surplus or a deficit and never assumed balanced [29]. Under the posted price the Shapley transfers of the year's end are settled on Earth with the cash. The amounts settled on Earth and left open at the end of the year (pay due on jobs unfinished at the horizon) are outcomes of E46.

Individual rationality. An owner is individually rational in the market if its profit over the year, earnings less its private costs, the tariff it pays for its own cargo and its cargo's delay cost, is at least what it would have made working alone with its own vehicles on its own cargo: the owners-alone regime of [60] decided on the Moon (A_MOON of the companion paper [60]) on the same seeds, so that the comparison carries no Earth-availability effect (change 7 of [59]). The alone profit is dominated by the owner's cargo's delay cost at its own value of time, because an owner alone has few vehicles for its cargo and no one pays it. E46 reports each owner's mean profit under each rule against its mean alone profit and counts the owners, per cell, whose market profit fell below alone; under the posted price the quantity compared is the owner's Shapley value of the coalition's value, and H5's third clause tests it with a bootstrap interval for every owner in every coalition. As the harder comparison, E46 also reports each owner's profit under each paid rule against its profit in the pooled market with no payments, paired by seed (Section 5.6), which asks whether a payment rule leaves an owner worse off than the cooperative market does.

Manipulation. One owner scales every bid of its vehicles by a factor k in {0.8, 0.9, 1.1, 1.25} while the other two bid honestly (Assumed levels, from the research design); the test is run for each owner in turn. The outcome is the largest gain in that owner's profit over honest bidding across the four factors, the factor that gives it, and the cost to the system: the change in the mean wait of urgent cargo, the primary outcome of [60], on the same seeds.

Audits. Because every job is in the ledger with the vehicle's reported cost and its logged energy and distance, Earth can audit afterwards. The model charges the expected penalty of an audit rather than drawing one. At an audit share q in {0, 5, 20 %}, every job of an owner that asks under first or second price is charged q times m times the absolute difference between its ask and its true cost, so asks above and below cost are fined alike and no random draw enters the paired comparison; the multiple m is stated with the tariff above. The outcome is what the audits recover of the manipulating owner's gain, and whether the gain net of expected penalties is still positive at each q.

4. Experiment design

4.1 Questions and hypotheses

The pre-registration [59] states two hypotheses for this paper. H5 (incentives). First-price bids let at least one owner gain by shading; per-item second price reduces the largest gain; posted price with Shapley sharing leaves no owner worse off than alone in any tested coalition; the operator's budget is reported, not assumed balanced. H10 (sizing, exploratory). The safety check, the bidding and the radio traffic of Lunar ATC fit a flight-class computer, reported as measured CPU time, memory and bytes per robot-hour scaled to a reference radiation-tolerant processor with a stated factor, and a 16 kbit/s-class link for 20 robots. H5 was written before any run as part of the original plan and was moved to this paper on 27 October 2026, when the three papers were regrouped (change 1 of [59]). H10 was added on the same date, before any run of either experiment. Two things must be said about H5 as registered. First, the reduced design of 27 October had no first-price rule, which would have left H5's first two clauses untestable; a first-price cell was restored on 29 October (change 7 of [59]), before any E46 row, so H5 is tested as registered. Second, H5 carried no numeric decision rule. The rules, registered as change 7 of [59] before the first E46 row, are: clause 1 is supported if, under first price, the largest gain in an owner's annual profit over bidding truthfully across the tested k has a 95 % interval above zero; clause 2 if the largest such gain under per-item second price is smaller than under first price with the interval of the paired difference excluding zero; clause 3 if, for every owner in every tested coalition, the 95 % interval of the owner's profit in the coalition less its profit alone does not lie entirely below zero, with the point estimates reported either way; intervals are percentile bootstraps over seeds with 20,000 resamples, and the operator's budget is reported per rule, not tested. H10 is exploratory and has no decision rule; it is reported as the measured values against the stated capacities.

4.2 Experiments

E46, the market. Three owners, with equal shares of the cargo and with 50/35/15 shares (the split of [39]), at the middle load, under the four payment rules of Section 3.5; for the two bid rules, first price and second price, the manipulation test with k in {0.8, 0.9, 1.1, 1.25} for each owner in turn, and the audit share q in {0, 5, 20 %}; the owners-alone regime on the same seeds as the individual-rationality baseline, and every subset of owners for the Shapley shares. Seeds 1–30 in every cell, a simulated year each. {{P3-E46-DESIGN-SENTENCE}} The dispatcher is the pooled market run by the base computer or leader over the mesh of [60]. {{P3-E46-LOSS-SENTENCE}} The road layer of [61] is on with its defaults.

E48, sizing. Our implementation is instrumented and run, not changed: processor time per safety_check call and per auction round, as the median and the 99th percentile over every call in a run; peak memory of the on-robot part; and the radio traffic computed from each run's event counts (road traversals, auction rounds, landings) at the assumed byte layouts of Section 3.4, plus a status message at 1 Hz per robot, from runs at one, four and eight times today's fleet and demand, the density ladder of [61]; the ledger's size is the year's jobs times the entry's bytes. {{P3-E48-HORIZONS-SENTENCE}} The measurements are scaled to a reference radiation-tolerant flight processor by the ratio of its published benchmark to this laptop's, flagged Assumed with a range. {{P3-E48-REFERENCE-SENTENCE}} The scaled values are compared with the design targets of Section 3.4. The link budget per vehicle class takes the antenna height, frequency and transmit power of Section 3.3 at the mesh's worst hop, and reports the data rate at that hop, the latency of a three-hop exchange of a 100 B message at the hop latency of Section 3.3 (one hop for the base computer) plus one scaled safety check, against a decision interval of 1 s (Assumed; a target of this paper's design that no design file holds), and the share of route sample points in direct view of the mast. Failover sweeps the base computer's mean time between failures. {{P3-E48-MTBF-SENTENCE}} The sweep measures the base computer's downtime share and the share of time without a leader, and checks the assignment table after every rebuild for a job assigned twice. {{P3-E48-DESIGN-SENTENCE}}

4.3 Seeds and common random numbers

Every cell of E46 uses seeds 1–30, so a seed's demand, landings and faults (and message losses, of which E46 has none) are identical across payment rules, share splits, scaling factors and audit shares, and the difference between two cells on one seed is the effect of the rule alone; the owners-alone and coalition runs of a seed share its demand too. Pairs are formed on the seed. E48 instruments existing runs and adds no randomness of its own; where it times a fresh run it uses the seeds of the run it reproduces. No run is excluded after the fact; a run that crashes is fixed and repeated with the same seed, and the crash is reported in Section 5.8.

4.4 Outcomes

The outcomes of E46 are, per owner and year: profit under each rule; the difference between the owner's Shapley value in each coalition and its alone profit on the same seed (individual rationality); the operator's budget surplus or deficit; the money settled on Earth and left open at the year's end; the efficiency loss against the oracle of [60] in system cost, true carrier cost plus delay cost; and, for the manipulation cells, the largest gain from scaling, its factor, its cost to the system in cargo delay hours and in system cost, and what each audit share recovers of it. The outcomes of E48 are the measurements of Section 4.2 per vehicle class, the scaled values, the link-budget quantities per class (the rate at the worst hop, the latency of a decision, the share of route sample points in direct view of the mast), the base computer's downtime share and the share of time without a leader per MTBF level at the assumed re-election delay, and the count of jobs found assigned twice after a rebuild of the assignment table, which must be zero.

4.5 Statistics

Differences between cells are paired means over seeds with a percentile bootstrap 95 % confidence interval of 20,000 resamples [62]. H5's clauses use the one-sided rules of Section 4.1; the Holm correction of the companion paper's confirmatory family does not apply here, because H5 is a single hypothesis with three clauses reported separately. Sizing values are reported as medians and 99th percentiles over calls with their counts, scaled values as a point estimate with the range the scaling factor's range implies, and the H10 comparison as the ratio of each scaled value to its capacity. All intervals are descriptive of the simulated population of seeds, not of a lunar fleet. The analyses are descriptive (cell means), inferential (paired bootstrap intervals), causal by design (same seeds, one factor changed) and, for the link budget, mechanistic.

4.6 Choices made after looking at results

Two choices were made before either experiment ran and are recorded here. First, the market experiment was reduced on 27 October 2026, when the three papers were regrouped: the McAfee trade-reduction rule, charger hogging, false availability and cherry-picking of easy items were dropped, and the first-price rule with them; the first-price rule was restored on 29 October (change 7 of [59]) because H5 needs it, leaving four rules, one manipulation and the audits; the reduction was made for the time the runs would take, before any E46 row existed, and its consequence for H5 is stated in Section 4.1. Second, the dispatch fixed-point fix of the companion papers (change 5 of [59]) was adopted before any run of E46 or E48, so neither experiment has a before-and-after. One choice was made after the runs. The decomposition of the largest first-price gain into the part the scaled asks pay with the allocation held fixed and the change in the deviator's own cargo delay once its asks move jobs was made after E46 had run, at the lead's request, when the registered interval was seen to cover zero; it is reported in Sections 5.7 and 6.1, in the abstract and in the conclusion, labelled post hoc each time, and no registered verdict depends on it. {{P3-CHANGES-SENTENCE}} No other choice was made after looking at data.
