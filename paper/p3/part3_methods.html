3. The vehicles, the network and the design

This section describes the vehicles of 2028–2032 as published, the radio network as it stands and as we model it, what Lunar ATC puts inside each vehicle, and how its marketplace works. Provenance follows the convention of [57, 60]: Measured values come from a cited measurement or document, Spec from a published specification or a company claim, Derived (or Modelled) from a computation on such values, and Assumed values are our choices, each swept in Section 4 or named in Section 7. Results of this study are never flagged; they come from the result files listed under Availability. Facts that the sources we reached do not hold are marked as checks to be made before submission rather than filled in.

3.1 The base and the shared engine

Everything in this paper runs on the engine of the companion papers [60, 61], which extends the discrete-event simulation of [56, 57, 58]. The base is on the Connecting Ridge between Shackleton and de Gerlache craters, on NASA Goddard's 5 m terrain tile (Measured). Its sites are the habitat and control centre (BASE), a cargo depot, a power plant, a construction site, an oxygen pilot plant, a science site and a permanently shadowed crater, with two landing pads 1 km from the base. Demand follows the landings and bulk flows of [56], scaled so that the two cargo-class vehicles are busy about 30 % of the time (load factor {{P3-K30}}, Assumed, calibrated in [60]); landed cargo belongs to the owner who ordered it; a tenth of landed items are team items that need two vehicles (Assumed). The pooled marketplace of [56] offers every job to every vehicle in auction rounds, each vehicle bids the marginal cost in hours of adding the job to its plan, weighted by the item's urgency, and the best bid wins; [60] runs those rounds on the robots over the radio mesh of Section 3.3 and adds a learned correction to the bids; [61] adds the rules of the road and the safety program. With the features of this paper switched off, the engine reproduces the results of those papers exactly (Section 3.6).

3.2 The vehicles of 2028–2032 and what they carry

Table 2 lists the {{P3-N-CLASSES}} vehicle classes of the catalogue, read from the catalogue file so that every value, flag and source in the table is the one the simulation uses. {{P3-N-CLASSES-FLEET}} of them make up the simulated fleet of {{P3-N-FLEET}} vehicles owned by {{P3-N-OWNERS}} companies: owner A has the crew rover CLV-1 and the cargo rover FLEX, owner B the crew rover Pegasus and the cargo rover Eagle, and owner C two small rovers and two hoppers (the fleet of [60]). The crew rovers are the two NASA contracted in May 2026 [1]; their payload of {{P3-CLV1-PAYLOAD-KG}} and range of {{P3-CLV1-RANGE-KM}} per charge are NASA's October 2026 statement [1], and their battery sizes are set so that the range comes out, because no battery size is published. The cargo rovers are the same companies' uncontracted designs [2, 3, 4]; FLEX's 2,000 kg is a company claim, and Eagle's 800 kg is the original Lunar Terrain Vehicle requirement [5] assumed for a vehicle whose capacity is not published. The small rover is the HL-MAPP of the same catalogue [3]. The hoppers are the MoonFall drones of 2028 [1, 7], with a propellant budget of 50 km over 14 days (Spec) and a cargo capacity we assumed, because they are survey drones; a smaller hopper, Micro-Nova, with 8–10 kg over 25 km [8], is in the catalogue and in none of the fleets simulated here.

The capacity plateau. The largest single payload in the fleet is {{P3-CAP-MAX-SINGLE-KG}}, the largest pair that can couple lifts {{P3-CAP-MAX-PAIR-KG}} together (the two-rover carry of [25] is the one measured case, at about 0.3 of the pair's combined mass), and the fleet's eight vehicles carry {{P3-CAP-FLEET-SUM-KG}} in all if every one is loaded at once. Against that, NASA puts single base elements at 500–15,000 kg and occasional deliveries at 12,000–15,000 kg [5, 6], and the landers of the period put 150 kg (Blue Ghost [13]), 455 kg (Griffin as tested [12]), 1.5 t (Argonaut, from 2030–2031 [14]) and 3 t (Blue Moon MK1 [11]) on the surface at a time. Nothing heavier than {{P3-CAP-MAX-PAIR-KG}} moves at all in this fleet, whatever the dispatcher does; the companion paper found that most of the value of sharing vehicles across owners is cargo that owners alone never deliver [60]. The plateau is a fact about the fleet, not about Lunar ATC, and we state it here because the sizing of Section 3.4 is for these vehicles and not for the cargo rovers NASA has not yet bought. Larger fleets in Section 4.2 multiply every vehicle class and the demand by the same factor.

3.3 The radio network

Earth. Earth is in view of the base when any part of its disc is above the terrain horizon of a 2.5 m antenna at BASE, the criterion of [58]; the windows are those of [58] for 2028–2031 and the same pipeline's windows to 2046 after that (Modelled). A message to Earth takes 2.6 s round trip plus 2.4 s of ground processing (Measured for the light time, Assumed for the processing, as in [56]). Earth in this paper never dispatches; it publishes prices and rules, receives the ledger, settles accounts and audits (Section 3.5).

LunaNet. The surface links of Lunar ATC are not LunaNet links. LunaNet 1.0 standardises the links between orbit and the surface and the network layer above any surface radio, and leaves the surface wireless link to bilateral agreement [15]. Lunar ATC therefore assumes only what LunaNet provides to a surface user: IP routing, the Bundle Protocol for anything that can wait, and a shared time base from the navigation signal or the relay's clocks [15, 16] (Assumed that every vehicle holds it; a free-running oscillator would drift by about 86 ms a day at 1 ppm, Assumed, which the cooperative lifts of [60] cannot tolerate). The robot-to-robot link below IP is ours, and Section 3.4 states what it carries. Its physical layer is left open by this paper; the link budget of Section 4.2 (E48) is computed for a 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi-class radio as the Griffin lander flies [18] and for a 400 MHz UHF fallback, because knife-edge diffraction over a low ridge loses less at UHF [20] and a 390–405 MHz lunar band is in draft [24]. [[CHECK: whether B-81's link budget uses these two frequencies, and the transmit power per class it assumes]]

The mesh. Two robots can talk directly when their antennas see each other over the terrain and are within range. The mesh is the data product of [60]: line of sight for every pair of {{P3-MESH-N-POINTS}} points (the sites, the pads and samples every 100 m along every route) on the 5 m terrain with the Moon's curvature and no refraction, for antennas 2.5 m high on rovers and 1.0 m on small robots and hoppers (Assumed) and a 10 m mast at BASE that carries the base computer (Assumed, after the surface-relay concept of [16]). The range is {{P3-RADIO-RANGE-KM}} and the clearance margin 0 m (Assumed). Line of sight is scarce on this ridge: two rover antennas within 500 m see each other in {{P3-MESH-LOS-25-0TO05}} of point pairs and at 2–5 km in {{P3-MESH-LOS-25-2TO5}}; for two 1 m antennas at 2–5 km the share is {{P3-MESH-LOS-10-2TO5}}. The mast sees {{P3-MESH-MAST-DIRECT-SHARE}} of the route points directly, none of them beyond {{P3-MESH-MAST-DIRECT-MAX-KM}} from the base. Rovers nevertheless form {{P3-MESH-COMPONENTS-2KM}} connected network from 2 km range upward; at {{P3-RADIO-RANGE-KM}} {{P3-MESH-REACH-1HOP}} of the points reach the mast in one hop, {{P3-MESH-REACH-2HOPS}} in two, and every point in at most {{P3-MESH-MAX-HOPS-5KM}} (median {{P3-MESH-MEDIAN-HOPS-5KM}}); small robots leave {{P3-MESH-SMALL-ISOLATED}} points with no neighbour at all within 2 km and need up to {{P3-MESH-SMALL-MAX-HOPS}} hops. The build script reads these from the mesh summary. A message hop takes {{P3-HOP-LATENCY-MS}} (Assumed, terrestrial practice; no lunar measurement exists) and is lost with probability p, independently or in bursts of mean length {{P3-MEAN-BURST}} messages (Assumed), with p = 0.10 unless stated [60]. A robot that nobody hears drives to a rally point that sees the mast (Assumed; a lost-link procedure every robot carries).

Why the numbers are what they are. On a smooth Moon the horizon of a 2.5 m antenna is 2.95 km and of a 10 m mast 5.89 km, so two rovers can never see each other beyond 5.9 km and a rover cannot see the mast beyond about 8.5 km [63]; the mesh above reaches less than half of that because the ridge is not smooth, which is the same finding as the relay viewsheds of [16]. A rover antenna cannot clear the first Fresnel zone at kilometre ranges (5.6 m at 2.4 GHz over 1 km, 13.7 m at 400 MHz), so a link in line of sight still sits near grazing; beyond a two-ray breakpoint of about 128 m at 2.4 GHz for 2 m antennas, loss grows about 40 log d instead of 20 log d; free-space loss alone is 100 dB at 1 km and 120 dB at 10 km at 2.4 GHz, and 85 and 105 dB at 400 MHz (all Derived in the research note from standard formulae [20, 63]). The lunar regolith's permittivity at the pole is unmeasured [21]. The link budget of E48 therefore reports a data rate at the worst hop under these models and not a measured range.

3.4 What Lunar ATC puts inside each vehicle

Lunar ATC is four pieces of software and the radio that carries their messages. Table 3 lists them per vehicle class, with the measurements E48 makes of each (Section 4.2). The design is ours; the parts of the vehicles themselves, their avionics, batteries and motor controllers, are not described here beyond what Section 3.2 needs, because few of their layouts are published.

The safety program. One function, safety_check(state, proposal), is the only place where the rules of the road of [61] are decided. It answers two kinds of proposal. A reservation proposal asks whether this vehicle may hold a resource, a one-lane road segment, a junction tile or a pad apron, for a time window, and is decided on the reservation entries the vehicle can see; a reservation that has not been confirmed is never assumed [49], so a lost message can only add delay. A move proposal is the planner's acceleration for the next step with the vehicle's kinematic situation, and is accepted only if, after the step, every rule still holds with the vehicle braking at its guaranteed deceleration; otherwise the first safe candidate, no acceleration and then full braking, is substituted. It is explicit switching in the Simplex form [47], a post-posed shield [48]: it never plans, it only refuses. The same function is called by the logistics model at job commit, by the encounter simulator at 10 Hz for every vehicle, and by the model checker for every move [61], so what E48 times is what was checked. Its state is the vehicle's own reservations, the broadcasts it has heard and the published constraints: landing closures, crew zones, the priority ladder.

The planner. The planner may be anything; the safety program does not trust it. In this study it is the nominal driving logic of [57] for moves and, for bids, the marginal-cost bid of the marketplace [56] with the learned correction of [60]: a linear model of {{P3-LIN-N-WEIGHTS}} weights, which was the model that improved on hand-built bids, or a multilayer network of {{P3-MLP-N-PARAMS}} parameters, which did not. Both are read from the policy files; both are small enough that the question for sizing is the auction's bookkeeping, not the arithmetic of a bid.

The radio unit. The unit floods bids, winners' rebroadcasts, status reports, reservation requests and grants, team-lift proposals, answers and commits, and ledger entries over the mesh, hop by hop through other robots or the mast, with the loss and latency of Section 3.3. A robot in a part of the network that holds the mast knows every waiting item; a robot elsewhere knows only the items at sites where one of its part's robots stands [60]. Status and intent are broadcast at 1 Hz for conformance [61]. E48 measures the bytes of each message type as serialised by our implementation and the messages per robot-hour the runs of [60, 61] generate. The design targets we set before measuring were 100 B/s per robot, a 16 kbit/s-class link for 20 robots, and under 1 MB a day of market messages (Assumed; hypothesis H10 of [59]). For comparison, the one measured inter-rover coordination load on record, a two-rover convoy controller, used 13.8 kB/s [25].

Local copies. Each vehicle keeps a copy of the reservation entries it has heard and of the ledger entries it has produced or relayed, so that it can check a proposal while out of contact (rule R7 of [61]: a vehicle out of contact keeps the reservations it holds and takes no new ones) and can give Earth its own account of every job. E48 measures the peak memory of the on-robot part, planner, safety program, reservation table and ledger copy together, in our implementation.

The base computer and its spare. The base computer on the 10 m mast holds the shared reservation table, the manifests of waiting items, the market's rounds when the leader regime runs them, and the ledger that Earth settles from. It has a spare board; in the model the computer fails as a process with a mean time between failures of {{P3-BASE-MTBF-H}} and a repair time of {{P3-BASE-REPAIR-H}} (both Assumed, swept in E48). While it is down the robots elect a leader among themselves, the robot that reaches the most others over the mesh, after a re-election delay of {{P3-REELECT-MIN}} (Assumed, after CADRE's wake cycle [27]); robots outside the leader's part of the network act on their last instructions and, when idle, drive to the rally point. The leader runs the market for the robots it reaches; the reservation table it holds is the union of what those robots have heard, and when the base computer returns or two parts of the network merge, the tables and ledgers are reconciled by the rule that an earlier commit keeps its reservation and a job is never assigned twice [61]. E48 checks that rule on every merge. The auction regime of [60] needs no leader: nothing waits for one, and the base computer's failure only removes the mast from the mesh.

3.5 The marketplace

Jobs and bids. The base computer posts every waiting item with its mass, origin, destination and urgency. In each auction round every free vehicle computes its bid, the marginal cost in hours of inserting the job into its plan, weighted by the item's urgency [56], adjusted by the learned correction [60], and floods it; the lowest bid wins, ties going to the lower vehicle number. Under the leader regime the base computer or the elected leader collects the bids; under the auction regime the robots settle the winner among themselves by the rebroadcast rule of [42, 43, 44]. A team item is won by a pair that agrees by a two-phase commit over the radio, with a rendezvous timeout of {{P3-TIMEOUT-H}} (Assumed) [60]. All of this is the companion paper's machinery; what this paper adds is the money.

Private costs. Each owner has a private cost of running a vehicle: energy, wear and the opportunity cost of the vehicle's time, drawn per owner from assumed distributions that Section 4.2 sweeps. A bid is a vehicle's cost in hours; its owner's money cost is that time at the owner's private rate. An honest owner bids its true marginal cost. [[CHECK: B-76 REVISED does not say whether step 1's private-cost distributions are kept; the sweep ranges must come from BACKEND's design file]]

Three payment rules. E46 runs the pooled market under three rules. None: no money changes hands; each owner's vehicles work for everyone and the owner bears its own costs, the cooperative case of [60]. Per-item second price: the winner of an item is paid the second-lowest bid for it, the sealed-bid rule of Vickrey [30] applied item by item; because the auction is sequential across items and rounds, the rule is not strategyproof across rounds, which is what the manipulation test measures. Posted price per tonne-kilometre with Shapley gain sharing: Earth publishes a price per tonne-kilometre before the year; a delivered item earns its owner's vehicle that price times the item's mass and road distance; at the end of the year the gain of the grand coalition over what each owner would have earned alone is shared among the owners by their Shapley values [37], computed exactly from the {{P3-N-COALITION-RUNS}} coalition runs per seed that three owners need (every subset of owners is run on the same seed), the rule that freight alliances most often use [36]. [[CHECK: the posted price's value or how Earth sets it; not in the research notes or B-76]]

Money flow and settlement. No money moves on the Moon. The base computer's ledger records every posting, bid, grant, delivery and payment due, signed by the vehicle that produced the entry (Design; the signing is not simulated). Earth receives the ledger in every window and settles afterwards: each owner's earnings less its payments, netted across owners, and the operator's budget, what the market pays out less what it takes in, is reported per year as a surplus or a deficit and never assumed balanced [29]. Under the second-price rule the operator pays the difference between the winning and the second bid; under the posted price it pays the posted price for every delivery and recovers nothing; under the none rule it pays nothing. The amounts settled on Earth and left open at the end of the year are outcomes of E46.

Individual rationality. An owner is individually rational in the market if its profit over the year, earnings less private costs, is at least what it would have made working alone with its own vehicles on its own cargo, the owners-alone regime of [60] decided on the Moon. [[CHECK: whether the alone baseline is the owners-alone regime decided on the Moon or the one decided from Earth; the research note's metric names the Moon one]] E46 reports each owner's profit under each rule against its alone profit on the same seed and counts the owners and seeds in which the market made an owner worse off.

Manipulation. One owner scales every bid of its vehicles by a factor k in {0.8, 0.9, 1.1, 1.25} while the other two bid honestly (Assumed levels, from the research design); the test is run for each owner in turn. The outcome is the largest gain in that owner's profit over honest bidding across the four factors, the factor that gives it, and the cost to the system: the change in the mean wait of urgent cargo, the primary outcome of [60], on the same seeds.

Audits. Because every job is in the ledger with the vehicle's reported cost and its logged energy and distance, Earth can audit afterwards. After each year Earth draws a share q of the jobs at random, q in {0, 5, 20 %}, compares the bid with the cost the vehicle's log implies, and charges a penalty of m times the overcharge found [[CHECK: the penalty multiple m; B-76 REVISED keeps the audit levels and drops the value]]. The outcome is what the audits recover of the manipulating owner's gain, and whether the gain net of expected penalties is still positive at each q.

3.6 Checks

The engine's existing checks are those of [56, 57, 58, 60, 61]. This paper adds its own: under the none rule every outcome of E46 is bit-identical to the pooled regime of [60] on the same seed; a market with one owner has no payments between owners and a zero budget; the Shapley shares sum to the coalition's gain on every seed; a bid scaled by k = 1.0 reproduces the honest run exactly; the audit at q = 0 recovers nothing and at q = 100 % finds every overcharge; the E48 timers add nothing to a run's event trace (a run with timing on is bit-identical to one with timing off); and the ledger after a partition merge holds no job twice. {{P3-CHECKS-SENTENCE}}

4. Experiment design

4.1 Questions and hypotheses

The pre-registration [59] states two hypotheses for this paper. H5 (incentives). First-price bids let at least one owner gain by shading; per-item second price reduces the largest gain; posted price with Shapley sharing leaves no owner worse off than alone in any tested coalition; the operator's budget is reported, not assumed balanced. H10 (sizing, exploratory). The safety check, the bidding and the radio traffic of Lunar ATC fit a flight-class computer, reported as measured CPU time, memory and bytes per robot-hour scaled to a reference radiation-tolerant processor with a stated factor, and a 16 kbit/s-class link for 20 robots. H5 was written before any run as part of the original plan and moved to this paper, and H10 was added, on 27 October 2026 when the three papers were regrouped (change 1 of [59]), before any run of either experiment. Two things must be said about H5 as registered. First, the reduced design of E46 (Section 4.2) has no first-price rule, so H5's first two clauses cannot be tested as written unless a first-price cell is added; [[CHECK: the lead decides whether E46 gains a first-price cell or H5's first two clauses are reported as not tested, and records the decision as a dated change in PREREG.md before E46 runs]]. Second, H5 carries no numeric decision rule. The rule we will apply, to be registered before the first E46 row exists, is: clause 1 is supported if the largest gain from scaling under first price has a 95 % interval above zero; clause 2 if the largest gain under second price is below that under first price with an interval excluding zero; clause 3 if, for every owner and both share splits, the lower bound of the paired difference between the owner's market profit and its alone profit is at least zero. H10 is exploratory and has no decision rule; it is reported as the measured values against the stated capacities.

4.2 Experiments

E46, the market. Three owners, with equal shares of the cargo and with 50/35/15 shares (the split of [39]), at the middle load, under the three payment rules of Section 3.5; for the two rules with money, the manipulation test with k in {0.8, 0.9, 1.1, 1.25} for each owner in turn, and the audit share q in {0, 5, 20 %}; the owners-alone regime on the same seeds as the individual-rationality baseline, and every subset of owners for the Shapley shares. Seeds 1–30 in every cell, a simulated year each. {{P3-E46-DESIGN-SENTENCE}} The dispatcher is the pooled market run by the base computer or leader with the mesh and loss of [60] at p = 0.10; the road layer of [61] is on with its defaults.

E48, sizing. Our implementation is instrumented and run, not changed: processor time per safety_check call and per auction round, as the median and the 99th percentile over every call in a run; peak memory of the on-robot part; bytes per message type as serialised; messages and bytes per robot-hour from runs at one, four and eight times today's fleet and demand, the density ladder of [61]; the ledger's size after a simulated year. The measurements are scaled to a reference radiation-tolerant flight processor by the ratio of its published benchmark to this laptop's, flagged Assumed with a range [[CHECK: the reference processor and benchmark B-81 chooses; the research notes name none]], and compared with the design targets of Section 3.4. The link budget per vehicle class takes the antenna height, frequency and transmit power of Section 3.3 against the mesh, and reports the data rate at the worst hop, the latency budget for a decision within 1 s, and the share of the time each class is reachable from the mast. Failover sweeps the base computer's mean time between failures over [[CHECK: the MTBF levels of the sweep; B-81 says “sweep” without levels]] with repair at {{P3-BASE-REPAIR-H}}, times the robots' re-election, and checks the ledger after every partition merge for a job assigned twice. {{P3-E48-DESIGN-SENTENCE}}

4.3 Seeds and common random numbers

Every cell of E46 uses seeds 1–30, so a seed's demand, landings, faults and message losses are identical across payment rules, share splits, scaling factors and audit shares, and the difference between two cells on one seed is the effect of the rule alone; the owners-alone and coalition runs of a seed share its demand too. Pairs are formed on the seed. E48 instruments existing runs and adds no randomness of its own; where it times a fresh run it uses the seeds of the run it reproduces. No run is excluded after the fact; a run that crashes is fixed and repeated with the same seed, and the crash is reported in Section 5.8.

4.4 Outcomes

The outcomes of E46 are, per owner and year: profit, earnings and payments under each rule; the difference between market profit and alone profit on the same seed (individual rationality); the operator's budget surplus or deficit; the money settled on Earth and left open at the year's end; the efficiency loss against the oracle of [60] in the mean wait of urgent cargo; and, for the manipulation cells, the largest gain from scaling, its factor, its cost to the system in urgent wait, and what each audit share recovers of it. The outcomes of E48 are the measurements of Section 4.2 per vehicle class, the scaled values, the link-budget quantities per class, the share of time without a leader and the re-election time per MTBF level, and the count of jobs found assigned twice after a merge, which must be zero.

4.5 Statistics

Differences between cells are paired means over seeds with a percentile bootstrap 95 % confidence interval of 20,000 resamples [62]. H5's clauses use the one-sided rules of Section 4.1; the Holm correction of the companion paper's confirmatory family does not apply here, because H5 is a single hypothesis with three clauses reported separately. Sizing values are reported as medians and 99th percentiles over calls with their counts, scaled values as a point estimate with the range the scaling factor's range implies, and the H10 comparison as the ratio of each scaled value to its capacity. All intervals are descriptive of the simulated population of seeds, not of a lunar fleet. The analyses are descriptive (cell means), inferential (paired bootstrap intervals), causal by design (same seeds, one factor changed) and, for the link budget, mechanistic.

4.6 Choices made after looking at results

Two choices were made before either experiment ran and are recorded here. First, the market experiment was reduced on 27 October 2026, when the three papers were regrouped: the McAfee trade-reduction rule, charger hogging, false availability and cherry-picking of easy items were dropped, and the first-price rule with them, leaving the three rules, one manipulation and the audits; the reduction was made for the time the runs would take, before any E46 row existed, and its consequence for H5 is stated in Section 4.1. Second, the dispatch fixed-point fix of the companion papers (change 5 of [59]) was adopted before any run of E46 or E48, so neither experiment has a before-and-after. {{P3-CHANGES-SENTENCE}}
